## Sub-assemblies

A **sub-assembly** is a group of components assembled as a separate unit before being incorporated into the final product. Examples include a bicycle braking system, an electronic circuit-board module or a drawer unit fitted into a cabinet.

### Why manufacturers use sub-assemblies

Sub-assemblies allow different parts of a product to be manufactured **on separate production lines or at the same time**. They can then be brought together during final assembly. This is useful for complex products because manufacture does not have to wait for every component to be produced in one sequence.

A manufacturer may also buy specialist sub-assemblies from an external supplier. This gives access to specialist equipment and expertise and can reduce the need to manufacture every component in-house. Standard or bought-in units can also reduce development and tooling costs.

### Accuracy and quality

A sub-assembly must meet the required **dimensions, tolerances, connections and performance requirements** so that it fits and functions correctly in the final product. It can be tested before final assembly, making faults easier to identify and preventing a defective unit from progressing further through production.

### Production benefits and limitations

Producing sub-assemblies concurrently can shorten lead time and make production easier to organise. Separate teams or suppliers can specialise in particular systems, while final assembly becomes the controlled joining of completed units.

The system depends on reliable coordination. A late supplier, incompatible dimensions or a quality problem in one sub-assembly can delay the complete product. Sub-assembly is therefore most effective when specifications, tolerances, quality checks and delivery schedules are clearly controlled.
