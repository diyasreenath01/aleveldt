<section class="note-panel">
<div class="note-intro"><p>Computer systems are used throughout commercial manufacture to plan production, control stock and components, coordinate suppliers, automate manufacturing and link sales data to future production.</p></div>
<div class="note-grid">
<div class="study-card"><h3>Planning and control software</h3>
<p>Planning and control software is used to organise complex manufacturing processes, schedules and resources.</p>
<p>Spreadsheets, charts and live production data can give managers an instant overview of factory schedules and operations.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Barcodes, RFID and EPOS</h3>
<p><strong>Barcodes</strong> and <strong>RFID (radio frequency identification)</strong> tags allow components, products and stock to be identified and tracked accurately.</p>
<p><strong>EPOS (electronic point of sale)</strong> records product sales. Sales information can be used to monitor stock, trigger re-ordering and provide useful marketing data.</p>
<p>Linking EPOS data to production planning helps manufacturers respond to changes in demand.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>PPC and the supply chain</h3>
<p><strong>Production planning and control (PPC)</strong> systems help to:</p>
<ul><li>plan and control production</li><li>organise the availability of materials and components</li><li>coordinate suppliers and customers</li><li>schedule machines and skilled workers</li></ul>
<p>An efficient supply-chain network is especially important for <strong>just-in-time (JIT)</strong> manufacture because materials and components must arrive when they are required rather than being held as large stocks.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Kanban and master production schedules</h3>
<p><strong>Kanban</strong> is a material/component monitoring and re-ordering system associated with JIT production. Traditional cards may be replaced by RFID tags and scanners.</p>
<p>A <strong>master production schedule (MPS)</strong> coordinates materials, production timings, machines and labour. It can trigger supplier orders and help keep the flow of components through a factory synchronised.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Modular / cell production</h3>
<p>CNC machines, robots and <strong>AGVs (automated guided vehicles)</strong> can be organised into manufacturing cells.</p>
<p>Robots may load and unload CNC machines while AGVs transport materials and components between workstations and buffer zones. Cells can be reprogrammed to manufacture different products, improving flexibility.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>JIT and lean production</h3>
<p><strong>JIT</strong> manufactures products and receives components when they are needed, reducing stockholding, storage space, damage and the risk of components becoming obsolete.</p>
<p>Lean production aims to remove waste from manufacturing. It can involve continuous improvement (<strong>kaizen</strong>), visual/electronic stock control, reliable suppliers, efficient factory layouts and a right-first-time approach.</p>
<p><strong>Limitation:</strong> JIT depends heavily on reliable suppliers and transport; disruption can stop production quickly because little reserve stock is held.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Flexible manufacturing systems and QRM</h3>
<p><strong>FMS (flexible manufacturing systems)</strong> use programmable CNC and robot-based cells so production can switch between different products or variants.</p>
<p><strong>QRM (quick response manufacturing)</strong> focuses on reducing lead time for lower-volume or customised products. CAD, rapid prototyping, flexible cells and multi-skilled workers can shorten the transition from design to manufacture.</p>
<p><strong>SMED (single-minute exchange of dies)</strong> reduces machine downtime by making die or mould changes very quick, often using quick-release and interchangeable elements.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>CIM</h3>
<p><strong>Computer integrated manufacture (CIM)</strong> links computer-controlled stages of bringing a product to market. This can include CAD, rapid prototyping, material and stock monitoring, CAM, automated storage/warehousing and EPOS sales information.</p>
<p>Integration allows information to move between design, manufacture, storage, distribution and sales rather than each area operating separately.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Standardised components</h3>
<p>Standardised components are manufactured to common, interchangeable standards. They improve compatibility, replacement and assembly.</p>
<p><strong>Examples:</strong> ISO-standard screw threads, bolts, light bulbs and standard fittings.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Bought-in components</h3>
<p>Bought-in components are sourced from specialist external suppliers rather than manufactured in-house.</p>
<ul><li>bulk purchasing can reduce unit cost</li><li>specialist suppliers provide equipment and expertise</li><li>greater consistency can be achieved</li><li>supplier CAD files can be incorporated directly into product designs</li><li>the manufacturer avoids investing in facilities to make every component</li></ul>
<p><strong>Examples:</strong> tyres, batteries, fuses, motors, memory cards and printer cartridges.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Sub-assemblies</h3>
<p>A <strong>sub-assembly</strong> is a self-contained group of components manufactured separately and then incorporated into the final product. This can simplify and speed up final assembly because different parts of a product can be produced at the same time.</p>
<p><strong>Example:</strong> bicycle manufacturers may produce their own frame while using bought-in gear, chain and braking sub-assemblies from specialist suppliers.</p></div>
