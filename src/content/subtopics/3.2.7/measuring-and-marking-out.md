<section class="note-panel"><h2>Accuracy in Design and Manufacture</h2><div class="note-intro"><p><strong>Accuracy</strong> is how close a manufactured dimension is to its required value. <strong>Measurement</strong> checks an existing dimension, while <strong>marking out</strong> transfers positions and dimensions onto material before manufacture.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Tolerances</h3><p>A <strong>tolerance</strong> is the permitted variation from a specified dimension. A part specified as <strong>50 ± 0.5 mm</strong> is acceptable between <strong>49.5 mm and 50.5 mm</strong>. Tight tolerances are important where parts must fit or move together, but they can increase cost.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Precision Measuring</h3><p><strong>Vernier calipers and micrometers</strong> provide precise measurements. For a one-off product, a skilled worker may check each part with calipers.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Checking Manufactured Parts</h3><p>A <strong>go/no-go gauge</strong> gives a quick pass or fail result: one side must fit and the other must not. It checks whether a part is within tolerance without showing its exact dimension.</p><p>A <strong>coordinate measuring machine (CMM)</strong> records points on a component with a probe and compares them with the required dimensions. Non-contact scanners can make similar checks without touching the part.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Sampling</h3><p><strong>Sampling</strong> means testing selected products from a production run rather than measuring every feature on every item. It saves time, but the sample must be taken regularly and represent the production output. A critical safety feature may require more extensive checking.</p></div></section>
