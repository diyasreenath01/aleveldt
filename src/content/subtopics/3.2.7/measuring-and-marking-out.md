## Measuring and marking out

**Measurement** checks a dimension, while **marking out** transfers dimensions, positions and shapes onto a material before it is cut, drilled or formed. Accurate measuring and marking out reduce errors that can affect fit, function and assembly.

### Reference points and marking out

Measurements should be taken from a consistent **datum** or reference edge where possible. Measuring each new position from the previous mark can allow small errors to accumulate.

The equipment should suit the material and the accuracy required. A rule may be adequate for general dimensions, while **Vernier calipers or micrometers** are more suitable for precise measurements. Squares, gauges, templates and other marking aids can improve repeatability when positions or shapes must be reproduced.

### Tolerances

A **tolerance** is the permitted variation from a specified dimension. A part specified as **50 ± 0.5 mm** is acceptable between **49.5 mm and 50.5 mm**. Tight tolerances are important where parts must fit or move together, but unnecessary precision increases manufacturing and inspection cost.

### Checking manufactured parts

A **go/no-go gauge** gives a quick pass or fail result: one side must fit and the other must not. It checks whether a part is within tolerance without giving its exact dimension.

A **coordinate measuring machine (CMM)** records points on a component and compares them with the required geometry. Non-contact measuring systems can perform similar checks without touching the component.

### Sampling

In production, **sampling** means checking selected products rather than every feature on every item. This saves time, but samples must be taken often enough to detect process problems. Critical dimensions or safety-related features may need more extensive checking.

The required method therefore depends on the **material, feature, tolerance, production quantity and consequence of an error**.
