<section class="note-panel"><h2>Metal Enhancement</h2></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>METAL ENHANCEMENTS</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>Metal enhancement uses controlled heating or mechanical working to change the internal structure of a metal. By changing how the metal’s crystals behave, we can make the metal harder, stronger, or easier to shape, depending on the process used.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Work hardening (cold working)</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>Work hardening happens when a metal is shaped without heating, for example, by bending, rolling, hammering, or pressing.</p>
<p>Cold working forces the metal’s crystal structure out of its original shape, which makes the metal stronger and harder in the worked area.</p>
<p>As the crystals become distorted, they cannot move easily, which reduces ductility and makes the metal more likely to crack if worked further.</p>
<p>This is why metals can become difficult to shape after repeated bending or hammering.</p>
<p>The effects of work hardening can be removed by annealing.<br> </p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Annealing (undoing work hardening)</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>Annealing is used to make a work-hardened metal easier to shape again.</p>
<p>The metal is heated and then cooled very slowly.</p>
<p>Slow cooling allows the metal crystals to rearrange into a more regular structure.<br> This reduces internal stress, lowers hardness, and restores ductility.</p>
<p>As a result, the metal becomes less brittle and easier to work.<br> </p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Case hardening (hard outside, tough inside)</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>Case hardening is used when a component needs a hard, wear-resistant surface but a tough inner core.<br>  It is used on steels with less than 0.4% carbon content.</p>
<p>The surface of the steel is hardened to resist wear and indentation.<br> The core remains softer, allowing the part to absorb shock without breaking.<br> This makes the process ideal for components such as gears and shafts.<br> </p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Case hardening process (step by step)</h2>
<div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Step 1: Carburising (adding carbon to the surface)</h3>
<p>Carburising increases the carbon content only at the surface of low-carbon steel.</p>
<p>The steel is placed in a sealed ceramic box packed with carbon.<br> </p>
<p>The box is heated to about 930–950°C, allowing carbon atoms to diffuse into the steel’s surface.<br> </p>
<p>The longer the steel remains at this temperature, the deeper the carbon-rich layer becomes.<br> </p>
<p>After carburising, the steel is reheated to around 760°C to prepare it for quenching.<br> </p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Step 2: Quenching (locking in hardness)</h3>
<p>Quenching rapidly cools the steel to lock the hard surface in place.</p>
<p>The hot steel is quenched in water to cool the surface quickly.<br> </p>
<p>Rapid cooling hardens the carbon-rich outer layer.<br> </p>
<p>The inner core cools more slowly and remains tough rather than brittle.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Hardening</h3>
<p>Used on medium- and high-carbon steels to alter the crystalline structure.</p>
<p>Steel is heated, held at temperature, and rapidly quenched in water, oil, or salt-water baths.</p>
<p>Increases hardness but also increases brittleness.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Tempering</h3>
<p>A secondary heat treatment carried out after hardening.</p>
<p>Reduces excess hardness and brittleness while increasing toughness and ductility.</p>
<p>Metal is reheated below the critical temperature, held, then air cooled.</p>
<p>Tempering colour indicates the temperature reached and the amount of brittleness removed.</p></div></div></section>
