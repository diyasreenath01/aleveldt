## 2D and 3D drawing

Designers choose drawing methods according to the information that must be communicated and the intended audience.

### 2D drawing

**Orthographic projection** communicates accurate manufacturing information through separate front, plan and side views. **Third-angle projection** is a standard convention used to position these views. **Sectional drawings** reveal internal features that would otherwise be hidden.

2D CAD is widely used for working drawings because dimensions, geometry, line types and tolerances can be edited accurately.

### 3D drawing

- **Isometric drawing** uses axes at 30° to the horizontal and is useful for clear 3D technical sketches.
- **One-point perspective** gives a realistic view where receding lines converge towards one vanishing point.
- **Two-point perspective** uses two vanishing points and is useful for showing products or spaces from an angled viewpoint.
- **Oblique/crating techniques** can help construct proportioned 3D forms quickly during idea development.

### Rendering and mixed media

Rendering communicates material and surface qualities using colour, texture, highlights, shadows and grain. Marker rendering, coloured pencils, digital media and mixed-media drawings can all be used depending on the speed and realism required.

### Choosing the communication method

A client presentation may prioritise realistic form, colour and finish, while a manufacturer needs dimensions, tolerances, materials and construction details. The most effective drawing is therefore the one that communicates the required information clearly, rather than the most visually impressive one.
### Reading technical drawings: conventions and theory

| Convention | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Orthographic** | Separate front, plan and side views projected at right angles | Shows true sizes/shapes of faces for manufacture when properly dimensioned |
| **Third-angle projection** | The plan is placed above the front elevation; right-side view is placed to the right | Keeps views in a consistent convention; check the projection symbol on a drawing |
| **First-angle projection** | The plan is placed below the front elevation; right-side view appears to the left | Avoids mistaking one international convention for another |
| **Visible outline** | Continuous, prominent line | Identifies visible edges |
| **Hidden detail** | Short dashed line | Indicates an edge not visible from that view |
| **Centre line** | Thin chain line | Marks centres, axes or symmetry |
| **Section lines** | Hatching on cut surfaces | Communicates internal details in a sectional view |

An **isometric drawing** represents three principal axes at 120° to one another (typically two at 30° to the horizontal and one vertical), usually using equal scales along the axes. Parallel edges remain parallel, so it is not perspective: distant parts do not become smaller. Circles on an isometric plane appear elliptical.

**Common error:** A pictorial isometric view is useful for visualising a product, but it normally does not replace dimensioned orthographic views for manufacture.

### Interpreting a drawing rather than just recognising it

When reading **orthographic views**, identify which edges and features line up between the front, plan and side views: a hole seen as a circle in one view may appear as hidden parallel lines in another. A **sectional view** can reveal the hole or internal cavity more clearly than hidden detail alone. Dimensions should be read from the stated measurements, not estimated from the drawing.

In **isometric**, parallel edges stay parallel and the three axes show the product's overall form in one image. This makes it easier to visualise how parts fit together, but sloping faces and circles are not shown in their true shape. Use dimensioned orthographic views when precise manufacture is required.

**Choosing between them:** a designer might use isometric to explain a proposed casing to a client, then orthographic front, plan, side and sectional views to communicate hole positions, wall thickness and assembly details to a manufacturer.
