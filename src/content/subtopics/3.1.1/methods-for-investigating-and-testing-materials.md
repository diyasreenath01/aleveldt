<section class="note-panel"><h2>Methods for investigating and testing materials</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>Workshop tests should allow materials to be compared under controlled conditions. A fair test keeps relevant variables consistent and measures the result objectively.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Flexural test: malleability and ductility</h3><ul><li>A bending test can use a mandrel pushing upwards and a plunger pushing downwards.</li><li>The uploaded workshop notes tested acrylic sheet, metal sheet and wood.</li><li>For a fairer comparison, specimens should be held consistently, tested in the same environment and subjected to a measured, repeatable load.</li></ul></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Hardness test</h3><ul><li>The workshop method uses a centre punch to compare resistance to indentation.</li><li>Keep the centre-punch diameter, specimen size, support, impact method and angle consistent.</li><li>A different hammer, angle or applied force would reduce the reliability of the comparison.</li></ul></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Toughness test</h3><ul><li>The workshop test uses a pendulum impact arrangement.</li><li>Keep specimen sizes, pendulum/string length and starting position consistent.</li><li>Release rather than push the pendulum so no extra force is added.</li><li>Measurement error can occur if the protractor is not positioned correctly.</li></ul></div>

<div class="study-card"><h3>Additional AQA specification coverage</h3><p>The AQA specification also requires you to be able to describe how workshop and industrial tests are set up and identify what is <strong>tested, measured and compared</strong>.</p>
<h4>Tensile strength</h4><p>A standard test piece is held in a tensile-testing machine and pulled in opposite directions by a gradually increasing force. The applied force and the extension of the material are measured. Materials can then be compared by the load or stress they withstand and how much they extend before failure.</p>
<h4>Toughness</h4><p>Toughness is a material's ability to absorb energy and resist fracture when subjected to impact. In an industrial impact test, a standard specimen is struck by a pendulum. The energy absorbed by the specimen during fracture can be measured and compared between materials.</p>
<h4>Hardness</h4><p>Hardness is resistance to indentation, scratching or wear. Industrial hardness tests use a controlled force to press an indenter into a material. The resulting indentation is measured so materials can be compared under consistent conditions.</p>
<h4>Malleability</h4><p>Malleability is the ability of a material to be permanently deformed under compressive forces without cracking. A controlled bend or forming test can be used to compare how far samples can be deformed before cracking or failure.</p>
<h4>Corrosion</h4><p>Corrosion tests compare how materials resist degradation in specified environmental conditions. Identical samples can be exposed to controlled conditions, such as moisture or a salt solution, for the same period. Changes such as visible corrosion or loss of mass can then be measured and compared.</p>
<h4>Conductivity</h4><p>Conductivity testing compares a material's ability to transfer electricity or heat. For electrical conductivity, samples of controlled dimensions can be placed in the same electrical circuit and their electrical resistance measured. Lower electrical resistance indicates greater electrical conductivity. Thermal conductivity can be compared by applying equivalent heating conditions to samples and measuring the transfer of heat.</p>
<p><strong>Exam point:</strong> when describing any test, state what is kept constant, what is measured and how the results allow materials to be compared.</p></div></section>
