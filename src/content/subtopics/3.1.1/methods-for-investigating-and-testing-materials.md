## Methods for investigating and testing materials

Material tests allow properties to be compared under controlled conditions. A useful test identifies **what is being changed, what is measured and what must be kept constant** so that the comparison is reliable.

### Flexural test: malleability and ductility

A bending test can use a mandrel and plunger to apply a controlled bending force to a specimen.

- Samples should have consistent dimensions and be supported in the same way.
- The position and amount of force should be controlled.
- The amount of deformation or the point at which cracking occurs can then be compared.
- Different specimen sizes, loading positions or applied forces would make the comparison less reliable.

### Hardness test

Hardness is resistance to indentation, scratching or wear.

A simple workshop comparison can use a centre punch to create an indentation in different materials. The centre-punch diameter, specimen size, support, angle and applied force should remain consistent. The size or depth of the indentation can then be compared.

Industrial hardness tests use a controlled force and standard indenter so results from different materials can be compared more accurately.

### Toughness test

Toughness is the ability to absorb energy and resist fracture under impact.

A pendulum impact test uses a standard specimen struck by a pendulum released from a controlled starting position. The energy absorbed by the material during impact or fracture can then be compared.

For a reliable test, specimen dimensions, pendulum length, starting position and release method should remain consistent. The pendulum should be released rather than pushed so that no additional force is introduced.

### Tensile strength test

A tensile test measures how a material behaves when pulled in opposite directions.

A standard test piece is held securely and subjected to a gradually increasing tensile force. The applied force and extension are measured. Materials can then be compared by the load or stress they withstand, the amount they extend and the point at which they fail.

### Malleability

Malleability is the ability of a material to undergo permanent deformation under compressive forces without cracking.

A controlled forming or bending test can compare how far specimens can be deformed before cracking or failure. Sample dimensions, tools and applied force should be kept consistent.

### Corrosion resistance

Corrosion tests compare how materials resist environmental or chemical degradation.

Identical samples can be exposed to controlled conditions, such as moisture or a salt solution, for the same period. Visible corrosion, surface change or loss of mass can then be measured and compared.

### Electrical conductivity

Electrical conductivity can be compared by placing samples of controlled dimensions into the same electrical test arrangement and measuring their electrical resistance. Lower resistance indicates greater electrical conductivity when the other test conditions are equivalent.

### Thermal conductivity

Thermal conductivity can be compared by exposing samples to equivalent heating conditions and measuring how rapidly or effectively heat is transferred through them. Sample dimensions, starting temperature, heat input and measurement position should be controlled.

### Reliable testing

Whatever property is being investigated, the test should use a consistent method and objective measurements. Repeating tests and calculating a representative result can reduce the effect of random error. A conclusion is only useful when the method allows the materials to be compared fairly.
