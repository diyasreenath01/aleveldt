# Mechanical and physical properties

A **material property** describes how a material behaves under particular conditions. This is different from a **characteristic**, which is a more general description such as “can be coloured”, “can be shaped” or “can be easily turned”. Your class notes make this distinction explicitly. fileciteturn43file0L5-L11

AQA expects you to use relevant physical and mechanical properties to explain and justify why a material is suitable for a product. Important examples named by AQA include **malleability, toughness, hardness, resistance to corrosion/degradation, thermal conductivity and electrical conductivity**.

> **Exam warning:** **cost is not a material property.** Cost can still affect material selection, but it changes with market conditions. Also avoid vague statements such as “the material is strong” when you can identify the actual type of strength required.

## The five useful property groups

Your property-word notes organise properties into five groups: **mechanical, physical, electrical, thermal and optical**. fileciteturn43file0L21-L28

| Property group | What it describes | Important examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Mechanical / working** | How a material behaves when forces act on it or it is manipulated | tensile strength, compression strength, shear strength, torsional strength, hardness, toughness, plasticity, malleability, elasticity, ductility |
| **Physical** | Inherent behaviour or condition of a material | density, fusibility, corrosion/degradation resistance, absorbency/water resistance, chemical resistance |
| **Electrical** | Behaviour when electricity is applied | electrical conductor, electrical insulator |
| **Thermal** | Behaviour when exposed to heat/temperature change | thermal conductor, thermal insulator, thermal expansion |
| **Optical** | How light passes through a material | transparent, translucent, opaque |

## Mechanical / working properties

Mechanical properties describe how a material reacts to external forces. Your class notes identify tensile, compression, torsion and shear strength alongside hardness, toughness, plasticity, malleability, elasticity and ductility. fileciteturn43file0L64-L73

### Strength

**Strength** is the ability of a material to resist an applied force without failing. In an exam, identify the type of loading rather than simply writing “strong”.

- **Tensile strength:** resistance to forces pulling the material apart in opposite directions. fileciteturn43file0L75-L78
- **Compressive strength:** resistance to forces pushing or crushing the material together. fileciteturn43file0L81-L84
- **Torsional strength:** resistance to twisting. fileciteturn43file0L87-L89
- **Shear strength:** resistance to opposing forces acting across the material, similar to a cutting/scissor action. fileciteturn43file0L92-L95

**Strength-to-weight ratio** compares the useful strength of a material with its mass. It is especially useful when explaining lightweight high-performance materials such as CFRP or aluminium alloys.

### Toughness

**Toughness** is the ability to resist cracking or breaking when subjected to impact or a sudden blow. A tough material can absorb energy before fracturing. fileciteturn43file0L125-L127

Do not confuse toughness with hardness: a material may be hard but brittle.

### Hardness

**Hardness** is resistance to surface wear, scratching or indentation. fileciteturn43file0L116-L118

Hardness matters for products such as cutting tools, work surfaces, bearings and products that experience repeated surface contact.

### Brittleness

A **brittle** material is likely to crack or shatter with relatively little plastic deformation. fileciteturn43file0L129-L130

Brittleness is essentially the opposite of toughness in many product-selection contexts.

### Ductility

**Ductility** is the ability to undergo permanent deformation in tension — becoming longer and thinner without cracking. fileciteturn43file0L104-L107

This is important when metals are drawn into wire or stretched during forming.

### Malleability

**Malleability** is the ability to undergo permanent deformation under compression and be reshaped without cracking. Your notes describe it as changing shape in different directions without breaking. fileciteturn43file0L110-L113

Malleable metals can be rolled, pressed, hammered or formed into sheet and 3D shapes.

### Elasticity

**Elasticity** is the ability to deform and then return to the original shape when the force is removed. fileciteturn43file0L133-L134

This is important for springs, flexible clips and elastomer products.

### Plasticity

**Plasticity** is the ability to be permanently reshaped without returning to the original form. Your notes describe the ability to be manipulated into shape, often when heated. fileciteturn43file0L120-L122

Plasticity is useful in forming processes because the material must retain the manufactured shape.

### Bending / flexibility

A material's behaviour in bending describes how it responds when a force causes it to curve. fileciteturn43file0L98-L101 A flexible material bends relatively easily; a stiff material resists bending and deformation.

## Physical properties

Your class notes describe physical properties as properties associated with the material's inherent state/structure and identify **density, fusibility, magnetism and corrosion/degradation** as key examples. fileciteturn43file0L137-L142

### Density

**Density** is mass per unit volume. A low-density material can reduce the mass of a product; a high-density material may be useful where weight, stability or compact mass is required.

Do not define density simply as “weight”. The relationship is:

**Density = mass ÷ volume**

### Fusibility / melting behaviour

**Fusibility** describes the ability of a material to melt or change from solid to liquid when heated. fileciteturn43file0L150-L153

It is treated as a physical/thermal behaviour rather than a working property because it describes how the material inherently responds to heat, not how easily it can be mechanically shaped. fileciteturn43file0L156-L161

### Corrosion and degradation resistance

**Corrosion resistance** is the ability to resist deterioration caused by chemical/environmental attack. For metals, corrosion can involve oxidation such as rusting. Polymers and woods can also **degrade** through UV, moisture, chemicals, fungi or other environmental conditions.

AQA specifically expects resistance to corrosion and degradation to be used when justifying material selection.

### Absorbency / water resistance

**Absorbency** describes how readily a material takes in water; water-resistant materials resist this. fileciteturn43file0L170-L171

This can affect hygiene, dimensional stability, outdoor suitability and durability.

### Chemical resistance

**Chemical resistance** is the ability to withstand exposure to chemicals without significant degradation. fileciteturn43file0L174-L177

This is important for laboratory equipment, cleaning products, food containers and chemical storage.

### Magnetism

**Magnetism** describes whether a material is attracted to a magnet or can exhibit magnetic behaviour. This can influence material choice in motors, catches, sensors, tools and electrical products.

## Electrical properties

### Electrical conductivity

An **electrical conductor** allows electric current to pass through it relatively easily. Metals such as copper are selected for wiring because of their high electrical conductivity.

### Electrical insulation

An **electrical insulator** resists the flow of electric current. Many polymers are used around wires, plugs and electrical housings because they provide electrical insulation. Your notes distinguish conductors and insulators by whether electricity can pass through the material. fileciteturn43file0L180-L189

## Thermal properties

### Thermal conductivity

A **thermal conductor** transfers heat readily. Metals generally conduct heat well, which can be useful for cookware, heat sinks and heat exchangers.

### Thermal insulation

A **thermal insulator** slows the transfer of heat. Polymers, foams and some woods are useful where the user needs protection from hot/cold surfaces or where heat loss must be reduced.

### Thermal expansion

**Thermal expansion** is the change in dimensions that occurs when temperature changes. Designers must allow for expansion/contraction where components experience large temperature ranges or where dimensional accuracy matters.

Your notes group thermal conductivity, insulation and thermal expansion together as thermal properties. fileciteturn43file0L192-L202

## Optical properties

- **Transparent:** allows light through so objects can be seen clearly through the material.
- **Translucent:** allows some light through but scatters it, so objects are not seen clearly.
- **Opaque:** does not allow light to pass through.

These distinctions are shown in your class notes as the three main optical properties. fileciteturn43file0L205-L209

## Property vs characteristic vs other selection factors

This distinction is useful in exam answers:

- **Property:** toughness, hardness, tensile strength, conductivity, density, etc.
- **Characteristic:** can be coloured, shaped, machined or given a particular surface appearance.
- **Other selection factor:** cost, aesthetics, availability, manufacture, disposal and environmental impact.

AQA expects material choices to be justified not only through properties but also through **product function, aesthetics, cost, manufacture and disposal**.

## Applying properties to products

Do not just name a property. Use:

**property → what the property means → why that benefits the specific product**

For example:

**PET is tough, meaning it resists cracking when knocked or dropped; this makes it suitable for a drinks bottle that will be repeatedly handled during transport and use.**

Your class notes use a PET bottle and a polypropylene school chair to demonstrate this style of application. The chair example links chemical/water resistance and insulation to service conditions, and toughness, tensile/compressive strength and hardness to its mechanical requirements. fileciteturn43file0L217-L240

## What to remember

1. Know the difference between **mechanical/working** and **physical** properties.
2. Be able to distinguish **tensile, compression, torsion and shear** loading.
3. Do not confuse **hardness** with **toughness**, or **ductility** with **malleability**.
4. Know **electrical, thermal and optical** property terms as well.
5. **Cost is not a material property.**
6. In application questions, always connect the property to the product requirement rather than simply listing property words.
