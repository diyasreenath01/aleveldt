<section class="note-panel"><h2>Third-Party Feedback</h2><div class="note-intro"><p><strong>Third-party feedback</strong> is information gathered from people other than the designer. It can make evaluation more objective because designers may overlook problems in their own work.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Who can provide feedback?</h3><ul><li>target users and clients</li><li>focus groups</li><li>manufacturing specialists and engineers</li><li>retailers or marketing specialists</li><li>experts such as ergonomists, healthcare professionals or accessibility specialists where relevant</li></ul><p>The best source depends on what is being tested. A target user is useful for comfort and usability; a manufacturing engineer may identify production or assembly problems.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Ways of collecting feedback</h3><ul><li><strong>User trials:</strong> observe people interacting with a prototype in a realistic task.</li><li><strong>Interviews:</strong> allow detailed follow-up questions.</li><li><strong>Questionnaires:</strong> useful for collecting comparable responses from more people.</li><li><strong>Focus groups:</strong> target users discuss the product together and may reveal attitudes to appearance, controls, usability, price or desirability.</li><li><strong>Expert review:</strong> specialists assess technical, safety, ergonomic or manufacturing issues.</li></ul></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Making feedback useful</h3><p>Feedback should be relevant to the intended user and linked to the specification. Avoid relying only on vague comments such as 'I like it'. Ask focused questions or observe measurable performance.</p><p>Designers should also consider the reliability of feedback. A very small or unrepresentative group may not reflect the target market, and leading questions can introduce bias.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Feedback is part of iteration</h3><p>The important design process is <strong>prototype → third-party feedback/testing → analyse findings → modify → retest</strong>. Feedback only becomes valuable when it informs a justified design decision.</p><p>Example: several elderly users struggle to grip a small smooth control → increase its diameter and add a textured elastomer surface → retest with the same user group to see whether operation improves.</p></div><div class="exam-tip"><strong>Talking point:</strong> third-party feedback can reduce designer bias and reveal issues the design team missed, but its value depends on choosing appropriate participants and collecting evidence in a structured way.</div></section>
