<section class="note-panel"><h2>Critical Analysis and Evaluation</h2><div class="note-intro"><p><strong>Critical analysis</strong> is an objective examination of a design, not simply a description of whether it is 'good' or 'bad'. <strong>Evaluation</strong> uses evidence to judge how successfully a product or prototype meets its intended requirements and to identify justified improvements.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Paper 2 draws on your technical knowledge</h3><p>Evaluation is much stronger when it is supported by the technical principles learned elsewhere in the course. AQA examinations can require students to <strong>draw together information from different areas of the specification</strong>, so when analysing a product you should be ready to apply knowledge of materials, properties, manufacturing processes, scales of production, CAD/CAM, accuracy, finishes, fixings, sustainability and other relevant technical principles.</p><p>For example, do not only say that a chair is suitable for mass production. Use technical knowledge to explain <strong>why</strong>: a moulded polymer shell can be produced repeatedly after the tooling has been made, giving consistent forms and reducing unit cost at high production volumes.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>What should be analysed?</h3><ul><li><strong>Function and fitness for purpose:</strong> does it reliably do what it was designed to do?</li><li><strong>User needs:</strong> ergonomics, anthropometrics, accessibility, comfort and ease of use.</li><li><strong>Aesthetics:</strong> form, colour, texture, proportion and suitability for the target market.</li><li><strong>Materials:</strong> properties, durability, cost, availability and environmental impact.</li><li><strong>Manufacture:</strong> processes, scale of production, accuracy, tolerances, assembly and likely cost.</li><li><strong>Safety:</strong> hazards, misuse and relevant standards.</li><li><strong>Sustainability:</strong> material sourcing, energy, repair, disassembly, product life and end-of-life.</li></ul></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Use evidence, not opinion</h3><p>Strong evaluation is based on measurable evidence: user trials, dimensions, performance tests, questionnaires, observations, material tests or comparison with the design specification.</p><p>For example, rather than writing <em>'the handle is uncomfortable'</em>, state what the user test showed, explain why the shape or size caused the problem and propose a measurable modification.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Testing against a specification</h3><p>Each relevant specification point should be tested where possible. Quantitative criteria such as size, mass, load or time can be measured directly. Qualitative criteria such as comfort or appearance can use structured user feedback.</p><p>A useful chain is: <strong>criterion → test → result → judgement → modification → retest</strong>.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Balanced judgement</h3><p>Critical evaluation should recognise trade-offs. A thicker component may increase strength but also increase mass, material use and cost. A sealed enclosure may improve water resistance but make repair and battery replacement harder.</p><p>This kind of balanced reasoning is useful in extended-response questions because it shows that design decisions rarely have only one consequence.</p></div><div class="exam-tip"><strong>Exam technique:</strong> make every point contextual. Identify the design feature, apply the relevant technical knowledge, explain its consequence for the user, manufacturer or environment, then make a justified judgement rather than listing generic advantages and disadvantages.</div></section>
