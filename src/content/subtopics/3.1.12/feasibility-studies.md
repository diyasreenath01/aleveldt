<section class="note-panel"><h2>Feasibility studies</h2><div class="note-intro"><p><strong>Feasibility</strong> asks whether a proposed product is practical and likely to succeed when the relevant constraints are considered.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Factors affecting feasibility</h3><ul><li>Manufacturing time.</li><li>Demand and scale of production.</li><li>Whether the product is fit for purpose.</li><li>Manufacturing and other costs.</li><li>Whether the product can be profitable after expenses are considered.</li></ul></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Computer modelling and lean manufacturing</h3><p>Lean manufacturing aims to minimise wasted time and materials. Computer modelling can support feasibility assessment through simulation and planning, bottleneck prediction, cycle-time analysis and takt-time planning.</p><p>Spreadsheets can calculate production costs. More specialised software can model costs such as tooling, material use and machine cycle time against the number of units to be produced.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Bottlenecks</h3><p>Production bottlenecks can result from poorly designed workflows, outdated machinery, shortages of key staff, insufficient machine capacity, lack of automation or poor forecasting.</p><p>They can be reduced by training and cross-training staff, improving workflows, standardising or automating suitable processes, improving inventory management and investing in appropriate equipment or business systems.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Cycle time and takt time</h3><p><strong>Cycle time</strong> is the time taken to complete a particular task or produce one unit from start to finish.</p><p><strong>Takt time</strong> is the required production rate needed to meet customer demand.</p><p><strong>takt time = available production time per day ÷ customer demand per day</strong></p><p>Example from the notes: 1000 available minutes ÷ 500 pieces = 2 minutes per piece.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Computer modelling examples</h3><p>A kitchen can be modelled to assess ergonomics and the efficiency of the working triangle before installation. The notes also use a Boeing 787 engine as an example: computer modelling can examine assembly sequence and how components may later be disassembled for repair, replacement or recycling before an expensive product is manufactured.</p></div></section>
