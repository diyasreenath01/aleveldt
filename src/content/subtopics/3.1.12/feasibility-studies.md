<section class="note-panel"><h2>Feasibility Studies</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>A <strong>feasibility study</strong> is used to decide whether a proposed design is <strong>practical for production</strong> before significant time and money are committed to manufacture.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>What should be considered?</h3><p>Useful factors include:</p><ul><li><strong>Function:</strong> will the design work and be fit for purpose?</li><li><strong>User:</strong> does it meet the needs of the intended consumer?</li><li><strong>Manufacture:</strong> can it be made using suitable materials, processes, equipment and skills?</li><li><strong>Time and scale:</strong> can the required quantity be produced within the available time?</li><li><strong>Cost:</strong> are material, labour, tooling and production costs realistic?</li><li><strong>Demand:</strong> is there sufficient demand to justify production?</li></ul><p>The important exam skill is being able to explain <strong>why a factor could make a particular design feasible or unfeasible</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>How feasibility can be assessed</h3><p><strong>Computer modelling</strong> can test ideas before physical manufacture. It can be used to assess dimensions, ergonomics, assembly, performance, production planning and costs, depending on the product.</p><p><strong>Prototype testing</strong> provides physical evidence. A prototype can be tested for function and ergonomics and, importantly, can be tested with <strong>potential consumers</strong> to determine whether the design is practical and meets their needs.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Why test before production?</h3><ul><li>Problems can be identified before expensive tooling or full production begins.</li><li>Designs can be modified using evidence from modelling, testing and consumer feedback.</li><li>Material, production and financial risks can be reduced.</li><li>Designers can make a better-informed decision about whether to proceed with manufacture.</li></ul></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Production planning — useful supporting knowledge</h3><p>Computer modelling may also be used to investigate whether a production system can meet demand. Two useful terms from the lesson material are:</p><ul><li><strong>Cycle time:</strong> the time required to complete a manufacturing task or produce one unit.</li><li><strong>Takt time:</strong> the rate at which products need to be completed to meet customer demand. <strong>Takt time = available production time ÷ customer demand.</strong></li></ul><p>These ideas can support an answer about production feasibility, but the main AQA requirement is understanding <strong>how feasibility studies assess the practicality of producing a proposed design</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Exam technique</h3><p>If asked how a feasibility study could be carried out, give an appropriate method and explain what it would reveal.</p><p><strong>Example:</strong> A designer could produce a prototype of a new cup carrier and test it with potential consumers. They could assess whether it securely holds the cups, is comfortable to carry and is an appropriate size. Feedback could then be used to modify the design before committing to production.</p></div></section>
