<section class="note-panel"><h2>User-centred design (UCD)</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>User-centred design (UCD) means design focused around the end user. Designers investigate real users rather than designing around assumptions about them. AQA specifically expects designers to consider <strong>human factors, anthropometric data and percentiles, and ergonomic data</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>User involvement</h3><ul>
<li>End user involvement at the development stage makes the success of products more likely.</li>
<li>Tests with potential users in real-world situations facilitate objective product evaluations.</li>
<li>Useful research methods include interviews, observations, focus groups, product analysis and task analysis.</li>
</ul></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Anthropometrics</h3><p><strong>Anthropometrics</strong> is the study and measurement of human body dimensions. Designers use anthropometric data to decide dimensions such as seat height, reach distance, clearance, handle size and working height.</p><p>Measurements vary between people, so data is commonly presented using <strong>percentiles</strong>. The 5th percentile represents a relatively small user and the 95th percentile a relatively large user. Designers often aim to accommodate a broad range such as the 5th to 95th percentile, but the percentile selected depends on the design problem.</p><p><strong>Clearance</strong> dimensions usually need to accommodate larger users, while <strong>reach</strong> dimensions may need to accommodate smaller users. Adjustable products can accommodate a wider range of users.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Ergonomics</h3><p><strong>Ergonomics</strong> is the study of how people interact with products and their working environment. Good ergonomic design aims to improve <strong>comfort, safety, efficiency and ease of use</strong>.</p><p>Ergonomic decisions can involve posture, grip, force, reach, visibility, control layout, feedback, repetitive movement and the physical effort required to operate a product. Anthropometric measurements provide dimensions; ergonomics considers how effectively and comfortably the person actually interacts with the design.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Anthropometrics vs ergonomics</h3><p><strong>Anthropometrics = human measurements.</strong><br><strong>Ergonomics = designing the interaction between the user, product and environment.</strong></p><p>For example, hand-size data used to choose a handle diameter is anthropometric; shaping and texturing that handle so it is comfortable, secure and requires less strain is ergonomic.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Task analysis</h3><ul>
<li>Task analysis is useful when analysing a product.</li>
<li>Potential users need to be monitored while they perform specific tasks with a product to identify issues with existing products or prototypes in development.</li>
</ul>
<p><strong>Task analysis:</strong> improving products by close study of users carrying out a range of tasks.</p>
<p><strong>User-centred design (UCD):</strong> designing for product users’ actual needs, not the designer’s perception of them; consumers’ needs are prioritised at all stages of the process.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Inclusive design</h3><p>This is a key part of user-centred design (UCD), facilitating maximum accessibility of products.</p><ul>
<li>Inclusive design aims to meet the needs of the widest possible range of people who might interact with the product in question. This includes groups such as the elderly, people with disabilities and children.</li>
<li>Empathy with the target user is a key part of designing inclusively for a range of abilities.</li>
<li>Empathic design involves the designer analysing tasks while emulating user restrictions.</li>
<li>Ford Focus designers wore a ‘third age suit’ to experience the problems of less mobile motorists. The suit restricts joint movement, reduces the ability to grip and simulates visual impairment.</li>
<li>Inclusive design of hand tools avoids problems such as undue stress on joints and slow working.</li>
<li>Small, intricate movements such as using pencils and scalpels require a precision grip. Precision grip involves the thumb and index finger, and is usually between 8 and 16 mm diameter.</li>
<li>Large forces require a power grip, where the hand wraps around a handle. Power grip for tools like screwdrivers and hammers requires a diameter of 30–50 mm.</li>
<li>Indented finger grips should be avoided to reduce discomfort for small or large hands.</li>
<li>Gentle curves, with appropriate texture, offer multiple gripping positions for the user.</li>
</ul></div></section>
