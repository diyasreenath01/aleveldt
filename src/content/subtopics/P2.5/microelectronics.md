<section class="note-panel"><h2>Major Developments in Technology</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>Technological developments change <strong>what products can do</strong>, <strong>how designers develop them</strong>, and <strong>how they are manufactured</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Microelectronics and Miniaturisation</h3><p><strong>Microelectronics</strong> involves very small electronic components and circuits, particularly <strong>transistors</strong> and <strong>integrated circuits (ICs)</strong>. Their development has allowed electronic products to become progressively <strong>smaller, lighter, more reliable, more energy efficient and more powerful</strong>.</p><p>A useful development sequence to remember is <strong>thermionic valve → discrete transistor → integrated circuit → modern microprocessor / system-on-chip</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Thermionic Valves / Vacuum Tubes</h3><p>Early electronic products used <strong>thermionic valves</strong>, also called <strong>vacuum tubes</strong>. A valve controls the flow of electric current inside a sealed glass vacuum. An electric current heats a metal filament or <strong>cathode</strong>, releasing electrons by <strong>thermionic emission</strong>. A positively charged <strong>anode</strong> attracts the electrons, while a control grid can regulate the flow of current.</p><p>Valves made complex electronic products possible and were used in <strong>radios, amplifiers, televisions, communications equipment and early computers</strong>. During the Second World War they were produced in very large quantities for equipment including radio transmitters and receivers, radar and telecommunications.</p><p>However, valves created major design limitations:</p><ul><li><strong>Large and bulky:</strong> glass tubes and associated wiring required large product housings.</li><li><strong>High power consumption:</strong> the filament had to be heated continuously.</li><li><strong>Heat:</strong> significant energy was wasted as heat, so equipment could require cooling.</li><li><strong>Fragile:</strong> glass envelopes and filaments could break or fail.</li><li><strong>Poor portability:</strong> their size and power requirements made small battery-powered products difficult.</li></ul><p>These limitations explain why early electronic equipment and computers were physically very large despite performing relatively limited functions.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>1947: The Transistor Breakthrough</h3><p>In <strong>1947</strong>, John Bardeen, Walter Brattain and William Shockley developed the point-contact <strong>transistor</strong> at Bell Laboratories. Unlike a valve, a transistor controls current using a solid <strong>semiconductor</strong> rather than a heated filament inside a vacuum.</p><p>A <strong>semiconductor</strong> is a material whose electrical conductivity lies between that of a conductor and an insulator and can be controlled electrically. Semiconductor materials such as <strong>germanium and silicon</strong> enabled the development of solid-state electronics.</p><p>A transistor can work as a <strong>switch</strong> or an <strong>amplifier</strong>. A small input can control a larger current, allowing electronic signals to be switched or amplified.</p><p>Compared with thermionic valves, transistors offered:</p><ul><li><strong>Much smaller size</strong> — allowing smaller product housings and greater miniaturisation.</li><li><strong>Lower power consumption</strong> — no heated filament was required.</li><li><strong>Less heat</strong> — improving efficiency and reducing cooling requirements.</li><li><strong>Greater durability</strong> — solid-state components were less fragile than glass valves.</li><li><strong>Battery operation</strong> — low voltage requirements made genuinely portable electronics practical.</li><li><strong>Improved reliability</strong> — fewer fragile components meant fewer failures.</li></ul></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Product Example: Regency TR-1 Pocket Radio</h3><p>The <strong>Regency TR-1</strong>, introduced in <strong>1954</strong>, is a useful example of how a technological development changed product design. Earlier domestic radios were typically large products housed in wooden cabinets and connected to mains electricity. The TR-1 used four small transistors instead of bulky valves and could fit into a coat pocket.</p><p>This changed both the <strong>physical form</strong> and the <strong>way the product was used</strong>. Radio became personal and portable: users could carry music and news rather than using a fixed radio in the home. This is an important example of technology creating a new type of consumer behaviour as well as simply making an existing product smaller.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Integrated Circuits (ICs)</h3><p>As electronic circuits became more complex, connecting thousands of separate transistors, resistors and capacitors became bulky and difficult. In the late 1950s, developments associated with <strong>Jack Kilby and Robert Noyce</strong> led to the <strong>integrated circuit (IC)</strong>, where an entire electronic circuit could be formed on a single piece of semiconductor material.</p><p>By the <strong>mid-1960s</strong>, microelectronic ICs could contain around <strong>500 transistors on one small chip</strong>. Instead of assembling every transistor separately, many electronic functions could therefore be integrated into one compact component.</p><p>This further reduced product size while increasing <strong>processing capability, reliability and functionality</strong>. It also reduced the amount of separate wiring and assembly required.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>ULSI and Modern Microelectronics</h3><p>Continued improvements in semiconductor manufacture allowed more transistors to be placed onto increasingly small chips. By the <strong>1980s</strong>, highly integrated microprocessors could contain <strong>over one million transistors</strong>. This helped make compact computers, laptops and mobile electronic products commercially practical.</p><p>Modern fabrication works at <strong>nanometre scale</strong> and can place <strong>billions of transistors</strong> onto a single chip. A smartphone can therefore combine processing, graphics, communications and many other functions in a pocket-sized product.</p><p>The overall trend is <strong>more electronic functions in less physical space</strong>. This is one of the clearest examples of how a major technological development has shaped product design.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>How Microelectronics Changed Product Design and Manufacture</h3><ul><li><strong>Miniaturisation:</strong> products became smaller, thinner and lighter.</li><li><strong>Portability:</strong> low-power electronics made battery-powered products practical.</li><li><strong>More functions:</strong> many electronic functions can be combined into one product.</li><li><strong>Improved reliability:</strong> solid-state components have fewer fragile or moving parts.</li><li><strong>Lower energy use and heat:</strong> modern components require less power than thermionic valves.</li><li><strong>Reduced cost:</strong> large numbers of electronic components can be manufactured together on chips.</li><li><strong>Improved usability:</strong> digital displays, touch controls and intelligent functions can be incorporated into products.</li><li><strong>Changes to manufacture:</strong> electronic assembly has moved from extensive hand-wired components towards highly automated manufacture of miniature circuits and printed circuit assemblies.</li></ul><p><strong>Exam link:</strong> Do not simply state that technology made products smaller. Explain the chain of effect: <strong>smaller components → lower power and heat → battery operation → smaller product housings → portability → more functions and improved usability</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Microelectronics in Modern Products</h3><p>Portable music players show how several technological developments can combine:</p><ul><li><strong>Rechargeable batteries</strong> made repeated portable use possible.</li><li><strong>MP3 compression</strong> reduced the storage needed for each song.</li><li><strong>Flash memory</strong> stored large numbers of files without moving parts, improving shock resistance and reducing power use.</li><li><strong>LCD displays and touchscreens</strong> allowed compact products to display information and replace many physical controls.</li><li><strong>Streaming and wireless communication</strong> allowed content to be accessed online rather than stored entirely on the device.</li></ul><p>Greater electronic capability can also create new design considerations, including <strong>battery life, heat management, repairability, software support, data security and electronic waste</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>The Internet of Things</h3><p>The <strong>Internet of Things (IoT)</strong> connects physical devices to networks so they can collect and exchange data. For example, sensors in factory equipment can detect changes in performance and help predict when maintenance is needed. Designers must also consider data privacy, security and whether the product can function without a connection.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>How Technology Changed Design and Manufacture</h2><div class="note-intro"><p><strong>Computer aided design (CAD)</strong> lets designers create, edit and test digital models. <strong>Computer aided manufacture (CAM)</strong> uses digital information to control manufacturing equipment, such as CNC machines.</p><p>Digital models can be shared with other designers, modified quickly and tested before a physical prototype is made. In factories, CNC machinery, robotics and digital quality checks can improve accuracy and repeatability. However, digital predictions still need to be checked through physical testing.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Key developments</h3><ul><li><strong>3D printing:</strong> Builds a part layer by layer. It is useful for prototypes, customised parts and complex forms.</li><li><strong>Finite element analysis (FEA):</strong> Predicts how a model may respond to forces or heat, helping designers identify weak areas.</li><li><strong>Computational fluid dynamics (CFD):</strong> Models how liquids or gases flow around or through a design.</li><li><strong>Virtual reality (VR):</strong> Allows designers to experience a digital model at scale and assess features such as layout and ergonomics.</li><li><strong>Cloud based CAD:</strong> Allows people in different locations to work on shared design files.</li></ul></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>New Materials</h2><div class="note-intro"><p>The choice of material should be explained through a link between its <strong>properties</strong> and its <strong>use</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Glulam</h3><p>Layers of bonded timber form strong, lightweight beams that can be curved. Typical uses include buildings and bridges with large spans.</p><p><strong>Example:</strong> Glulam can be formed into a curved roof beam. Bonding several timber layers together helps produce the required shape while distributing defects found in individual pieces of wood.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Kevlar</h3><p>Strong, tough and lightweight fibre. Used for protective equipment, puncture resistant tyres and aircraft components.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Graphene</h3><p>Extremely thin carbon sheet with high tensile strength and good electrical and thermal conductivity. Potential uses include sensors, electronics, coatings and batteries.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Precious metal clay</h3><p>Mouldable metal particles that form a solid metal object after firing. Used for jewellery and decorative products.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>New Manufacturing Methods</h2>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Electrohydraulic Forming</h3><p>An electrical discharge creates a shockwave in water, forcing sheet metal against a former. This can form detailed shapes quickly, including <strong>car parts and appliance casings</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Metal Additive Manufacturing</h3><p>A laser fuses metal powder <strong>layer by layer</strong> to build a part from a digital model. This can produce complex shapes and internal channels that would be difficult to machine. It is useful for <strong>prototypes and customised parts</strong>, although finishing may still be required.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Fibre Injection Moulding</h3><p>A polymer mixed with reinforcing fibres is injected into a mould. The fibres can improve the <strong>strength and stiffness</strong> of lightweight parts used in products such as vehicles and sports equipment.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Laser Beam Welding</h3><p>A concentrated laser joins metal with a <strong>narrow, precise weld</strong>. It is useful for thin components and can cause less distortion than a wider heat source.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Physical Vapour Deposition</h3><p><strong>PVD</strong> deposits a thin coating onto a product's surface. The coating can improve <strong>wear resistance or appearance</strong>, for example on cutting tools or decorative products.</p></div></section>
