<section class="note-panel"><h2>Health and safety</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>Health and safety is everyone’s responsibility and employers have a duty of care to ensure that procedures are implemented to keep employees safe, and to prevent them from being harmed or becoming ill through work.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Safe working practices</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>Employers should ensure the following:</p>
<p>• The workplace is safe and free from risks to health (such as use of extraction systems to remove harmful dust or fumes).<br> • Welfare facilities such as first aid are provided.<br> • Dangerous items and substances are used and stored safely, and removed from use if necessary.<br> • Training, information and adequate supervision are provided as necessary.<br> • Personal protective equipment (PPE) is provided.<br> • Machinery has guards in place, is safe to use, maintained and in good working order.<br> • Appropriate safety signage is provided.<br> • Accident reporting systems are in place, RIDDOR (Reporting of Injuries, Diseases and Dangerous Occurrences Regulations, 2013).</p>
<p>Employees should ensure they:</p>
<p>• take reasonable care for their own health and safety, as well as that of those in the vicinity<br> • use the personal protective equipment (PPE) provided<br> • use machinery in accordance with the given training instructions<br> • report any accidents, near miss or dangerous event.</p>
<p>Employers must comply with specific legislation to protect employees in the manufacturing industry.</p>
<p>The <strong>Health and Safety Executive (HSE)</strong> is a national independent watchdog for work-related health, safety and illness.</p>
<p>• The HSE helps companies and employers to meet health and safety obligations.<br> • They produce guidance documents and investigate accidents in the workplace.</p></div>
<div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Health and Safety at Work Act (1974)</h3>
<p>• The <strong>Health and Safety at Work Act (HSWA)</strong> is the main piece of legislation for British health and safety law.<br> • To comply, all employers must ensure (so far as is reasonably practicable) that all employees and visitors to the workplace are protected in terms of health, safety and welfare.<br> • Compliance includes assessing risks and adopting appropriate safety measures to provide a safe environment for product manufacture.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Key terms</h3>
<p><strong>Health and Safety Executive (HSE):</strong> a national independent watchdog for work-related health, safety and illness.</p>
<p><strong>Health and Safety at Work Act (1974):</strong> the main piece of legislation for British health and safety law.</p></div>

<section class="note-panel"><h2>Control of Substances Hazardous to Health regulations (2002)</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>• To comply with <strong>Control of Substances Hazardous to Health regulations (COSHH)</strong> employers need to prevent, reduce or control their workers’ exposure, as well as any workers in the vicinity, to substances that may be hazardous or cause ill health.<br> • Hazardous substances include:<br> ○ paints, varnishes, cleaning agents and adhesives that may be used during work<br> ○ fumes that may be created as a result of a process such as soldering or brazing<br> ○ airborne particles such as dust created by cutting or sanding timber.<br> • Hazardous substances will usually show a symbol that meets the classification labelling and packaging (CLP) regulations.<br> • The CLP hazard pictograms in Figure 23.1 allow easy identification of a hazard as corrosive, flammable, toxic, etc.</p></div>
<div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Key term</h3>
<p><strong>Control of Substances Hazardous to Health regulations (2002):</strong> legislation to prevent, reduce or control people’s exposure to hazardous substances in the workplace.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Safe working practices</h3>
<p>These are primarily the same for schools, colleges and industry; they all serve to keep the person doing the work, as well as those in the vicinity, safe and free from harm.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Table 23.1 General guidelines to keep people safe and free from harm</h3>
<p>• Do not operate any machinery or equipment unless you have been trained in its use.<br> • PPE must be worn when using machinery.<br> • All guards and safety measures should be utilised.<br> • Work areas should be kept neat, clean and free of hazards. Spillages should be reported.<br> • All persons must be aware of the emergency procedures, such as fire evacuation routes, locations of fire extinguishers, emergency stops, etc.<br> • Walkways should be kept clear and free from trip hazards.<br> • If a person becomes ill or injured or in the event of an accident, this should be reported to a supervisor/teacher.</p>
<p>In a school these guidelines may be produced as workshop safety rules.</p>
<p>In industry there may be additional measures in place. For example:</p>
<p>• Where there is movement of goods around a factory floor, there may be designated safe zones or walkways that must be kept free from machinery.<br> • Forklift trucks or vehicles will usually be fitted with a flashing light and/or reverse signal alarm to alert those near the vehicle movements.<br> • Job rotation may also be used so that workers are not at risk of repetitive strain injury (RSI) or injury caused by a lapse in concentration from doing the same task with no variation.</p></div></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Safety precautions</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>• These refer to any action carried out in advance that protects against a possible danger or injury.<br> • For example, when sanding wood on a disc sander, the <strong>safety precautions</strong> would include wearing eye protection and the use of a dust extraction system.</p>

<div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Key terms</h3>
<p><strong>Safety precautions:</strong> any action carried out in advance that protects against a possible danger or injury.</p>
<p><strong>Risk assessment:</strong> a statutory process that considers what might cause harm to people and what reasonable steps might be taken to prevent that harm.</p>
<p><strong>Hazard:</strong> anything that could cause someone harm.</p></div></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Risk assessment</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>• An employer is required to carry these out by law. They should be recorded in any establishment where there are five or more employees.<br> • The <strong>risk assessment</strong> must consider what might cause harm to people, and if reasonable steps are being taken to prevent that harm.<br> • Not all risks can be eliminated, but the employer must take all reasonably practicable measures to identify potential <strong>hazards</strong> and minimise the risk.<br> • Risk assessments must be reviewed regularly by the employer.</p></div>
<div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Table 23.2 An example of a basic risk assessment for a manufacturing process</h3>
<p><strong>Process<br> </strong>Cutting wood on a band saw</p>
<p><strong>Hazard(s)<br> </strong>Cut risk<br> Inhalation of wood dust particles<br> Particles in the eye</p>
<p><strong>Potential harm<br> </strong>The machine operator<br> Persons in the immediate vicinity</p>
<p><strong>Risk and control measure<br> </strong>Risk level is medium<br> Provide training prior to machine use<br> Provide access to machine operating manual<br> Provision of machine guards and push sticks<br> Use of dust extraction system during machine operation<br> Good ventilation in the workplace<br> Provision of eye goggles<br> Post safety signs on machine and in vicinity<br> Mark out a safe zone around the machine that only the operator can enter</p>
<p><strong>Review date<br> </strong>The risk assessment would be signed and dated by the person assessing the risk. The 6- or 12-month review date would also be noted.</p></div></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Exam practice from my notes</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>COSHH QUESTION</p>
<p>COSHH regulations protect workers in manufacturing environments by ensuring hazardous substances are identified and controlled to reduce risks to health. Employers must first identify hazards such as toxic fumes, dust, solvents, adhesives, paints or chemicals that could cause breathing problems, burns, skin irritation or long-term illnesses.</p>
<p>A COSHH risk assessment is then carried out to determine how workers may be exposed and what control measures are needed to prevent harm. For example, in a workshop using spray paints or adhesives, extraction and ventilation systems may be installed to remove harmful fumes from the air.</p>
<p>COSHH also requires employers to provide suitable personal protective equipment (PPE) such as gloves, goggles and respirators to reduce direct contact with hazardous substances. Workers handling corrosive chemicals may wear chemical-resistant gloves and eye protection to prevent burns or injuries.</p>
<p>The regulations ensure that control measures are maintained and used correctly. Ventilation systems and extraction equipment must be regularly inspected to make sure they remain effective. Hazardous substances must also be stored safely in labelled containers to reduce accidental exposure.</p>
<p>Employers must provide training and information so workers understand the risks associated with substances and know the correct handling procedures. Safety data sheets and warning labels help employees use chemicals safely and respond correctly in emergencies such as spills or leaks.</p>
