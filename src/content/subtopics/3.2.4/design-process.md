<section class="note-panel"><h2>Design Processes</h2><div class="note-intro"><p>A <strong>design process</strong> is a structured way to develop a solution. Most product design is <strong>iterative</strong>: designers test ideas, receive feedback and return to earlier stages to improve them. A <strong>linear process</strong> follows stages in a fixed order with little return to earlier decisions. This may suit a clearly defined one-off commission, but iterative development is valuable when testing reveals changing needs or problems.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Design Brief and Research</h3><p>A <strong>design brief</strong> states the problem, intended user and purpose of the product without deciding the final solution.</p><p>Research can include observing and interviewing users, analysing or disassembling existing products, testing materials, and investigating the intended environment. Findings should influence decisions. For example, if users struggle to grip an existing handle, the designer should investigate its shape, dimensions and surface before developing alternatives.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Design Specification</h3><p>A <strong>design specification</strong> lists requirements the product should meet. It may cover <strong>function, users, size, ergonomics, materials, safety, appearance, cost, maintenance and environmental impact</strong>.</p><p>Requirements should be specific enough to test. "The box must hold six tools securely when carried" is more useful than "the box must have good storage". Designers use the specification during development and again when evaluating the finished product.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Generating Ideas</h3><p>Designers use sketches, CAD, models, mood boards and discussion to explore ideas.</p><p><strong>SCAMPER</strong> provides prompts for changing a design: <strong>Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to another use, Eliminate and Reverse</strong>.</p><p>A <strong>4×4 method</strong> begins with one idea, which is developed into four alternatives. Each alternative is then developed further, producing a wider range of ideas to compare.</p><p><strong>Six Thinking Hats</strong> is a discussion method that asks a group to consider an idea from different viewpoints, such as facts, benefits, risks and creative possibilities. It helps prevent a discussion from being dominated by one perspective.</p><p><strong>Collaborative working</strong> brings together people with different expertise, such as designers, engineers, manufacturers and users. Their feedback can reveal problems that one designer may miss.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Modelling, Planning and Evaluation</h3><p>A <strong>sketch model</strong> is a quick way to explore an idea. A <strong>scale model</strong> communicates shape and proportions, while a <strong>full size model</strong> is useful for checking ergonomics and interaction.</p><p>Manufacturing plans identify materials, equipment, the order of operations and quality checks. <strong>Flow charts</strong> show stages and decisions clearly.</p><p><strong>Evaluation takes place throughout the process.</strong> Designers test ideas against the specification, gather user feedback, make changes and test again. A final evaluation explains how well the product meets its requirements and identifies justified improvements.</p></div></section>
