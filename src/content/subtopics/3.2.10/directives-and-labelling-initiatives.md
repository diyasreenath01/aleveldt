## Product directives and environmental labelling

A **standard** gives recognised technical requirements or methods. A **directive or legal requirement** places requirements on products or manufacturers, while an **eco-label** communicates specific environmental information or certification to consumers.

For product designers, these requirements can directly affect **material and component selection, product construction, disassembly, disposal, recycling and labelling**.

### RoHS — Restriction of Hazardous Substances

**RoHS** restricts the use of specified hazardous substances in electrical and electronic equipment. The teacher notes identify **lead, mercury and cadmium** as examples of substances whose use is restricted.

This affects designers because electrical products may need alternative **materials, finishes, solder and electronic components** that comply with the restrictions. Reducing hazardous substances also makes electrical waste safer to handle when products reach the end of their life.

### WEEE — Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment

**WEEE** deals with waste electrical and electronic equipment at the **end of its useful life**. Its main purpose is to reduce the environmental impact of electronic waste by reducing disposal and increasing **reuse, recycling and recovery**. Electrical waste can contain valuable finite materials as well as substances that can cause environmental or health problems if the waste is poorly treated.

#### Main approaches used by WEEE

- **Separate collection:** electrical and electronic products should be collected separately from ordinary household waste. This allows them to be sent for appropriate treatment rather than simply entering general landfill or incineration.
- **Return and collection systems:** convenient collection facilities encourage consumers to return unwanted electrical products so that useful components and materials can be recovered.
- **Reuse:** products or usable components should be reused where practical instead of immediately becoming waste. This extends their useful life and reduces demand for replacement materials and components.
- **Recycling:** materials are separated and processed so they can be used again. Designing products so different materials can be identified and separated makes this easier.
- **Recovery:** useful value is recovered from discarded equipment, including components and materials, rather than treating the whole product as waste.
- **Design for dismantling:** designers are encouraged to make electrical products easier to take apart so components and materials can be removed for reuse, treatment or recycling. Permanent joining methods or constructions that unnecessarily prevent disassembly can make recovery more difficult.
- **Producer responsibility:** producers take greater responsibility for the end-of-life management of electrical products, including the collection, treatment, recovery and disposal of WEEE. This gives manufacturers an incentive to consider end-of-life during product development rather than leaving disposal entirely to the consumer.
- **Information for treatment and recovery:** information about components and materials helps organisations handling WEEE identify, dismantle, treat and recover them correctly.

#### WEEE symbol

The **crossed-out wheeled bin** symbol shows that the electrical or electronic product should **not be disposed of with unsorted household waste**. It should enter a separate collection system so it can be appropriately treated, reused, recycled or recovered.

#### Why WEEE affects product design

WEEE is not only about what happens after a product is thrown away. It can influence how the product is designed in the first place. A designer may use **removable fixings, separable materials, identifiable components and a construction that can be dismantled** so valuable parts can be recovered more easily.

These approaches can reduce the quantity of electrical waste going to landfill or incineration and reduce demand for finite raw materials by keeping components and materials in use. The AQA specification specifically requires knowledge of **WEEE directives**, so the important link is between the directive and the design decisions it encourages.

### Battery requirements

The AQA specification refers to the **Battery Directive**. Battery requirements aim to reduce the environmental harm caused by waste batteries and support their **separate collection, treatment and recycling**.

For designers, this can affect how batteries are incorporated into products. Making a battery identifiable and accessible can make **removal, replacement and end-of-life treatment** easier instead of forcing the whole product to be discarded with the battery still inside.

### Packaging requirements

Packaging directives aim to reduce the environmental impact of packaging waste and encourage **recovery and recycling**.

Designers can respond by reducing unnecessary packaging, lowering its **weight and volume**, choosing materials that can be recovered or recycled, avoiding unnecessarily difficult material combinations and providing appropriate material identification where required. The packaging must still protect the product effectively, so reducing material should not make it fail its protective function.

### Polymer identification codes

Polymer identification codes show the **type of polymer** used in a product or component. This helps different plastics to be identified and separated during recycling because different polymers can have different processing requirements.

Correct separation matters because contamination with incompatible polymers can reduce the quality of recycled material. However, an identification code does **not** mean that an item will definitely be recycled; suitable collection and recycling facilities must also exist.

### Eco-labelling

**Eco-labels** communicate particular environmental information to consumers. They may relate to recyclability, responsible sourcing, energy efficiency or compliance with defined environmental criteria.

They can help consumers compare products and can encourage manufacturers to improve environmental performance. However, the meaning of the particular label matters: one environmental label does **not** prove that every part of a product or its whole life cycle is sustainable. Vague or unsupported environmental claims can also contribute to **greenwashing**.

### Main labels required by AQA

- **Mobius Loop** – the three-arrow recycling symbol. It communicates recycling-related information. Its presence alone does not guarantee that the item will actually be recycled; local collection and processing facilities still matter.
- **European Ecolabel** – a voluntary environmental label awarded to eligible products and services that meet defined environmental criteria. It gives consumers evidence that specified environmental requirements have been considered rather than simply relying on a manufacturer's own claim.
- **EC/EU energy label** – gives standardised information about the energy performance of relevant products so consumers can compare alternatives. The current main scale runs from **A to G**, with A at the more energy-efficient end.
- **Energy Efficient label/logo** – indicates that a product meets specified energy-efficiency requirements, helping consumers identify products designed to use energy more efficiently.
- **FSC — Forest Stewardship Council** – identifies forest-based materials and products linked to certified responsible forest management and controlled supply chains. It relates specifically to the sourcing of forest products; it does not mean that the complete product has no environmental impact.
- **EPA ENERGY STAR** – identifies qualifying products that meet specified energy-efficiency criteria, allowing energy performance to form part of the consumer's purchasing decision.

The key is to know **what each label or directive actually does and how it changes a design, manufacturing or purchasing decision**, rather than simply recognising its logo.
