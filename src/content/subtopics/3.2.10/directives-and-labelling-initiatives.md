## Product directives and environmental labelling

Standards, directives and labels are related but have different purposes. A **standard** gives recognised technical requirements or methods. A **directive or legal requirement** can create compulsory obligations, while an **eco-label** communicates environmental information or certification to the consumer and may be voluntary.

They can affect material selection, product construction, disposal and the information given to consumers. The important point is what each requirement is intended to achieve and how it influences design decisions.

### RoHS

**RoHS — Restriction of Hazardous Substances** restricts specified hazardous substances in electrical and electronic equipment. It affects the selection of materials, finishes and electronic components, reducing the amount of hazardous material entering the waste stream.

### WEEE

**WEEE — Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment** aims to reduce the environmental impact of electrical and electronic waste by increasing **collection, reuse, recycling and recovery**.

The **crossed-out wheeled bin** indicates that electrical or electronic equipment should be collected separately rather than placed in ordinary household waste. WEEE also encourages designers to consider **disassembly, material separation and recovery** so products and components are easier to deal with at end-of-life.

This shifts some responsibility for end-of-life treatment towards producers and encourages design decisions that make recovery and recycling more practical.

### Battery requirements

The AQA specification uses the term **Battery Directive**. Its purpose is to reduce environmental harm from batteries and support appropriate collection, treatment and recycling. Product design can affect how easily a battery can be identified, removed, replaced and recovered at end-of-life.

### Packaging requirements

Packaging requirements encourage reduced material use and improved recovery and recycling. Designers should consider **packaging volume, material choice, material identification and whether packaging can be reused or recycled**.

### Polymer identification codes

Polymer identification codes identify the type of plastic used. A number or abbreviation allows different polymers to be recognised and sorted more effectively, reducing contamination during recycling and helping produce more consistent recycled material.

The code identifies the polymer; it does **not** guarantee that the product will be recycled or that local recycling facilities accept it.

### Eco-labelling

Eco-labelling communicates environmental information to consumers. Depending on the scheme, a label may show recyclability, responsible sourcing, energy performance or that specified environmental criteria have been met.

Useful labels can make information easier to compare and may encourage manufacturers to improve environmental performance. However, consumers need to understand **what the label actually proves**. Too many labels or vague environmental claims can become confusing, and unsupported claims can contribute to **greenwashing**.

### Main labels required by AQA

- **Mobius Loop** – the familiar recycling-loop symbol. It communicates recycling-related information, but does not by itself prove that a product will actually be recycled.
- **European Ecolabel** – a voluntary scheme for products and services that meet specified environmental criteria. The criteria consider environmental impacts across more than one stage or issue rather than energy use alone.
- **EC/EU energy label** – gives standardised energy-performance information so consumers can compare relevant products. The current scale uses **A to G**, with A representing the more energy-efficient end of the scale.
- **Energy Efficient label/logo** – communicates that specified energy-efficiency criteria have been met.
- **FSC — Forest Stewardship Council** – identifies forest-based materials and products connected to certified responsible forest management and supply chains. It gives consumers evidence about sourcing rather than proving that the whole product has no environmental impact.
- **EPA ENERGY STAR** – an energy-efficiency programme used to identify qualifying products that meet specified energy-performance criteria.

Labels can influence both **design and purchasing decisions**, but a single label normally addresses a particular environmental issue. A designer should therefore consider the whole product life cycle rather than assuming one symbol makes a product sustainable.
