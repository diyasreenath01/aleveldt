<section class="note-panel"><h2>Paper and Board Processes</h2><div class="note-intro"><p>These notes follow the teacher revision material, keeping the process sequences detailed but concise.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Die cutting and creasing</h2><div class="study-card"><p><strong>What it is:</strong> A steel-rule die cuts repeated profiles/nets while creasing rules compress the material to create accurate fold lines without cutting through it.</p><h3>Step by step</h3><ol><li>Position the paper or board accurately on the cutting bed so the printed design and required cut/crease lines are correctly registered.</li><li>Align the cutting die, which contains sharpened cutting rules and lower creasing rules arranged to match the required net/profile.</li><li>Close the press and apply controlled pressure so the cutting rules pass through the material while the creasing rules compress the fibres along the fold lines.</li><li>Open the press, remove the cut component and separate the surrounding waste material.</li><li>Check that all cuts are complete, crease lines are correctly positioned and the finished component matches the required dimensions.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Packaging nets, cartons and repeated paper/card profiles; for example, folding cartons and packaging nets.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong></p><ol><li>Cutting and fold lines are formed in one operation, reducing separate stages.</li><li>A reusable steel-rule die makes identical carton nets quickly.</li><li>Accurate crease alignment helps packaging fold and assemble consistently.</li></ol><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong></p><ol><li>A dedicated die is costly for a short production run.</li><li>Blunt cutting rules can leave incomplete cuts.</li><li>Incorrect crease pressure can crack coatings or weaken board.</li></ol><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> check registration, cutting-rule sharpness, crease depth, complete cuts, fold position and dimensional consistency.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> cutting, crushing and finger-trapping hazards around the press; guards and correct operating procedures are required.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Folding / bending</h2><div class="study-card"><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Paper or board is folded accurately along a crease where the fibres have been compressed to allow a clean bend.</p><h3>Step by step</h3><ol><li>Mark or align the required fold line and position the sheet accurately in the creasing equipment.</li><li>Select a suitable creasing rule/wheel for the thickness of the paper or board.</li><li>Apply controlled pressure along the line so the fibres are compressed without cutting through the material.</li><li>Fold the material accurately along the prepared crease, using the crease to control the position and direction of the bend.</li><li>Check the finished fold for correct alignment, consistent angle and any cracking or damage to the printed surface.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Boxes, cartons, folders and packaging; for example, cartons and sleeves.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong></p><ol><li>Pre-creased board folds accurately into packaging shapes.</li><li>Simple folding is fast and suitable for repeated carton assembly.</li><li>Folding flat nets reduces transport and storage volume before assembly.</li></ol><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong></p><ol><li>Incorrect crease position causes misaligned boxes.</li><li>Thick coated board may crack along the outside of folds.</li><li>Repeated folding can weaken fibres and tear the crease.</li></ol><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> check fold alignment, crease depth and consistency and make sure the surface has not cracked.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> powered creasing/folding equipment creates finger-trap and crushing hazards; use guards and keep hands clear.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Laser cutting</h2><div class="study-card"><p><strong>What it is:</strong> A CNC-controlled laser follows digital CAD/CAM data to cut or engrave paper and board accurately without a physical cutting die.</p><h3>Step by step</h3><ol><li>Create or load the required CAD/CAM file, checking the design, dimensions and scale before machining.</li><li>Place the paper or board flat on the laser bed and secure it where required so it cannot move during cutting.</li><li>Focus the laser and set the correct power and speed for the type and thickness of material.</li><li>Run the programmed cutting/engraving path with the enclosure closed and extraction operating to remove smoke and fumes.</li><li>When the machine has finished and it is safe to open, remove the component and check dimensions, edge quality and any scorching/burn marks.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Decorative card, prototypes, model making and customised packaging; for example, card prototypes and intricate invitations.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong></p><ol><li>CAD-controlled cutting makes intricate shapes without a physical die.</li><li>Design changes are quick, making prototypes and short runs economical.</li><li>A narrow cut allows precise details and close nesting of parts.</li></ol><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong></p><ol><li>High equipment cost is difficult to justify for simple high-volume cartons.</li><li>Heat can scorch or discolour paper edges.</li><li>Smoke and fire risk require extraction and close supervision.</li></ol><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> check CAD scale, material position, laser focus, power/speed settings, cut dimensions, kerf and burn marks.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> extraction is essential because paper/board produces smoke; there is a fire risk and the laser must never be viewed directly.</p></div></section>
