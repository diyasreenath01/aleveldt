<h2>Polymer Processes</h2>
<div class="study-card"><p><strong>POLYMER PROCESSES</strong></p></div>
<h2>Vacuum forming</h2>
<div class="study-card"><p><strong>Vacuum forming is a thermoforming process used to shape heated plastic sheet over a single mould using air pressure.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>How the process works</h3>
<p><strong>The mould is placed into the former and a sheet of thermoforming plastic (such as HIPS or acrylic) is clamped over it.</strong></p>
<p><strong>The plastic sheet is heated until it becomes soft and flexible.</strong></p>
<p><strong>The mould is raised and air is sucked out, pulling the plastic tightly over the mould surface.</strong></p>
<p><strong>The plastic cools, is removed from the mould, and excess material is trimmed and finished.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Key characteristics</h3>
<p><strong>Produces thin-walled products with sloping sides and rounded corners, as the plastic stretches over the mould.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Detail is limited, especially on deep shapes, due to thinning of the plastic.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Mould requirements</h3>
<p><strong>No undercuts or overhangs, with draft angles included for easy release.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Air holes are required to remove trapped air, and corners should be rounded to reduce webbing and tearing.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Advantages</h3>
<p><strong>Suitable for batch production with relatively low-cost moulds.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Moulds are easy to modify, and products can be made in a wide range of colours.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Disadvantages</h3>
<p><strong>Excess waste from trimming.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Poor mould design can cause webbing or uneven thickness.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Not suitable for highly complex shapes.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Typical materials</h3>
<p><strong>Acrylic, HIPS, PVC</strong></p></div>
<h2>Thermoforming</h2>
<div class="study-card"><p><strong>Thermoforming is similar to vacuum forming but uses both positive and negative moulds to increase accuracy and detail.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>How the process works</h3>
<p><strong>A heated plastic sheet is stretched between a positive and negative mould.</strong></p>
<p><strong>The positive mould pushes into the negative mould while air is removed to create a vacuum.</strong></p>
<p><strong>The plastic cools, then is removed and trimmed.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Key characteristics</h3>
<p><strong>Produces deeper shapes and sharper detail than vacuum forming.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Logos and textures can be formed more clearly due to pressure from both sides.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Production notes</h3>
<p><strong>Suitable for batch and mass production.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Often finished using die cutting for accuracy.</strong></p></div>
<h2>Compression moulding</h2>
<div class="study-card"><p><strong>Compression moulding uses heat and pressure to shape a pre-measured amount of plastic in a closed mould.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>How the process works</h3>
<p><strong>A warm, pre-measured slug (charge) of plastic is placed into a heated negative mould.</strong></p>
<p><strong>The positive mould clamps down, causing the plastic to spread and fill the cavity.</strong></p>
<p><strong>The plastic cools and the finished component is ejected.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Key characteristics</h3>
<p><strong>Produces strong, dense components with consistent thickness.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Accurate measurement of the charge is essential to avoid flash or incomplete moulding.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Production suitability</h3>
<p><strong>Used for batch and large-scale production.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Often requires trimming after moulding.</strong></p></div>
<h2>Lamination (lay-up moulding – composites)</h2>
<div class="study-card"><p><strong>Lamination is a manual composite process used to produce large or complex shapes, commonly using GRP or CFRP.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>How the process works</h3>
<p><strong>A negative mould is prepared and coated with a release agent such as silicone.</strong></p>
<p><strong>A pigmented gel coat is applied to create the surface finish.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Layers of fibre and resin are built up to the required thickness, with each layer rolled to remove air bubbles.</strong></p>
<p><strong>A sealant layer is applied, the product is left to cure, then removed for post-production.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Advantages</h3>
<p><strong>Low setup cost and simple equipment.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Can produce very large components and complex shapes.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Pigment can be added directly to the gel coat.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Disadvantages</h3>
<p><strong>Time-consuming mould making and curing.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Resin fumes are toxic.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Air bubbles can cause weak points and delamination.</strong></p></div>
<h2>Line bending</h2>
<div class="study-card"><p><strong>Line bending is used to produce accurate straight bends in thermoplastic sheet.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>How the process works</h3>
<p><strong>The plastic sheet is cut to size and the bend line is marked.</strong></p>
<p><strong>The sheet is placed over a heated wire until the plastic becomes soft along the line.</strong></p>
<p><strong>The sheet is removed and bent around a jig or mould to set the angle.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Advantages</h3>
<p><strong>Low cost and minimal equipment.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Suitable for one-off and small batch production.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Disadvantages</h3>
<p><strong>Risk of burning or bubbling if overheated.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Thicker sheets heat unevenly and may need rotating.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Safety risk from exposed heating element.</strong></p></div>
<h2>Injection moulding</h2>
<div class="study-card"><p><strong>Injection moulding is a high-pressure process used to mass-produce complex plastic components.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>How the process works</h3>
<p><strong>Plastic granules are fed into a hopper and moved forward by a rotating screw while heated jackets melt the polymer.</strong></p>
<p><strong>The screw winds back, the mould closes, and a hydraulic ram injects molten plastic into the mould cavity.</strong></p>
<p><strong>The plastic cools, the mould opens, and the sprue is removed.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Key characteristics</h3>
<p><strong>Produces complex, highly detailed components with excellent surface finish.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Very accurate and repeatable, with minimal finishing required.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Advantages</h3>
<p><strong>Ideal for mass production with low unit cost at high volumes.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Allows features such as clips, ribs, and living hinges to be moulded in.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Disadvantages</h3>
<p><strong>Very high tooling and setup costs.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Typical materials</h3>
<p><strong>ABS, HDPE, Nylon, PP, PS</strong></p></div>
<h2>Extrusion</h2>
<div class="study-card"><p><strong>Extrusion is a continuous process that produces products with a constant cross-section.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>How the process works</h3>
<p><strong>Plastic granules are melted and forced through a shaped die.</strong></p>
<p><strong>The plastic exits the die with a fixed cross-section and is cooled before being cut to length.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Key characteristics</h3>
<p><strong>Produces long lengths with identical cross-sections throughout.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Length can be varied without changing the die.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Advantages</h3>
<p><strong>Low unit cost for mass production.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Efficient continuous process.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Disadvantages</h3>
<p><strong>Limited to simple, uniform cross-sections.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Dies are expensive.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Typical materials</h3>
<p><strong>Acrylic, Nylon, Polystyrene, ABS, Polycarbonate</strong></p></div>
<h2>Blow moulding</h2>
<div class="study-card"><p><strong>Blow moulding is used to manufacture hollow plastic products such as bottles and containers.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>How the process works</h3>
<p><strong>A heated hollow tube of plastic (parison) is formed and placed between two halves of a mould.</strong></p>
<p><strong>The mould closes and air is blown in, forcing the plastic against the mould walls.</strong></p>
<p><strong>The plastic cools, and the hollow product is released.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Key characteristics</h3>
<p><strong>Produces hollow shapes with thin, even walls and no joins along the body.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Ideal for airtight and watertight containers.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Advantages</h3>
<p><strong>Efficient for mass production with minimal finishing.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Consistent wall thickness.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Disadvantages</h3>
<p><strong>High tooling and setup costs.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Typical materials</h3>
<p><strong>PVC, PET, Nylon, ABS, HDPE, LDPE, PP, PS</strong></p></div>
<h2>Rotational moulding</h2>
<div class="study-card"><p><strong>Rotational moulding produces seamless hollow products using heat and rotation rather than pressure.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>How the process works</h3>
<p><strong>Polymer powder or granules are placed into a sealed mould.</strong></p>
<p><strong>The mould rotates while being heated, causing the polymer to melt and coat the interior evenly.</strong></p>
<p><strong>The mould continues rotating while cooling, then opens to release the product.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Advantages</h3>
<p><strong>Uniform wall thickness with no internal stresses.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Can produce complex hollow shapes, including double-wall products.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Tooling costs are lower than injection or blow moulding.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Disadvantages</h3>
<p><strong>Long cycle times.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Limited range of suitable materials.</strong></p></div>
<h2>Calendaring</h2>
<div class="study-card"><p><strong>Calendaring is used to produce plastic sheets and films with accurate thickness.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>How the process works</h3>
<p><strong>Plastic is heated to a putty-like state or extruded through a screw.</strong></p>
<p><strong>The material passes through a series of heated rollers with progressively smaller gaps.</strong></p>
<p><strong>The sheet may be embossed, then cooled and wound into rolls.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Advantages</h3>
<p><strong>Produces smooth, uniform sheets and films.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Suitable for continuous mass production with good thickness control.</strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Disadvantages</h3>
<p><strong>High setup cost and large space requirement.</strong></p>
<p><strong>Not economical for short production runs.</strong></p></div>
