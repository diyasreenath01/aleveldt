<section class="note-panel"><h2>Wood Processes</h2><div class="note-intro"><p>These notes use the teacher revision material and focus on the processes you need to recognise and explain: <strong>full sequence, common uses, advantages/disadvantages, quality control and safety</strong>.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Forming wood</h2><div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Kerfing</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Parallel saw cuts are made on the inside of a bend so a board or strip can flex into a curve.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>Mark equally spaced kerf lines.</li><li>Secure the work firmly, using guides/jigs where required.</li><li>Cut each kerf to the same controlled depth without cutting through the board.</li><li>Apply adhesive into the kerfs if the final curve is to be fixed.</li><li>Bend around a jig/former.</li><li>Hold until the shape is stable/adhesive has cured.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> curved furniture/panels and decorative curved forms.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> allows sheet/solid wood to bend without steam equipment.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> cuts weaken the section and may remain visible; inaccurate depth/spacing gives an uneven curve.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> equal spacing and depth, consistent curve, use a jig and calculate spacing/depth before cutting.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> secure work, eye protection, extraction/ventilation, hair tied back and hands clear of the blade.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Steam bending</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Steam softens the wood so it can be bent around a former and retain the curved shape after cooling/drying.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>Select suitable straight-grained timber without major knots/defects and cut to size.</li><li>Place it in the steam box.</li><li>Steam for sufficient time until the timber is pliable and evenly saturated.</li><li>Remove promptly and bend around a jig/former.</li><li>Clamp securely to the required radius.</li><li>Leave to cool and dry before releasing.</li><li>Inspect for cracking, spring-back and dimensional accuracy.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> curved chair backs, furniture parts and walking sticks.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> creates curves from solid timber with no kerf cuts; grain can follow the curve.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> timber selection and steaming time are important; cracking/spring-back can occur.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> even saturation, correct steaming time, accurate former, secure clamping and even drying.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> steam/burn risk, eye protection, ventilation and clamping hazards.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Laminating</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Thin veneers/laminates are glued together and pressed to make a flat or curved component.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>Cut veneers/laminates accurately and reject damaged pieces.</li><li>Prepare the former if a curved component is required.</li><li>Arrange the layers: plywood uses alternating grain directions; curved laminations are arranged to suit the required bend.</li><li>Spread adhesive evenly over the bonding surfaces.</li><li>Place the layers in the former/press and apply even pressure.</li><li>Leave until the adhesive has cured.</li><li>Release, trim the edges and inspect the bond.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> curved furniture, school chairs, skateboards and plywood/laminated components.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> makes strong repeatable curves and allows thin material to form shapes difficult in one solid piece.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> time is needed for gluing/curing; poor pressure or glue coverage can cause delamination/air gaps.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> former accuracy, even adhesive, correct grain orientation, even pressure, no bubbles/dry joints and clean trimmed edges.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> ventilation, eye protection, cutting hazards and finger trapping during clamping/pressing.</p></div></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Machining / wasting</h2><div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Wood turning / lathe</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Timber rotates on a lathe while hand tools remove material to create a round profile.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>Prepare the blank and rough-cut it close to shape where appropriate.</li><li>Secure it to the faceplate/chuck or between headstock and tailstock; check balance.</li><li>Set the tool rest at the correct distance.</li><li>Start at an appropriate speed.</li><li>Use a gouge to rough out the shape.</li><li>Use suitable chisels to refine the profile; use parallel turning/facing-off as required.</li><li>Sand while rotating where appropriate.</li><li>Use a parting tool if required, apply finish and remove the work safely.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> bowls, table/chair legs, handles and banister components.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> efficient for accurate symmetrical rounded forms.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> restricted mainly to rotational shapes and removes material as waste.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> secure/balanced mounting, correct tool-rest position, sharp tools, suitable speed and profile/go-no-go gauges for repeated contours.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> eye protection, extraction/ventilation, hair/clothing secured and clear space around rotating work.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Routing</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> A high-speed rotating cutter forms grooves, slots, joints and edge profiles.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>Mark the route or prepare a template/jig.</li><li>Clamp the work securely.</li><li>Select the correct router bit for the feature.</li><li>Set the fence/routing gate to control distance from the edge.</li><li>Set the depth gauge.</li><li>Run the router at a controlled, consistent feed along the guide.</li><li>Use several passes for deeper cuts.</li><li>Inspect dimensions and edge quality.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> furniture components, housing/lap joints, slots, grooves and decorative edge profiles.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> versatile and repeatable with guides/templates.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> deep cuts may need several passes; incorrect feed/depth can cause tear-out.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> correct cutter width, routing gate/fence, depth gauge, sharp cutter and consistent feed speed.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> secure work, eye protection, extraction, hair/clothing secured and hands clear of cutter.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>CNC routing / milling / laser cutting</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Digital CAD/CAM data controls a machine to cut, mill, route or laser-cut the material accurately.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>Create the drawing accurately in CAD.</li><li>Export in the required format, such as DXF.</li><li>Import into the machine/CAM software and verify scale/toolpath.</li><li>Secure the material to the machine.</li><li>Set/focus the cutter or laser and choose depth, speed and power settings.</li><li>Set the datum/start point.</li><li>Ensure enclosure/extraction is operating.</li><li>Run the program.</li><li>Remove the work only when safe and inspect it.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> repeated profiles, grooves, panels, components and complex flat shapes.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> accurate and repeatable; complex shapes can be reproduced from digital data.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> incorrect CAD, datum or machine settings reproduce errors; setup/machine access is required.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> CAD drawn to scale, toolpath checked, correct datum, cutter/laser focus, speed/depth/power and secure material.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> enclosure closed, extraction on, eye/burn hazards and never look directly at a laser.</p></div></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Mechanical fixing</h2><div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Adding a screw</h3><ol><li>Align and hold the two pieces together.</li><li>Mark the screw position.</li><li>Drill a pilot hole to guide the screw and reduce splitting.</li><li>Drill a clearance hole through the top component where required.</li><li>Countersink if a flush screw head is required.</li><li>Drive the screw in securely without overtightening.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> furniture, carcasses and assemblies that may need dismantling.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> immediate mechanical strength; can usually be removed/replaced.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> visible fixing and risk of splitting/stripping if poorly prepared.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> screw spacing/depth, correct drill-bit sizes, accurate pilot/clearance holes and secure alignment.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> clamp pieces firmly; eye protection for drilling; keep hands clear of rotating tools.</p></div></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Timber preparation</h2><div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Felling</h3><ol><li>Clear loose branches and the fall path.</li><li>Assess/measure the tree and plan the direction of fall.</li><li>Cut the directional notch.</li><li>Make the felling cut from the opposite side.</li><li>Use a wedge where required to control the fall.</li></ol><p><strong>QC:</strong> sharp tools and correct notch/cut angle.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> face/ear protection, sharp-blade precautions and a completely clear fall zone.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Converting</h3><ol><li>Cut logs to manageable/even lengths.</li><li>Remove bark.</li><li>Saw into boards using the required conversion pattern, such as through/slab sawn or quarter sawn.</li></ol><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> sharp tools and accurate depth/alignment of cuts.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> ear/face protection and machine/blade precautions.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Natural seasoning</h3><ol><li>Seal/paint the ends to slow uneven moisture loss.</li><li>Stack boards with spacers so air can circulate.</li><li>Keep the stack covered and stable.</li><li>Leave to dry naturally; this can take years.</li></ol><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> simple and low-energy.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> very slow and less controlled; greater risk of insects/fungus, splitting and warping.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> stable stack, airflow and monitoring defects/moisture.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Kiln seasoning</h3><ol><li>Stack boards on a trolley with spacers for airflow.</li><li>Move into the kiln and seal it.</li><li>Use steam/humidity initially to equalise moisture and control drying.</li><li>Reduce humidity and increase heat gradually.</li><li>Continue controlled drying.</li><li>Introduce drier air until the required moisture content is reached.</li></ol><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> much faster and more controlled than natural seasoning.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> uses specialist equipment/energy; poor control can still cause defects.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> kiln seal, airflow, humidity/temperature and final moisture content.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> burns and emergency-stop procedures.</p></div></div></section>
