<section class="note-panel"><h2>Wood Processes</h2><div class="note-intro"><p>These notes follow the teacher revision material, with each process kept to a clear sequence while retaining the important detail.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Forming wood</h2><div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Kerfing</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Parallel saw cuts are made on the inside of a piece of wood so it can bend into a curve.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>Mark equally spaced lines where the kerf cuts are required and use a jig/guide so the spacing and depth remain consistent.</li><li>Secure the wood firmly and cut along each line with a tenon saw, making every cut the same depth without cutting all the way through.</li><li>Check that the remaining material is thin enough to flex but still strong enough to hold together.</li><li>Apply adhesive into the kerfs where the curve needs to become permanent, then carefully bend the wood around the required jig/former.</li><li>Hold or clamp the work against the jig until the adhesive has cured and the required curve is stable.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> curved furniture, panels and decorative curved forms.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> allows wood to bend without steam equipment; useful for controlled curves.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> kerf cuts weaken the section and may remain visible; inaccurate spacing or depth creates an uneven curve.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> use a jig to control cut depth and the final curve; keep kerf spacing/depth even and check the curve against the required shape.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> secure the work, wear eye protection, use extraction/ventilation, tie back hair and keep fingers clear of the saw.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Steam bending</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Steam softens the wood fibres so solid timber can be bent around a former and retain the curved shape after cooling and drying.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>Select suitable straight-grained timber, cut it to the required size and avoid pieces with major knots or defects that may split during bending.</li><li>Place the timber inside a steam box and steam it until the wood is hot, saturated and flexible enough to bend.</li><li>Remove the timber promptly from the steam box and position it against the prepared jig/former before it begins to cool.</li><li>Bend the softened timber around the former and clamp it securely so it follows the required radius.</li><li>Leave the timber clamped until it has cooled and dried, then release it and check for spring-back, splitting and dimensional accuracy.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> curved chair parts, furniture components and walking sticks.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> creates smooth curves from solid timber without removing material; the fibres remain continuous through the bend.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> time consuming; suitable timber must be selected; splitting and spring-back can occur.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> use an accurate jig/former, make sure the timber is evenly steamed and keep it clamped for sufficient time to retain the required shape.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> steam and hot timber can cause burns; wear eye protection and heat-resistant gloves and take care when clamping.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Laminating</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Thin veneers or strips of wood are bonded together under pressure to create strong flat or curved components.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>Cut the veneers/laminates accurately and prepare an appropriate former when a curved component is required.</li><li>Arrange the layers correctly: plywood uses alternating grain directions, while veneers for a curved lamination are arranged to follow the required curve.</li><li>Apply an even layer of adhesive across the bonding surfaces so there are no dry areas.</li><li>Place the layers into the former/press and apply even pressure across the whole component using clamps or a press.</li><li>Leave the adhesive to cure fully, then release the component, trim the edges and inspect for bubbles, gaps or delamination.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> curved furniture, school chairs, skateboards, plywood and laminated components.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> produces strong, repeatable curved forms; thin material can be formed into shapes difficult to produce from one solid piece.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> time is required for gluing and curing; poor adhesive coverage or pressure can cause air gaps and delamination.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> check the former is accurate, adhesive coverage is even, grain direction is correct and pressure is distributed evenly; inspect for bubbles and dry joints.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> use ventilation and eye protection when working with adhesives; take care with cutting tools and avoid finger trapping during clamping/pressing.</p></div></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Machining / wasting</h2><div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Wood turning / lathe</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Timber is rotated on a lathe while cutting tools remove material to produce cylindrical or symmetrical forms.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>Prepare the timber blank, rough-shape it if required and secure it correctly between centres, on a faceplate or in a chuck.</li><li>Position the tool rest close to the work at the correct height, check that the blank rotates freely and select a suitable lathe speed.</li><li>Start the lathe and use a gouge to remove the main waste, gradually bringing the work to a round and balanced form.</li><li>Use appropriate chisels to refine the profile and create the required details, checking the shape and dimensions as work progresses.</li><li>Sand the rotating component where appropriate, use a parting tool if required, apply the finish and remove the work safely when the lathe has stopped.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> bowls, table and chair legs, handles, spindles and banister components.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> efficient for producing smooth, accurate and symmetrical rounded forms.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> mainly limited to rotational forms; requires skill and removes material as waste.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> secure and balance the work correctly, use sharp tools and the correct tool-rest position/speed; use measurements or a go/no-go contour gauge for repeated profiles.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> wear eye protection, use extraction/ventilation, secure hair and loose clothing and keep clear of the rotating work.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Routing</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> A high-speed rotating cutter removes wood to form grooves, rebates, slots, joints and decorative edge profiles.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>Mark the required cut or prepare the jig/template, then clamp the work securely so it cannot move.</li><li>Select the correct router bit for the required groove or profile and fit it securely in the router.</li><li>Set the routing gate/fence to control the cutter position and use the depth gauge to set the required depth of cut.</li><li>Run the router along the guide at a controlled and consistent speed, keeping the base stable against the work.</li><li>For deep cuts, make several shallower passes rather than removing the full depth at once, then inspect the dimensions and edge quality.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> furniture components, housing/lap joints, slots, grooves and decorative edge profiles.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> versatile and repeatable when used with guides, fences or templates.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> deep cuts may require several passes; incorrect feed speed or depth can cause tear-out.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> use the correct cutter width, fence/routing gate and depth setting; keep the cutter sharp and maintain a consistent feed speed.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> secure the work, wear eye protection, use extraction, secure hair/clothing and keep hands clear of the cutter.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>CNC routing / milling / laser cutting</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> CAD/CAM data controls a CNC router, milling machine or laser to manufacture components accurately and repeatedly.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>Create the design accurately in CAD, export it in the required format such as DXF and import it into the relevant CAM/machine software.</li><li>Check that the drawing is at the correct scale and that the toolpath/cutting lines are correct before machining.</li><li>Secure the material firmly to the machine bed and fit/focus the correct cutter or laser for the material and operation.</li><li>Set the required depth, speed and power settings, establish the datum/start point and make sure the enclosure and extraction are operating.</li><li>Run the program, allow the machine to complete the operation, then remove the work only when safe and inspect its dimensions and finish.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> repeated profiles, grooves, panels, joints, components and complex flat shapes.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> high accuracy and repeatability; complex shapes can be reproduced directly from digital data.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> incorrect CAD, datum or machine settings reproduce the same error; equipment and programming are required.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> check CAD scale, toolpath, datum, cutter/laser focus, depth/speed/power settings and that the material is securely fixed.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> keep the enclosure closed and extraction on; follow machine procedures and never look directly at the laser.</p></div></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Mechanical fixing</h2><div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Adding a screw</h3><ol><li>Align the two components accurately, hold them together and mark the required screw position.</li><li>Drill a pilot hole to guide the screw and reduce the chance of the timber splitting.</li><li>Drill a clearance hole through the top component where required so the screw can pull the two pieces tightly together.</li><li>Countersink the hole when a flush screw head is required.</li><li>Drive the screw into position securely without overtightening or damaging the material.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> furniture, carcasses and assemblies that may need dismantling.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> provides immediate mechanical strength and can normally be removed and replaced.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> the fixing may remain visible and poorly prepared holes can cause splitting or stripping.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> check screw position/depth, use the correct drill-bit sizes and keep pilot, clearance and countersunk holes accurately aligned.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> clamp pieces firmly, wear eye protection when drilling and keep hands clear of rotating tools.</p></div></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Timber preparation</h2><div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Felling</h3><ol><li>Clear loose branches and the fall path, assess the tree and plan the direction in which it should fall.</li><li>Cut a directional notch on the side facing the planned direction of fall.</li><li>Make the felling cut from the opposite side, leaving enough wood to act as a hinge.</li><li>Use a wedge where required to help control the direction of the fall.</li><li>Allow the tree to fall into the completely clear fall zone before further processing.</li></ol><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> use sharp tools and ensure the notch and felling cuts are made at the correct position and angle.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> face and ear protection, sharp-blade precautions and a completely clear fall zone are essential.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Converting</h3><ol><li>Cut the felled logs into manageable and even lengths ready for conversion.</li><li>Remove the bark so the log can be processed cleanly and accurately.</li><li>Position and secure the log correctly for sawing.</li><li>Saw the log into boards using the required conversion method, such as through/slab sawing or quarter sawing.</li><li>Inspect the boards for cut accuracy and defects before seasoning.</li></ol><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> use sharp tools and maintain accurate alignment and depth of cut.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> use ear/face protection and follow machine and blade precautions.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Natural seasoning</h3><ol><li>Seal or paint the board ends to slow rapid moisture loss from the end grain and reduce splitting.</li><li>Stack the boards horizontally with spacers between each layer so air can circulate around them.</li><li>Keep the stack stable, raised and covered so it is protected from direct rain while remaining ventilated.</li><li>Leave the timber to dry naturally over a long period, allowing moisture to escape gradually.</li><li>Monitor the boards for warping, splitting, fungal/insect attack and moisture content before use.</li></ol><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> simple and uses little external energy.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> very slow and less controlled; there is greater exposure to insects, fungus, splitting and warping.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> maintain a stable stack and good airflow and monitor defects and moisture content throughout drying.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Kiln seasoning</h3><ol><li>Stack the boards on a trolley with spacers between them so heated air can circulate evenly around every board.</li><li>Move the timber into the kiln, seal it and initially use controlled steam/humidity to equalise moisture and prevent the surface drying too quickly.</li><li>Gradually increase the temperature and reduce the humidity so moisture is removed from inside the timber at a controlled rate.</li><li>Continue circulating progressively drier air through the stack until the timber approaches the required moisture content.</li><li>Check the final moisture content and allow the boards to stabilise before removing them for use.</li></ol><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> much faster and more controlled than natural seasoning.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> requires specialist equipment and energy; incorrect temperature or humidity control can still cause defects.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> monitor kiln seal, airflow, humidity, temperature and final moisture content.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> burns from hot equipment and steam; follow kiln operating and emergency procedures.</p></div></div></section>
