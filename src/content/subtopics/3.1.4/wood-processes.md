<section class="note-panel"><h2>Wood processes</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>Wood can be shaped by <strong>forming</strong>, <strong>machining/wasting</strong> and <strong>addition/fabrication</strong>. Process choice depends on the timber or board, required form, accuracy, finish and scale of production.</p></div></section>

<section class="note-panel"><h2>Forming processes</h2>
<div class="note-grid">
<div class="study-card"><h3>Steam bending</h3><p>Heat and moisture soften lignin so solid timber can be bent around a former and clamped until dry.</p><ul><li><strong>Suitable materials:</strong> straight-grained hardwoods such as ash and oak.</li><li><strong>Why use it:</strong> produces strong, smooth curves because the fibres remain continuous and little material is wasted.</li><li><strong>Limitations:</strong> restricted to suitable timbers; splitting and spring-back can occur.</li><li><strong>Typical uses:</strong> curved furniture components such as chair parts.</li></ul></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Laminating</h3><p>Thin veneers or strips are coated with adhesive, bent around a former and clamped or vacuum pressed until cured.</p><ul><li><strong>Why use it:</strong> creates strong, stable and repeatable curved components.</li><li><strong>Limitations:</strong> needs accurate formers and curing time; poor bonding can cause delamination.</li><li><strong>Typical uses:</strong> curved furniture and structural components.</li></ul></div>
</div></section>

<section class="note-panel"><h2>Machining and wasting</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>Machining removes material to create the required shape, dimensions or surface detail.</p></div>
<div class="note-grid">
<div class="study-card"><h3>Turning</h3><p>The timber rotates on a lathe while cutting tools remove material.</p><ul><li><strong>Between centres:</strong> used for spindle forms such as chair and table legs.</li><li><strong>Faceplate/chuck:</strong> used for bowls and other rotational forms.</li><li><strong>Strength:</strong> quick and capable of a smooth, symmetrical finish.</li><li><strong>Limitation:</strong> mainly restricted to rotational shapes and requires secure workholding.</li></ul></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Routing</h3><p>A high-speed rotating cutter removes material to produce grooves, rebates, joints and edge profiles.</p><ul><li><strong>Why use it:</strong> accurate and repeatable, especially with a jig or template.</li><li><strong>Limitation:</strong> produces dust and noise; poor feed direction or setup can cause tear-out.</li></ul></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Milling</h3><p>A rotating cutter removes material while the work is controlled relative to the cutter. It can produce slots, channels and accurate profiles.</p><ul><li><strong>Why use it:</strong> useful where controlled material removal and dimensional accuracy are required.</li><li><strong>Limitation:</strong> manual milling is slower and less repeatable than CNC for complex batch work.</li></ul></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>CNC routing / milling</h3><p>CAD geometry is converted into CAM toolpaths that control the cutter automatically.</p><ul><li><strong>Why use it:</strong> high accuracy and repeatability; complex shapes and repeated components can be produced efficiently.</li><li><strong>Limitations:</strong> high equipment cost and programming/setup are required; an incorrect file can repeat the same error across a batch.</li><li><strong>Typical uses:</strong> panel components, joints, profiles and decorative features.</li></ul></div>
</div></section>

<section class="note-panel"><h2>Traditional wood joints</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>Traditional joints shape the timber itself to create mechanical strength and a large bonding area. The joint should suit the direction of loading, appearance and production method.</p></div>
<div class="note-grid">
<div class="study-card"><h3>Common joints</h3><ul><li><strong>Mortise and tenon:</strong> strong frame joint used in tables and chairs.</li><li><strong>Dovetail:</strong> resists pulling apart; commonly used in drawers and boxes.</li><li><strong>Finger / comb joint:</strong> interlocking fingers create a large glue area for box construction.</li><li><strong>Housing joint:</strong> a slot supports another component; useful for shelves and cabinets.</li><li><strong>Half-lap:</strong> simple frame joint where material is removed from both pieces so they overlap.</li><li><strong>Dowel joint:</strong> accurately drilled dowels align and reinforce components while keeping the joint visually neat.</li></ul></div>
</div></section>

<section class="note-panel"><h2>Knock-down (KD) fittings</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>KD fittings are removable mechanical fixings widely used in flat-pack furniture. They allow products to be transported efficiently and assembled or dismantled using simple tools.</p></div>
<div class="note-grid">
<div class="study-card"><h3>Cam-lock connector</h3><p>A metal dowel screws into one panel and engages with a circular cam in the other. Turning the cam pulls the panels tightly together.</p><ul><li><strong>Advantages:</strong> quick assembly, mostly hidden and suitable for mass-produced panel furniture.</li><li><strong>Limitations:</strong> requires accurate drilling and can loosen after repeated assembly.</li></ul></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Barrel nut and bolt</h3><p>A cylindrical barrel nut sits in a cross-drilled hole. A bolt passes through the other component and screws into the barrel nut, pulling the components together.</p><ul><li><strong>Advantages:</strong> strong, removable joint; suitable for thicker components and repeated dismantling.</li><li><strong>Limitations:</strong> accurate drilling is essential; the fixing may be visible and can loosen over time.</li><li><strong>Typical use:</strong> bed frames and other demountable furniture.</li></ul></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Modesty block</h3><p>A small moulded polymer block is screwed into two panels at a corner.</p><ul><li><strong>Advantages:</strong> inexpensive, simple to fit and useful for cabinets and storage units.</li><li><strong>Limitations:</strong> visible inside the product and provides less structural strength than heavier-duty fittings.</li></ul></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Wood screws</h3><p>A clearance hole in the upper component allows the screw to pull it tightly against the lower component; a smaller pilot hole improves grip and reduces splitting.</p><ul><li><strong>Advantages:</strong> immediate mechanical strength and easy disassembly.</li><li><strong>Limitations:</strong> fixings may be visible and poorly prepared holes can split timber or damage boards.</li></ul></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Coach bolts</h3><p>A coach bolt has a domed head with a square section beneath it that bites into timber and prevents the bolt rotating while the nut is tightened.</p><ul><li><strong>Typical uses:</strong> robust timber assemblies such as outdoor and street furniture.</li><li><strong>Limitation:</strong> more visually prominent than concealed furniture fittings.</li></ul></div>
</div></section>

<section class="note-panel"><h2>Choosing a wood process</h2>
<div class="note-grid">
<div class="study-card"><h3>What matters</h3><ul><li><strong>Material:</strong> solid timber behaves differently from manufactured board.</li><li><strong>Form:</strong> steam bending or laminating suits curves; turning suits rotational forms; routing suits grooves and profiles.</li><li><strong>Production quantity:</strong> jigs and CNC become more valuable when components are repeated.</li><li><strong>Accuracy and finish:</strong> the process must achieve the required dimensions without unnecessary finishing.</li><li><strong>Assembly and repair:</strong> permanent joints suit products that stay assembled; KD fittings suit flat-pack, transport and repeated dismantling.</li></ul></div>
</div></section>
