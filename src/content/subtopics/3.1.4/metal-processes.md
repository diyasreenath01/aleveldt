<section class="note-panel"><h2>Metal Processes</h2><div class="note-intro"><p>The teacher material groups metal processing into <strong>forming, redistribution, addition/fabrication and wastage</strong>. The sequences below keep the important detail from the slides without splitting processes into unnecessary micro-steps.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Forming processes</h2><div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Press forming</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Sheet metal is forced into a die by a punch to produce a 3D form.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>Cut the sheet metal to the required dimensions and position it accurately over the lower die.</li><li>Align the punch with the die and hold the sheet securely so it cannot move during forming.</li><li>Use a mechanical or hydraulic press to apply high compressive force, pushing the sheet into the die cavity.</li><li>Withdraw the punch once the shape has formed and remove/eject the component from the die.</li><li>Trim excess material, finish the edges and check dimensions, cracking and surface finish.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> car body panels, boxes and containers.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> accurate, fast and repeatable once tooling is set up; suitable for mass production.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> high tooling cost; excessive forming can thin, tear or distort the sheet.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> punch/die alignment, draw force, dimensions, surface finish and signs of cracking/thinning.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> crushing/trapping at the press and sharp sheet edges; guards are essential.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Cupping and deep drawing</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> A sheet-metal blank is drawn deeply into a die to produce hollow cup or tube-like forms.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>Die-cut the sheet into a blank and clamp it over the deep-drawing die using a pressure pad/retainer.</li><li>A hydraulic press moves the punch into the blank while the retainer controls the sheet and reduces wrinkling.</li><li>The punch pushes the blank into the die cavity to form an initial cup shape.</li><li>The cup is drawn further through another die/stage where required to produce the deeper tubular form.</li><li>Remove and trim the component, then inspect wall thickness, wrinkles, tearing and depth.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> drinks/aerosol cans, fire extinguishers and deep containers.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> produces deep seamless hollow forms and is repeatable in mass production.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> high setup cost; often requires several operations; poor pressure control causes wrinkling or tearing.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> pressure-pad force, punch/die alignment, draw depth and consistent wall thickness.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> crushing/trapping at the hydraulic press and sharp edges.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Spinning</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> A circular sheet blank rotates while a roller progressively forms it over a mandrel.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>Mount the mandrel in the chuck and hold the metal blank securely between the mandrel and tailstock.</li><li>Rotate the blank and mandrel together at a controlled speed.</li><li>Bring the roller tool into contact so pressure begins stretching the metal over the mandrel.</li><li>Move the roller progressively along the mandrel while maintaining pressure until the blank follows its profile.</li><li>Remove the component, trim excess material and check symmetry, finish and wall thickness.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> saucepans, kettle bodies and light shades.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> smooth seamless rotational forms; can be automated.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> limited to radial symmetry; wall thickness must be controlled.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> secure blank/mandrel, correct speed and roller pressure, symmetry and thickness.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> entanglement and contact with rotating work.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Bending</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Sheet metal is cold formed into a straight bend using a punch and die, commonly in a press brake.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>Mark the required bend line and position the sheet accurately over the die.</li><li>Secure the sheet so the bend line remains aligned with the punch and die.</li><li>Lower the punch so pressure forces the sheet around/into the die to create the bend.</li><li>Raise the punch and remove the formed work from the machine.</li><li>Check the bend angle and dimensions, then trim or finish where required.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> brackets, frames, casings and housings.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> forms accurate bends without removing material.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> spring-back may affect accuracy and excessive bending can crack the metal.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> bend-line position, punch/die alignment and final angle.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> trapping/crushing and sharp sheet edges.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Rolling</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Metal passes between rotating rollers to reduce thickness and increase length.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>For hot rolling, heat the billet above its recrystallisation temperature; cold rolling is carried out below this temperature.</li><li>Feed the billet or sheet between hardened rotating rollers which apply compressive force.</li><li>Reduce the roller gap progressively so the metal becomes thinner and longer.</li><li>Repeat passes as required; hot rolling allows recrystallisation while cold rolling increases strength and hardness through work hardening.</li><li>Remove the rolled material and check thickness, dimensions and surface finish.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> railway/structural sections, sheet and foil; cold-rolled products include containers, drums and saucepans.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> efficient and consistent production of sheet/sections.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> large machinery; poor control creates thickness variation and cold working reduces ductility.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> roller gap, temperature where applicable, thickness and surface consistency.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> entanglement/crushing at rollers and burns during hot rolling.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Drop forging</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> A heated billet is forced between shaped dies to produce a tough component.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>Secure the cast tool-steel lower die on the anvil and matching upper die to the hydraulic ram.</li><li>Heat the billet above its recrystallisation temperature so it can deform without excessive work hardening.</li><li>Use tongs to place the heated billet accurately into the lower die.</li><li>Apply the hydraulic ram so the upper die forces the billet into the die cavity.</li><li>Lift the ram, remove the forging, cool it and trim/finish excess material before inspection.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> spanners, pliers and other tough tools.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> very strong components; suitable for high-volume production.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> expensive dies and machinery.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> billet temperature, die alignment/fill and final dimensions.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> hot metal, heavy dies and crushing hazards.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Wrought-iron forging</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Heated wrought iron is repeatedly hammered over an anvil to create decorative or bespoke forms.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>Heat the iron until it reaches a cherry-red colour and becomes malleable.</li><li>Place the hot metal securely on an anvil or suitable former.</li><li>Hammer the metal repeatedly, manually or with powered equipment, to develop the required form.</li><li>Reheat when necessary so the metal remains workable while shaping continues.</li><li>Allow the completed work to cool before further heat treatment or finishing.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> gates, fencing and decorative metalwork.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> flexible for individual decorative shapes and bespoke work.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> labour/skill intensive and slower than industrial forming.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> heating and comparison against jigs/templates.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> burns, sparks and hammering hazards.</p></div></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Redistribution / casting</h2><div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Sand casting</h3><ol><li>Make a pattern slightly larger than the finished component and place it inside the two-part flask.</li><li>Pack prepared sand tightly around the pattern, separate the flask and remove the pattern to leave the cavity.</li><li>Create the runner, riser and vents so metal can enter and displaced gases can escape.</li><li>Reassemble the mould, pour molten metal through the runner and allow it to cool and solidify.</li><li>Break/shake away the sand, cut off the runner/riser and clean and finish the casting.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> engine parts, post boxes and larger cast components.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> suitable for large shapes and low quantities; cheaper tooling than permanent dies.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> mould is destroyed; rougher finish and lower accuracy than die casting.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> pattern accuracy, packed sand, runners/risers/vents, controlled pour and porosity.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> severe molten-metal burn risk; PPE and ventilation.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Gravity die casting</h3><ol><li>Preheat the permanent metal die and apply a coating to improve flow and release.</li><li>Melt the required metal and transfer it safely in a ladle.</li><li>Pour the molten metal slowly into the die cavity so it fills under gravity.</li><li>Leave the metal until it has cooled and solidified sufficiently.</li><li>Open the die, remove the casting and machine/trim excess material before inspection.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> door knobs and automotive components.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> reusable mould and more consistent finish than sand casting.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> higher die cost; mainly suitable for appropriate lower-melting alloys.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> die temperature/coating, pour, fill, dimensions and finish.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> molten-metal and hot-die burns.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Pressure die casting — hot chamber</h3><ol><li>Maintain low-melting-point metal in the integral furnace with the gooseneck submerged in the molten metal.</li><li>Close and clamp the reusable steel die securely.</li><li>Use the hydraulic plunger to force molten metal through the gooseneck into the die at high pressure.</li><li>Hold it while the metal cools and solidifies rapidly in the cavity.</li><li>Open the die and use ejector pins to remove the component before trimming and inspection.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> toy cars, door handles and zinc components.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> very fast, accurate and repeatable.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> expensive tooling and unsuitable for high-melting metals.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> temperature, injection pressure, complete filling, porosity and dimensions.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> high-pressure molten metal and crushing hazards.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Pressure die casting — cold chamber</h3><ol><li>Melt the higher-melting-point metal in a separate furnace.</li><li>Measure and ladle the required amount of molten metal into the cold shot chamber.</li><li>Close and clamp the steel die securely.</li><li>A hydraulic plunger forces the molten metal from the chamber into the die at high pressure.</li><li>Allow it to solidify, then open the die, eject the component and inspect/finish it.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> aluminium components and repeated engineering castings.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> accurate high-volume casting of metals unsuitable for hot chamber.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> slower because metal is transferred from a separate furnace; expensive tooling.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> measured charge, temperature, pressure, fill, porosity and dimensions.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> molten-metal transfer and high-pressure machinery.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Investment casting</h3><ol><li>Produce an accurate wax replica and attach it to a wax sprue/tree.</li><li>Coat the wax assembly in ceramic/clay slurry and stucco until a strong shell forms.</li><li>Fire the shell in a kiln so it hardens and the wax melts out, leaving the cavity.</li><li>Pour molten metal into the ceramic mould and allow it to cool and solidify.</li><li>Break away the shell, clean the casting and machine off the runner/sprue.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> jewellery, hip-joint replacements and precision components.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> excellent detail, accuracy and surface finish.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> several stages make it slower and more costly.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> wax accuracy, shell integrity, complete filling and dimensions.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> hot kiln, molten metal and ceramic dust.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Low-temperature pewter casting</h3><ol><li>Make the mould from MDF with the required cavity and pouring channel.</li><li>Sandwich the mould between two MDF pieces and clamp it securely.</li><li>Melt the pewter in a ladle and carefully pour it into the cavity.</li><li>Allow it to cool before opening the mould and removing the casting.</li><li>Remove the sprue with a junior hacksaw, then file and finish with wet-and-dry paper.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> jewellery, keychains and small decorative products.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> simple workshop casting because pewter has a low melting temperature.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> limited to small suitable products; poor pouring causes incomplete casts.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> secure mould, complete filling, surface and finishing.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> molten pewter can cause serious burns.</p></div></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Addition / fabrication</h2><div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Arc welding</h3><ol><li>Clean, position and clamp the work, attach the earth clamp and secure the coated filler electrode in the holder.</li><li>Set the welding current to suit the material and electrode.</li><li>Strike the arc between the electrode and work to create the heat needed to melt the metal.</li><li>Maintain the arc and move along the joint so a continuous molten weld pool and bead are produced.</li><li>Allow the weld to cool, remove slag/flux with a wire brush and inspect the joint.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> rough/general steel fabrication.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> strong, portable and suitable for general fabrication.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> slag/spatter and rougher finish.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> current, stable arc, bead, penetration, cracks and porosity.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> welding mask, leather PPE and ventilation.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>MIG welding</h3><ol><li>Clean, align and clamp the components securely.</li><li>Set the wire feed, electrical settings and inert shielding-gas flow.</li><li>Operate the torch so the thin consumable wire is fed continuously towards the joint.</li><li>An electric arc forms between the wire and workpiece, melting both to create the weld pool.</li><li>Move steadily along the joint to form an even weld bead, then cool and inspect it.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> bike frames, vehicle exhausts and general fabrication.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> fast with automatic wire feed; suitable for thicker materials.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> rougher/more spatter than TIG; shielding gas required.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> wire feed, gas flow, travel speed, bead consistency and porosity.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> welding mask, gloves/leather PPE and ventilation.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>TIG welding</h3><ol><li>Clean, align and clamp the work accurately.</li><li>Fit the tungsten electrode and set the argon shielding-gas flow.</li><li>Strike an arc between the non-consumable tungsten electrode and workpiece so the parent metal melts.</li><li>Feed a separate filler rod manually into the molten pool when required.</li><li>Move the torch/filler consistently along the joint, then cool and inspect the precise weld.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> pool ladders, car exhausts, stainless steel and precision work.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> clean, precise, high-quality weld with little spatter.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> slower and more skill dependent than MIG.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> clean surfaces, gas flow, arc/filler control and even bead.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> welding mask, leather PPE and ventilation.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Spot welding</h3><ol><li>Clean and overlap the two thin metal sheets at the joint.</li><li>Place the joint between the copper electrodes.</li><li>Close the electrodes so they clamp the sheets under pressure.</li><li>Pass a high current through the sheets; electrical resistance produces heat and forms a molten weld nugget.</li><li>Stop the current but maintain pressure while the nugget solidifies, then release and inspect it.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> mild-steel sheet, car bodies and mass-produced assemblies.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> fast and repeatable with no filler or shielding gas.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> mainly for overlapping thin sheet and needs access from both sides.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> electrode position/pressure, current/time and nugget consistency.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> crushing, electrical hazards, hot metal and sparks.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Oxy-acetylene welding</h3><ol><li>Clean, align and secure the metal components.</li><li>Mix oxygen and acetylene at the torch, ignite it and adjust to the required neutral flame.</li><li>Heat the edges of the parent metal until a molten weld pool forms.</li><li>Dip a filler rod of the same/suitable metal into the pool and move the torch and rod along the joint.</li><li>Complete the weld, allow it to cool and inspect the finished joint.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> mild-steel sheets and plates.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> portable and does not require an electrical welding supply at the joint.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> slower than MIG and can cause more heat distortion.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> correct flame, clean joint, weld pool/filler and distortion.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> pressurised gas cylinders, open flame, hot metal and fumes.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Soft soldering</h3><ol><li>Clean the metal surfaces and position the components accurately together.</li><li>Apply flux to prevent oxidation and help the solder flow.</li><li>Heat the joint using a soldering iron or suitable heat source.</li><li>Apply the low-melting-point solder so it melts and flows into the joint by capillary action.</li><li>Remove the heat, allow the solder to solidify and inspect/clean the joint.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> electronics and light metal/jewellery work.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> low heat and simple equipment; parent metals do not melt.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> weaker than welding/brazing and may leave a visible joint.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> clean surfaces, flux, sufficient heating and complete solder flow.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> hot iron, molten solder and flux fumes.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Brazing / hard soldering</h3><ol><li>Clean the surfaces with emery cloth and align the metals closely at the joint.</li><li>Apply flux to prevent oxidation and help the filler flow.</li><li>Heat the surrounding parent metals evenly with a flame.</li><li>Apply the higher-melting-point spelter so it melts and flows through the joint by capillary action.</li><li>Allow the joint to cool and solidify, then clean away remaining flux and inspect it.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> pipework and joining similar or dissimilar metals.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> stronger than soft soldering and can join different metals without melting them.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> higher temperature and careful surface preparation/close fit required.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> clean surfaces, joint gap, even heat and complete spelter flow.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> flame, hot metal and flux fumes.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Pop riveting</h3><ol><li>Overlap and clamp the components, then mark the rivet position.</li><li>Drill a hole through both pieces to the correct diameter.</li><li>Insert the pop rivet and place its mandrel into the rivet gun.</li><li>Squeeze the gun so the mandrel pulls and expands the rivet behind the material, clamping the sheets together.</li><li>Continue until the mandrel snaps, then check that the joint is tight.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> sheet-metal products and one-sided-access assemblies.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> quick permanent fixing with access needed from only one side.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> needs drilling and is not intended for easy dismantling.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> correct hole/rivet diameter, alignment and fully set rivet.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> eye protection and care with sharp sheet edges/broken mandrels.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Snap-and-set riveting</h3><ol><li>Align and clamp the components and drill matching holes.</li><li>Insert a solid rivet of the correct diameter and length.</li><li>Support the manufactured rivet head firmly on the snap/anvil.</li><li>Use the setting tool/hammer to deform the projecting end and form the second head.</li><li>Check both heads and make sure the joint is tight without damaging the material.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> permanent metal joints and traditional sheet-metal fabrication.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> strong mechanical joint without heat.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> needs access from both sides and is not easily dismantled.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> hole alignment, rivet size and properly formed heads.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> eye protection and care with drilling, hammering and sharp edges.</p></div></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Wastage processes</h2><div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Milling</h3><ol><li>Clamp the metal securely to the milling-machine table, normally using a vice.</li><li>Fit the correct multi-tooth milling cutter into the spindle.</li><li>Set the required spindle speed and depth of cut.</li><li>Feed the work/cutter so the rotating teeth progressively remove material and create the required form.</li><li>Stop the machine, measure the component, deburr and complete final finishing.</li></ol><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> accurate and versatile.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> creates waste and needs correct setup/tool selection.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> secure work, cutter, speed/depth and dimensional measurement.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> guards, eye protection and hands/clothing clear of cutter and swarf.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Turning</h3><ol><li>Secure the metal bar accurately in the lathe chuck/between centres.</li><li>Fit and position the single-point cutting tool at the correct height.</li><li>Select a suitable spindle speed and rotate the workpiece.</li><li>Move the cutting tool along or into the rotating metal to remove material and produce the required profile.</li><li>Stop the lathe, measure, deburr/finish and remove the component safely.</li></ol><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> accurate production of rotational forms.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> mainly rotational forms and creates swarf.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> secure work, tool position, speed/feed and measurements.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> entanglement, sharp swarf and cutting tools.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Flame cutting</h3><ol><li>Clean and secure the low-carbon steel plate and mark the cutting line.</li><li>Mix oxygen and acetylene, ignite the torch and adjust the flame.</li><li>Heat the start of the cut until the steel reaches the required temperature.</li><li>Apply the additional oxygen jet and move steadily so molten/oxidised metal is blown from the cut.</li><li>Allow the work to cool, remove slag and finish/check the edges.</li></ol><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> useful for thick steel and does not require electrical cutting equipment.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> less accurate than plasma/laser and gives a larger heat-affected edge.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> torch speed/distance, flame/oxygen and cut straightness.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> pressurised gases, flame, hot metal and sparks.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Plasma cutting</h3><ol><li>Clean, mark and secure the conductive metal.</li><li>Set the plasma cutter's current and gas settings for the material/thickness.</li><li>Generate the high-temperature ionised plasma jet and position it at the start of the cut.</li><li>Move the torch steadily so the plasma melts the metal and the gas blows it from the kerf.</li><li>Allow the component to cool, remove slag and inspect/finish the edges.</li></ol><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> fast, clean and more accurate than flame cutting.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> only works with conductive materials and needs specialist equipment.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> settings, torch speed/distance, dimensions and edge finish.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> intense heat/light, sparks, fumes and electrical hazards.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Laser cutting</h3><ol><li>Clean the metal and position it flat and securely on the cutting bed.</li><li>Load/check the digital cutting path and set the laser focus.</li><li>Select suitable power and speed for the material and thickness.</li><li>Run the machine so the focused laser follows the programmed path and melts the metal along the cut line.</li><li>Remove the component safely, clean/deburr it and inspect dimensions and edge quality.</li></ol><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> very high accuracy and complex CAD/CAM shapes.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> expensive and less suitable for very thick material than some alternatives.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> path, focus, power/speed, kerf, dimensions and edge quality.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> enclosure, extraction and laser safety procedures; never view the beam directly.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Punching / stamping</h3><ol><li>Clean and position the thin sheet metal accurately on the die.</li><li>Program/set the CNC press for the required shape and fit the correct punch/die.</li><li>Locate the sheet accurately so the cutting position is correct.</li><li>The punch moves down at high force and shears through the sheet into the die.</li><li>Remove the slug/waste and finished component, then inspect and deburr where necessary.</li></ol><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> very fast and accurate once tooling is set up.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> dedicated tooling/setup cost and mainly suited to sheet metal.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> sheet position, punch/die alignment, dimensions and burr quality.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> severe crushing/shearing hazard; guards are essential.</p></div></div></section>
