<section class="note-panel"><h2>Metal Processes</h2><div class="note-intro"><p>Metal processes can be grouped into <strong>forming, redistribution, addition/fabrication and wastage</strong>. The sequences below explain the essential stages, product suitability, production scale and quality considerations.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Forming processes</h2><div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Press forming</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Sheet metal is forced into a die by a punch to produce a 3D form.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>Cut the sheet metal to the required dimensions and position it accurately over the lower die.</li><li>Align the punch with the die and hold the sheet securely so it cannot move during forming.</li><li>Use a mechanical or hydraulic press to apply high compressive force, pushing the sheet into the die cavity.</li><li>Withdraw the punch once the shape has formed and remove/eject the component from the die.</li><li>Trim excess material, finish the edges and check dimensions, cracking and surface finish.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Car body panels, boxes and containers; for example, car body panels and appliance casings.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Accurate, fast and repeatable once tooling is set up; suitable for mass production. Can form complex sheet profiles in one press stroke; little material is removed.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> High tooling cost; excessive forming can thin, tear or distort the sheet. Only ductile sheet metals are suitable; springback or tearing can affect accuracy.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> punch/die alignment, draw force, dimensions, surface finish and signs of cracking/thinning.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> crushing/trapping at the press and sharp sheet edges; guards are essential.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Cupping and deep drawing</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> A sheet-metal blank is drawn deeply into a die to produce hollow cup or tube-like forms.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>Die-cut the sheet into a blank and clamp it over the deep-drawing die using a pressure pad/retainer.</li><li>A hydraulic press moves the punch into the blank while the retainer controls the sheet and reduces wrinkling.</li><li>The punch pushes the blank into the die cavity to form an initial cup shape.</li><li>The cup is drawn further through another die/stage where required to produce the deeper tubular form.</li><li>Remove and trim the component, then inspect wall thickness, wrinkles, tearing and depth.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Drinks/aerosol cans, fire extinguishers and deep containers; for example, sink bowls, drinks cans and cookware.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Produces seamless hollow shapes without welded joints; consistent wall profiles in repeated runs.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> High setup cost; often requires several operations; poor pressure control causes wrinkling or tearing. Excessive drawing can thin or split walls; deep shapes may require several drawing stages.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> pressure-pad force, punch/die alignment, draw depth and consistent wall thickness.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> crushing/trapping at the hydraulic press and sharp edges.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Spinning</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> A circular sheet blank rotates while a roller progressively forms it over a mandrel.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>Mount the mandrel in the chuck and hold the metal blank securely between the mandrel and tailstock.</li><li>Rotate the blank and mandrel together at a controlled speed.</li><li>Bring the roller tool into contact so pressure begins stretching the metal over the mandrel.</li><li>Move the roller progressively along the mandrel while maintaining pressure until the blank follows its profile.</li><li>Remove the component, trim excess material and check symmetry, finish and wall thickness.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Saucepans, kettle bodies and light shades; for example, lampshades, funnels and metal bowls.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Low-cost tooling for circular, hollow forms; seamless curved profiles.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Limited to radial symmetry; wall thickness must be controlled. Limited to rotationally symmetrical shapes; skilled control or CNC programming is needed.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> secure blank/mandrel, correct speed and roller pressure, symmetry and thickness.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> entanglement and contact with rotating work.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Bending</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Sheet metal is cold formed into a straight bend using a punch and die, commonly in a press brake.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>Mark the required bend line and position the sheet accurately over the die.</li><li>Secure the sheet so the bend line remains aligned with the punch and die.</li><li>Lower the punch so pressure forces the sheet around/into the die to create the bend.</li><li>Raise the punch and remove the formed work from the machine.</li><li>Check the bend angle and dimensions, then trim or finish where required.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Brackets, frames, casings and housings; for example, brackets, shelving and enclosures.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Forms accurate bends without removing material. Creates strong angled profiles without joins; fast for brackets and folded sheet parts.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Spring-back may affect accuracy and excessive bending can crack the metal. Springback affects final angle; tight bends may crack or distort metal.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> bend-line position, punch/die alignment and final angle.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> trapping/crushing and sharp sheet edges.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Rolling</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Metal passes between rotating rollers to reduce thickness and increase length.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>For hot rolling, heat the billet above its recrystallisation temperature; cold rolling is carried out below this temperature.</li><li>Feed the billet or sheet between hardened rotating rollers which apply compressive force.</li><li>Reduce the roller gap progressively so the metal becomes thinner and longer.</li><li>Repeat passes as required; hot rolling allows recrystallisation while cold rolling increases strength and hardness through work hardening.</li><li>Remove the rolled material and check thickness, dimensions and surface finish.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Railway/structural sections, sheet and foil; cold-rolled products include containers, drums and saucepans; for example, steel sheet, rails and structural sections.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Efficient and consistent production of sheet/sections. Produces uniform sheet thickness or long constant-section profiles efficiently.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Large machinery; poor control creates thickness variation and cold working reduces ductility. Large machinery and high forces; repeated passes may be needed for major reductions.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> roller gap, temperature where applicable, thickness and surface consistency.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> entanglement/crushing at rollers and burns during hot rolling.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Drop forging</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> A heated billet is forced between shaped dies to produce a tough component.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>Secure the cast tool-steel lower die on the anvil and matching upper die to the hydraulic ram.</li><li>Heat the billet above its recrystallisation temperature so it can deform without excessive work hardening.</li><li>Use tongs to place the heated billet accurately into the lower die.</li><li>Apply the hydraulic ram so the upper die forces the billet into the die cavity.</li><li>Lift the ram, remove the forging, cool it and trim/finish excess material before inspection.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Spanners, pliers and other tough tools; for example, spanners, crankshafts and connecting rods.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Very strong components; suitable for high-volume production. Compresses metal to improve grain flow and strength for highly loaded parts.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Expensive dies and machinery. Expensive dies and presses; flash removal and finishing may be required.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> billet temperature, die alignment/fill and final dimensions.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> hot metal, heavy dies and crushing hazards.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Wrought-iron forging</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Heated wrought iron is repeatedly hammered over an anvil to create decorative or bespoke forms.</p><h4>Step by step</h4><ol><li>Heat the iron until it reaches a cherry-red colour and becomes malleable.</li><li>Place the hot metal securely on an anvil or suitable former.</li><li>Hammer the metal repeatedly, manually or with powered equipment, to develop the required form.</li><li>Reheat when necessary so the metal remains workable while shaping continues.</li><li>Allow the completed work to cool before further heat treatment or finishing.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Gates, fencing and decorative metalwork; for example, decorative gates and bespoke railings.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Flexible for individual decorative shapes and bespoke work. Allows custom shapes and local changes in section without complex dies.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Labour/skill intensive and slower than industrial forming. Labour intensive; heating and repeated hammering reduce dimensional consistency.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> heating and comparison against jigs/templates.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> burns, sparks and hammering hazards.</p></div></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Redistribution / casting</h2><div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Sand casting</h3><ol><li>Make a pattern slightly larger than the finished component and place it inside the two-part flask.</li><li>Pack prepared sand tightly around the pattern, separate the flask and remove the pattern to leave the cavity.</li><li>Create the runner, riser and vents so metal can enter and displaced gases can escape.</li><li>Reassemble the mould, pour molten metal through the runner and allow it to cool and solidify.</li><li>Break/shake away the sand, cut off the runner/riser and clean and finish the casting.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Engine parts, post boxes and larger cast components; for example, engine blocks, pump housings and machine bases.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Suitable for large shapes and low quantities; cheaper tooling than permanent dies. Cheap mould tooling accommodates large castings and complex cavities.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Rougher finish and lower accuracy than die casting; sand mould is destroyed.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> pattern accuracy, packed sand, runners/risers/vents, controlled pour and porosity.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> severe molten-metal burn risk; PPE and ventilation.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Gravity die casting</h3><ol><li>Preheat the permanent metal die and apply a coating to improve flow and release.</li><li>Melt the required metal and transfer it safely in a ladle.</li><li>Pour the molten metal slowly into the die cavity so it fills under gravity.</li><li>Leave the metal until it has cooled and solidified sufficiently.</li><li>Open the die, remove the casting and machine/trim excess material before inspection.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Door knobs and automotive components; for example, aluminium cookware and automotive components.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Reusable metal dies give more consistent dimensions and finish than sand moulds.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Higher die cost; mainly suitable for appropriate lower-melting alloys. Higher tooling cost than sand casting; gravity limits filling of very thin sections.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> die temperature/coating, pour, fill, dimensions and finish.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> molten-metal and hot-die burns.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Pressure die casting — hot chamber</h3><ol><li>Maintain low-melting-point metal in the integral furnace with the gooseneck submerged in the molten metal.</li><li>Close and clamp the reusable steel die securely.</li><li>Use the hydraulic plunger to force molten metal through the gooseneck into the die at high pressure.</li><li>Hold it while the metal cools and solidifies rapidly in the cavity.</li><li>Open the die and use ejector pins to remove the component before trimming and inspection.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Toy cars, door handles and zinc components; for example, zinc-alloy door handles and small fittings.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Very fast, accurate and repeatable. Very short cycles because metal feeds directly from the integral furnace.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Expensive tooling and unsuitable for high-melting metals. Immersed injection parts restrict use to compatible low-melting alloys.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> temperature, injection pressure, complete filling, porosity and dimensions.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> high-pressure molten metal and crushing hazards.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Pressure die casting — cold chamber</h3><ol><li>Melt the higher-melting-point metal in a separate furnace.</li><li>Measure and ladle the required amount of molten metal into the cold shot chamber.</li><li>Close and clamp the steel die securely.</li><li>A hydraulic plunger forces the molten metal from the chamber into the die at high pressure.</li><li>Allow it to solidify, then open the die, eject the component and inspect/finish it.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Aluminium components and repeated engineering castings; for example, aluminium gearbox housings and motor casings.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Accurate high-volume casting of metals unsuitable for hot chamber. High-pressure filling makes detailed aluminium housings with repeatable dimensions.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Slower because metal is transferred from a separate furnace; expensive tooling. Molten metal must be ladled each cycle; trapped gas can cause porosity.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> measured charge, temperature, pressure, fill, porosity and dimensions.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> molten-metal transfer and high-pressure machinery.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Investment casting</h3><ol><li>Produce an accurate wax replica and attach it to a wax sprue/tree.</li><li>Coat the wax assembly in ceramic/clay slurry and stucco until a strong shell forms.</li><li>Fire the shell in a kiln so it hardens and the wax melts out, leaving the cavity.</li><li>Pour molten metal into the ceramic mould and allow it to cool and solidify.</li><li>Break away the shell, clean the casting and machine off the runner/sprue.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Jewellery, hip-joint replacements and precision components; for example, turbine blades, jewellery and precision brackets.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Excellent detail and near-net-shape accuracy for intricate parts.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Several stages make it slower and more costly. Wax patterns and disposable ceramic shells increase cost and lead time.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> wax accuracy, shell integrity, complete filling and dimensions.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> hot kiln, molten metal and ceramic dust.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Low-temperature pewter casting</h3><ol><li>Make the mould from MDF with the required cavity and pouring channel.</li><li>Sandwich the mould between two MDF pieces and clamp it securely.</li><li>Melt the pewter in a ladle and carefully pour it into the cavity.</li><li>Allow it to cool before opening the mould and removing the casting.</li><li>Remove the sprue with a junior hacksaw, then file and finish with wet-and-dry paper.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Jewellery, keychains and small decorative products; for example, badges, jewellery and decorative tokens.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Simple workshop casting because pewter has a low melting temperature. Low pouring temperature enables detailed small castings with simpler equipment.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Limited to small suitable products; poor pouring causes incomplete casts. Pewter is relatively soft and unsuitable for heavily loaded parts.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> secure mould, complete filling, surface and finishing.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> molten pewter can cause serious burns.</p></div></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Addition / fabrication</h2><div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Arc welding</h3><ol><li>Clean, position and clamp the work, attach the earth clamp and secure the coated filler electrode in the holder.</li><li>Set the welding current to suit the material and electrode.</li><li>Strike the arc between the electrode and work to create the heat needed to melt the metal.</li><li>Maintain the arc and move along the joint so a continuous molten weld pool and bead are produced.</li><li>Allow the weld to cool, remove slag/flux with a wire brush and inspect the joint.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Rough/general steel fabrication; for example, steel frames and structural repairs.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Strong, portable and suitable for general fabrication. Strong permanent joins on thick steel with relatively portable equipment.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Slag/spatter and rougher finish. Heat can distort metal; slag removal and operator skill may be needed.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> current, stable arc, bead, penetration, cracks and porosity.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> welding mask, leather PPE and ventilation.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>MIG welding</h3><ol><li>Clean, align and clamp the components securely.</li><li>Set the wire feed, electrical settings and inert shielding-gas flow.</li><li>Operate the torch so the thin consumable wire is fed continuously towards the joint.</li><li>An electric arc forms between the wire and workpiece, melting both to create the weld pool.</li><li>Move steadily along the joint to form an even weld bead, then cool and inspect it.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Bike frames, vehicle exhausts and general fabrication; for example, car body fabrication and steel frames.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Fast with automatic wire feed; suitable for thicker materials. Continuous wire feed makes long welds fast and easy to automate.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Shielding gas is vulnerable to wind; poor settings can cause spatter.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> wire feed, gas flow, travel speed, bead consistency and porosity.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> welding mask, gloves/leather PPE and ventilation.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>TIG welding</h3><ol><li>Clean, align and clamp the work accurately.</li><li>Fit the tungsten electrode and set the argon shielding-gas flow.</li><li>Strike an arc between the non-consumable tungsten electrode and workpiece so the parent metal melts.</li><li>Feed a separate filler rod manually into the molten pool when required.</li><li>Move the torch/filler consistently along the joint, then cool and inspect the precise weld.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Pool ladders, car exhausts, stainless steel and precision work; for example, stainless-steel pipework and bicycle frames.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Clean, precise, high-quality weld with little spatter. Precise heat control produces clean welds on thin stainless steel or aluminium.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Slower and more skill-intensive than MIG; clean surfaces are essential.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> clean surfaces, gas flow, arc/filler control and even bead.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> welding mask, leather PPE and ventilation.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Spot welding</h3><ol><li>Clean and overlap the two thin metal sheets at the joint.</li><li>Place the joint between the copper electrodes.</li><li>Close the electrodes so they clamp the sheets under pressure.</li><li>Pass a high current through the sheets; electrical resistance produces heat and forms a molten weld nugget.</li><li>Stop the current but maintain pressure while the nugget solidifies, then release and inspect it.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Mild-steel sheet, car bodies and mass-produced assemblies; for example, car body panels and sheet-metal cabinets.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Very fast, repeatable sheet-metal joints with no filler metal.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Mainly for overlapping thin sheet and needs access from both sides. Requires overlapping accessible sheets; joint is localised rather than continuous.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> electrode position/pressure, current/time and nugget consistency.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> crushing, electrical hazards, hot metal and sparks.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Oxy-acetylene welding</h3><ol><li>Clean, align and secure the metal components.</li><li>Mix oxygen and acetylene at the torch, ignite it and adjust to the required neutral flame.</li><li>Heat the edges of the parent metal until a molten weld pool forms.</li><li>Dip a filler rod of the same/suitable metal into the pool and move the torch and rod along the joint.</li><li>Complete the weld, allow it to cool and inspect the finished joint.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Mild-steel sheets and plates; for example, repair work and thin steel fabrication.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Portable and does not require an electrical welding supply at the joint. Portable flame can weld, braze or heat metal without electricity.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Large heat-affected zone can cause distortion; slower than many arc methods.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> correct flame, clean joint, weld pool/filler and distortion.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> pressurised gas cylinders, open flame, hot metal and fumes.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Soft soldering</h3><ol><li>Clean the metal surfaces and position the components accurately together.</li><li>Apply flux to prevent oxidation and help the solder flow.</li><li>Heat the joint using a soldering iron or suitable heat source.</li><li>Apply the low-melting-point solder so it melts and flows into the joint by capillary action.</li><li>Remove the heat, allow the solder to solidify and inspect/clean the joint.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Electronics and light metal/jewellery work; for example, electronic connections and light-gauge metalwork.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Low heat and simple equipment; parent metals do not melt. Low joining temperature limits heat damage to delicate components.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Weaker than welding/brazing and may leave a visible joint. Joint has relatively low mechanical strength and poor high-temperature resistance.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> clean surfaces, flux, sufficient heating and complete solder flow.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> hot iron, molten solder and flux fumes.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Brazing / hard soldering</h3><ol><li>Clean the surfaces with emery cloth and align the metals closely at the joint.</li><li>Apply flux to prevent oxidation and help the filler flow.</li><li>Heat the surrounding parent metals evenly with a flame.</li><li>Apply the higher-melting-point spelter so it melts and flows through the joint by capillary action.</li><li>Allow the joint to cool and solidify, then clean away remaining flux and inspect it.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Pipework and joining similar or dissimilar metals; for example, copper pipe fittings and carbide tool tips.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Joins dissimilar metals with less base-metal melting and distortion than welding.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Higher temperature and careful surface preparation/close fit required. Joint preparation and capillary clearance are critical; filler adds cost.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> clean surfaces, joint gap, even heat and complete spelter flow.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> flame, hot metal and flux fumes.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Pop riveting</h3><ol><li>Overlap and clamp the components, then mark the rivet position.</li><li>Drill a hole through both pieces to the correct diameter.</li><li>Insert the pop rivet and place its mandrel into the rivet gun.</li><li>Squeeze the gun so the mandrel pulls and expands the rivet behind the material, clamping the sheets together.</li><li>Continue until the mandrel snaps, then check that the joint is tight.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Sheet-metal products and one-sided-access assemblies; for example, sheet-metal enclosures and aircraft panels.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Quick permanent fixing with access needed from only one side. One-sided access is enough, useful for hollow or closed assemblies.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Needs drilling and is not intended for easy dismantling. Permanent hole and rivet head remain; joints may be less strong than welding.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> correct hole/rivet diameter, alignment and fully set rivet.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> eye protection and care with sharp sheet edges/broken mandrels.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Snap-and-set riveting</h3><ol><li>Align and clamp the components and drill matching holes.</li><li>Insert a solid rivet of the correct diameter and length.</li><li>Support the manufactured rivet head firmly on the snap/anvil.</li><li>Use the setting tool/hammer to deform the projecting end and form the second head.</li><li>Check both heads and make sure the joint is tight without damaging the material.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Permanent metal joints and traditional sheet-metal fabrication; for example, traditional metalwork and structural brackets.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Creates a robust mechanical joint without melting parent metals.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Needs access to both sides and accurate aligned holes.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> hole alignment, rivet size and properly formed heads.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> eye protection and care with drilling, hammering and sharp edges.</p></div></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Wastage processes</h2><div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Milling</h3><ol><li>Clamp the metal securely to the milling-machine table, normally using a vice.</li><li>Fit the correct multi-tooth milling cutter into the spindle.</li><li>Set the required spindle speed and depth of cut.</li><li>Feed the work/cutter so the rotating teeth progressively remove material and create the required form.</li><li>Stop the machine, measure the component, deburr and complete final finishing.</li></ol><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Accurate and versatile. Produces precise slots, pockets and flat faces with controlled tool paths.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Creates waste and needs correct setup/tool selection. Material waste and tool wear; machining complex parts can take time.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> secure work, cutter, speed/depth and dimensional measurement.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> guards, eye protection and hands/clothing clear of cutter and swarf.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Turning</h3><ol><li>Secure the metal bar accurately in the lathe chuck/between centres.</li><li>Fit and position the single-point cutting tool at the correct height.</li><li>Select a suitable spindle speed and rotate the workpiece.</li><li>Move the cutting tool along or into the rotating metal to remove material and produce the required profile.</li><li>Stop the lathe, measure, deburr/finish and remove the component safely.</li></ol><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Accurate production of rotational forms. Accurate cylindrical diameters and threads from rotating workpieces.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Mostly limited to rotational features; creates swarf and tool wear.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> secure work, tool position, speed/feed and measurements.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> entanglement, sharp swarf and cutting tools.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Flame cutting</h3><ol><li>Clean and secure the low-carbon steel plate and mark the cutting line.</li><li>Mix oxygen and acetylene, ignite the torch and adjust the flame.</li><li>Heat the start of the cut until the steel reaches the required temperature.</li><li>Apply the additional oxygen jet and move steadily so molten/oxidised metal is blown from the cut.</li><li>Allow the work to cool, remove slag and finish/check the edges.</li></ol><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Economical for thick carbon-steel plate and large profiles.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Less accurate than plasma/laser and gives a larger heat-affected edge. Wide heat-affected zone and rougher edges; unsuitable for aluminium.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> torch speed/distance, flame/oxygen and cut straightness.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> pressurised gases, flame, hot metal and sparks.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Plasma cutting</h3><ol><li>Clean, mark and secure the conductive metal.</li><li>Set the plasma cutter's current and gas settings for the material/thickness.</li><li>Generate the high-temperature ionised plasma jet and position it at the start of the cut.</li><li>Move the torch steadily so the plasma melts the metal and the gas blows it from the kerf.</li><li>Allow the component to cool, remove slag and inspect/finish the edges.</li></ol><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Fast, clean and more accurate than flame cutting. Fast cutting of conductive metals, including stainless steel and aluminium.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Only works with conductive materials and needs specialist equipment. Heat-affected edges and fumes; precision is below fine laser cutting.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> settings, torch speed/distance, dimensions and edge finish.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> intense heat/light, sparks, fumes and electrical hazards.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Laser cutting</h3><ol><li>Clean the metal and position it flat and securely on the cutting bed.</li><li>Load/check the digital cutting path and set the laser focus.</li><li>Select suitable power and speed for the material and thickness.</li><li>Run the machine so the focused laser follows the programmed path and melts the metal along the cut line.</li><li>Remove the component safely, clean/deburr it and inspect dimensions and edge quality.</li></ol><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Very high accuracy and complex CAD/CAM shapes. Narrow kerf, precise detailed shapes and minimal tool contact.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Expensive and less suitable for very thick material than some alternatives. High machine cost; reflective/thick materials can be challenging.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> path, focus, power/speed, kerf, dimensions and edge quality.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> enclosure, extraction and laser safety procedures; never view the beam directly.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Punching / stamping</h3><ol><li>Clean and position the thin sheet metal accurately on the die.</li><li>Program/set the CNC press for the required shape and fit the correct punch/die.</li><li>Locate the sheet accurately so the cutting position is correct.</li><li>The punch moves down at high force and shears through the sheet into the die.</li><li>Remove the slug/waste and finished component, then inspect and deburr where necessary.</li></ol><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> Very fast and accurate once tooling is set up. Very fast identical holes or profiles using reusable dies.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Dedicated tooling costs and burrs; shape changes require new tooling.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> sheet position, punch/die alignment, dimensions and burr quality.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> severe crushing/shearing hazard; guards are essential.</p></div></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Applying metal-process knowledge in AQA answers</h2><div class="study-card"><p>AQA requires students to <strong>describe processes and justify their suitability for specific products and scales of production</strong>. Connect the required shape, material, volume, surface finish and tooling cost to the method selected.</p><ul><li><strong>Sand casting:</strong> a suitable choice for a large, complex metal housing made in small numbers, because relatively inexpensive sand moulds can form intricate cavities; expect rougher surfaces and secondary machining.</li><li><strong>Pressure die casting:</strong> suitable for high-volume, dimensionally consistent small components made from appropriate alloys; expensive permanent dies are economical when spread over many units.</li><li><strong>Deep drawing:</strong> suitable for seamless hollow sheet-metal products such as cans; multiple stages and control of wrinkling or tearing may be needed.</li><li><strong>Drop forging:</strong> suitable for tough load-bearing tools because deformation can improve the grain flow and mechanical performance, although dies and equipment are costly.</li></ul><p><strong>Comparison:</strong> explain why one process meets the product requirements <em>better than an alternative</em>, then acknowledge a limitation. Avoid vague claims such as “casting is cheap”: tooling, material, size and production volume all affect cost.</p></div></section>
