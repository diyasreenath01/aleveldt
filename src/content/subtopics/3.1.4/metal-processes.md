<section class="note-panel"><h2>Metal Processes</h2><div class="note-intro"><p>The teacher material groups metal processing into <strong>forming</strong> (deforming metal), <strong>redistribution</strong> (melting/casting), <strong>addition</strong> (joining) and <strong>wasting</strong> (removing material). For each process, learn the sequence, uses, quality-control points and safety risks.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Forming processes</h2><div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Press forming</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Sheet metal is pressed into a shallow 3D shape using a punch and die.</p><h4>Steps</h4><ol><li>Inspect the sheet for defects/thickness variation.</li><li>Position and align it between punch and die.</li><li>Clamp/hold the blank.</li><li>Apply mechanical or hydraulic force.</li><li>Release and eject the formed component.</li><li>Inspect dimensions, cracking and finish.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> car panels and other shallow sheet forms.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> fast and repeatable for industrial production.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> high tooling cost; excessive draw can tear/thin material.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> alignment, dimensions, pressure and avoiding over-draw.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> guards; trapping/crushing; sharp sheet edges.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Cupping & deep drawing</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> A punch draws sheet into a deeper die cavity; deep drawing can be carried out in stages.</p><h4>Steps</h4><ol><li>Lubricate and position the blank over the die.</li><li>Hold it with the blank holder/retainer to reduce wrinkling.</li><li>Drive the punch into the die to make a cup.</li><li>Draw further/in stages for a deeper tube-like form.</li><li>Eject and trim if required.</li><li>Inspect wall thickness, wrinkles, tearing and depth.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> cans, aerosol cans and fire extinguishers.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> makes deep seamless shapes.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> high setup cost, so suited to mass production; risk of thinning/wrinkling.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> blank-holder pressure, punch/die clearance, lubrication, thickness and draw depth.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> guards, trapping/crushing and sharp edges.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Spinning</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> A circular sheet blank rotates at high speed while a roller forms it over a mandrel.</p><h4>Steps</h4><ol><li>Secure the mandrel in the chuck.</li><li>Hold the blank between mandrel and tailstock.</li><li>Rotate at a controlled speed.</li><li>Move the roller progressively against the blank.</li><li>Maintain pressure until the blank follows the mandrel.</li><li>Remove and trim excess material.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> bowls, reflectors, kettles, saucepans and light shades.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> useful for rotationally symmetrical hollow forms; can be automated.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> limited to radial symmetry; wall thinning must be controlled.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> mandrel, spin speed, roller pressure/size, symmetry and wall thickness.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> entanglement, sharp edges and contact with rotating work.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Bending</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Sheet/section metal is bent using a press brake, punch/die or jig.</p><h4>Steps</h4><ol><li>Inspect and mark the bend line.</li><li>Position material accurately.</li><li>Clamp it in the bending machine/jig.</li><li>Apply force to create the bend.</li><li>Allow for spring-back if required.</li><li>Check angle, radius, cracking and distortion.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> brackets, frames, casings and housings.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> forms bends without cutting away material.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> spring-back or cracking can reduce accuracy.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> bend-line position, angle, radius, secure work and avoiding overstretching.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> pinch points, trapping and crushing.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Rolling</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Metal passes through rollers to reduce thickness; it may be hot or cold rolled.</p><h4>Steps</h4><ol><li>Inspect the slab/billet.</li><li>Heat for hot rolling or use at room temperature for cold rolling.</li><li>Feed through rotating rollers.</li><li>Reduce thickness gradually over multiple passes.</li><li>Control roller gap to reach the required gauge.</li><li>Trim/check thickness, flatness and finish.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> sheet, foil, structural sections; cold-rolled products include chairs, drums and saucepans.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> continuous, consistent reduction of thickness.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> large machinery; roller setup must be controlled.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> roller gap, thickness, temperature, lubricant, staged rolling and edge trimming.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> entanglement, crushing and heat.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Drop forging</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> A heated billet is repeatedly forced between shaped dies.</p><h4>Steps</h4><ol><li>Prepare the matched dies.</li><li>Cut and heat the billet above its recrystallisation temperature.</li><li>Place it in the lower die.</li><li>Apply the upper die/ram repeatedly until the cavity fills.</li><li>Remove and cool the forging.</li><li>Trim flash and inspect.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> tough tools/components such as spanners, pliers, screwdrivers and crankshafts.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> produces tough, strong components; suited to mass production.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> expensive dies/equipment; excess flash may need trimming.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> billet temperature, die fill, pressure/number of strikes, shape accuracy and flash.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> heat PPE, burns, crushing and trapping.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Wrought-iron forging</h3><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Heated iron is shaped by repeated hammering, traditionally by a blacksmith.</p><h4>Steps</h4><ol><li>Inspect the bar/billet.</li><li>Heat until malleable/cherry red.</li><li>Place on an anvil or former.</li><li>Hammer repeatedly to shape.</li><li>Reheat when required.</li><li>Cool and inspect the final form.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> decorative gates, bed frames, candlesticks and bespoke work.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> flexible for one-off decorative forms.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> labour/skill intensive and slower than mass-production forging.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> correct heat, jigs/formers, hammer pressure and final shape.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> burns, ventilation, crushing and repetitive strain.</p></div></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Casting / redistribution</h2><div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Sand casting</h3><ol><li>Make an accurate pattern and compact suitable sand around it.</li><li>Remove the pattern to leave the mould cavity; provide gates, risers and vents.</li><li>Melt the metal to the correct temperature.</li><li>Pour at a controlled rate.</li><li>Allow to solidify and cool.</li><li>Break away the sand, remove sprues/risers and inspect.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> engine parts, larger components and workshop/school castings.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> suitable for one-offs/low quantities and relatively large shapes.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> mould is destroyed; rougher finish/lower accuracy than permanent dies.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> sand type/density, pattern, vents/risers, temperature, pour rate, porosity and dimensions.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> molten-metal burns and ventilation/PPE.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Gravity die casting</h3><p>Molten metal is poured by gravity into a reusable permanent die.</p><ol><li>Prepare, align and lubricate the die.</li><li>Melt metal to the correct temperature/viscosity.</li><li>Pour into the die and allow air to vent.</li><li>Allow controlled cooling.</li><li>Open the die and eject.</li><li>Trim and inspect.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> handles, toys and housings.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> reusable mould; better repeatability/finish than sand casting.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> higher die cost; less suited to very low quantities.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> die alignment, temperature/viscosity, vents, cooling, flash/leaks and parting lines.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> burns and ventilation.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Pressure die casting — hot chamber</h3><p>Lower-melting-point molten alloy is held in an attached heated chamber and injected into the die.</p><ol><li>Prepare and lubricate the die.</li><li>Maintain molten metal in the attached chamber.</li><li>Draw metal into the injection system.</li><li>Inject under high pressure.</li><li>Cool rapidly in the die.</li><li>Eject and inspect.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> small repeated components, toy cars and door handles.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> fast, accurate, repeatable production.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> expensive tooling; hot-chamber system is limited to suitable lower-melting alloys.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> die/ejector pins, metal temperature/viscosity, injection pressure, cooling, porosity, parting lines and dimensions.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> molten metal, burns, PPE and ventilation.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Pressure die casting — cold chamber</h3><p>Higher-melting-point alloy is melted separately, transferred to a chamber and forced into the die.</p><ol><li>Prepare and lubricate the die.</li><li>Melt metal in a separate furnace.</li><li>Ladle it into the injection chamber.</li><li>Force it into the closed die with a piston.</li><li>Cool and solidify.</li><li>Eject and inspect.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> repeated precision metal components.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> allows pressure die casting of alloys unsuitable for hot-chamber machines.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> extra transfer stage; high equipment/tooling cost.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> die cleanliness/alignment, temperature, transfer, pressure, fill, cooling, porosity/misruns and dimensions.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> molten-metal burns, PPE and ventilation.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Investment casting</h3><p>A wax pattern is replaced by molten metal inside a ceramic shell.</p><ol><li>Create accurate wax patterns and assemble a wax tree.</li><li>Coat with ceramic slurry and build/harden the shell.</li><li>Melt out the wax.</li><li>Pour molten metal into the cavity.</li><li>Allow to cool.</li><li>Break away the ceramic, clean and inspect.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> precision parts, complex repeated shapes and jewellery.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> high detail and complex shapes.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> more stages and slower than simpler casting methods.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> wax accuracy, ceramic thickness, cavity integrity, metal temperature/flow, porosity and dimensions.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> ceramic dust and molten-metal burns.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Pewter casting</h3><ol><li>Prepare a clean mould.</li><li>Melt pewter at its relatively low casting temperature.</li><li>Pour carefully into the mould.</li><li>Allow to cool and solidify.</li><li>Remove the casting.</li><li>Inspect the surface and dimensions.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> craft items and tankards.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> low-melting metal suitable for workshop casting.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> air traps/poor flow can create defects.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> vents, mould, temperature/viscosity, bubbles and shape.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> burns and ventilation.</p></div></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Welding and joining</h2><div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Arc welding</h3><p>An electric arc between the work and a consumable electrode creates enough heat to fuse the joint.</p><ol><li>Clean the joint surfaces.</li><li>Select the correct electrode.</li><li>Set current/polarity.</li><li>Clamp and align the work.</li><li>Strike the arc and deposit a weld bead.</li><li>Cool, remove slag/finish and inspect.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> steel, rough/general fabrication.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> portable, relatively easy and strong.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> spatter/slag and rougher finish.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> current, joint gap, arc stability, bead, penetration, cracks/porosity.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> welding mask, leather PPE and ventilation.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>MIG welding</h3><p>A continuously fed consumable wire acts as electrode and filler while shielding gas protects the weld.</p><ol><li>Clean and align the metal.</li><li>Set voltage, wire feed and shielding-gas flow.</li><li>Clamp components.</li><li>Strike the arc and feed wire continuously through the gun.</li><li>Move steadily to create an even bead.</li><li>Cool and inspect.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> frames, structures, automotive/general fabrication and thicker materials.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> fast; wire is supplied automatically; high deposition rate.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> more spatter/rougher finish than TIG; shielding gas/equipment required.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> gas flow, voltage/wire feed, alignment, bead consistency, porosity, cracks and distortion.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> welding mask, leather PPE and ventilation.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>TIG welding</h3><p>A non-consumable tungsten electrode creates the arc; filler rod is fed separately if required and argon shields the weld.</p><ol><li>Clean the metal thoroughly.</li><li>Set the tungsten electrode and argon flow.</li><li>Clamp accurately.</li><li>Strike/control the arc.</li><li>Feed filler rod manually if required.</li><li>Cool and inspect the precise weld.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> thin material, precision work, stainless steel and high-quality fabrication.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> clean, precise, high-quality weld with little/no spatter.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> slower and more skill dependent than MIG.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> surface cleanliness, gas purity/flow, alignment, bead control, penetration and distortion.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> welding mask, leather PPE and ventilation.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Oxy-acetylene welding</h3><p>Oxygen and acetylene burn in a torch to melt the joint; a separate filler rod is added.</p><ol><li>Clean and clamp the metals.</li><li>Set the oxygen/acetylene mixture and correct flame.</li><li>Heat the joint area evenly.</li><li>Add filler rod into the molten pool.</li><li>Move along the joint consistently.</li><li>Cool and inspect.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> portable/field work and steel fabrication; can also be used for cutting.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> very portable and does not require electricity.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> slower/messier finish than precision electric processes; high heat input.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> gas mixture/flame, alignment, even heat, bead and distortion.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> burns/fire, gas safety, leather PPE and ventilation.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Spot welding</h3><p>Overlapping sheet is clamped between copper electrodes and electrical resistance creates a local weld spot.</p><ol><li>Clean and overlap the sheets.</li><li>Position between electrodes.</li><li>Apply electrode pressure.</li><li>Pass high current for a controlled time.</li><li>Hold pressure during cooling.</li><li>Inspect weld spacing and strength.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> thin sheet, especially car body shells and mass production.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> very fast; no filler rod; easy to automate.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> limited mainly to overlapping sheet and local spots.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> alignment, clamp force, current/time and weld consistency.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> crushing/pinch risk, electrical/welding PPE and ventilation.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Soft soldering</h3><ol><li>Clean the joint surfaces.</li><li>Apply flux where required.</li><li>Heat the joint with the soldering iron.</li><li>Apply low-melting solder and allow it to flow.</li><li>Remove heat and let it solidify without movement.</li><li>Inspect the electrical/mechanical bond.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> electrical/electronic connections and copper pipework.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> low heat and relatively easy; parent metal is not melted.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> weaker than welding/brazing and can leave a visible solder build-up.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> clean surface, flux, heat, solder flow and electrical continuity/seal.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> burns and ventilation.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Brazing / hard soldering</h3><ol><li>Clean the close-fitting joint with abrasive/emery.</li><li>Apply flux.</li><li>Arrange securely on the brazing hearth/fire bricks.</li><li>Heat the joint evenly with a brazing torch.</li><li>Apply spelter/filler so capillary action draws it into the joint.</li><li>Cool without movement, clean away flux and inspect.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> pipework and joining similar or dissimilar metals.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> strong joint without melting the parent metals; useful for dissimilar metals.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> surfaces/gap must be prepared carefully; heat and flux cleanup required.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> cleanliness, close fit, flux coverage, even temperature, filler flow and seal/strength.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> burns and ventilation.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Pop riveting</h3><ol><li>Select the correct rivet size/grip range.</li><li>Drill aligned holes through the sheets and remove burrs.</li><li>Insert the rivet body.</li><li>Place the mandrel in the rivet gun.</li><li>Operate the gun so the rivet expands and the mandrel snaps.</li><li>Inspect seating, head formation and gaps.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> sheet assemblies such as aircraft skins and boat hulls; useful where access is from one side.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> quick permanent mechanical fixing with one-sided access.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> permanent joint; drilled holes are required.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> hole alignment, sheet contact, correct grip, complete mandrel snap and joint tightness.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> eye protection from snapped mandrels/drilling.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Snap-and-set riveting</h3><ol><li>Mark and drill aligned holes.</li><li>Insert the solid rivet.</li><li>Support one head with the set.</li><li>Use the snap/hammer to form the second head.</li><li>Ensure sheets remain tightly together.</li><li>Inspect head shape and joint rigidity.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> sheet metal where both sides are accessible.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> strong permanent mechanical joint.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> requires access to both sides and careful head forming.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> aligned holes, secure sheets and correctly formed rivet head.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> hammer/hand and eye injury risk.</p></div></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Wasting / cutting processes</h2><div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Milling</h3><ol><li>Inspect and clamp the work securely.</li><li>Select/install the correct cutter.</li><li>Set speed, feed and depth of cut.</li><li>Machine with the rotating cutter.</li><li>Use repeated passes where needed.</li><li>Measure dimensions and inspect finish.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> slots, grooves, flat faces and profiles.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> accurate and versatile.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> creates waste/swarf; setup and tool condition affect quality.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> cutter sharpness, alignment, speed/feed/depth, lubricant/cooling, dimensions and finish.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> secure work, eye protection and entanglement controls.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Turning</h3><ol><li>Prepare and secure the cylindrical work in the chuck.</li><li>Check alignment/concentricity.</li><li>Select the cutting tool and set correct tool height.</li><li>Set spindle speed and feed.</li><li>Turn to the required diameter/profile.</li><li>Measure with gauges/micrometers and inspect finish.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> shafts, cylindrical components, threads and turned profiles.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> accurate rotational components and good finish.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> limited mainly to rotational forms; creates swarf.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> concentricity, speed/feed, tool sharpness, coolant/lubricant, diameter and run-out.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> eye protection, entanglement and cutting hazards.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Flame cutting</h3><ol><li>Mark the cut line.</li><li>Set up oxy-fuel equipment and gas pressures.</li><li>Heat steel to ignition temperature.</li><li>Apply the oxygen jet to oxidise/cut the metal.</li><li>Follow the line steadily.</li><li>Remove slag and inspect the edge.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> structural steel.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> effective for cutting thick steel and portable work.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> heat-affected/rough edge may need finishing.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> gas ratio, temperature, cutting speed, line accuracy and slag/edge quality.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> fire/burn PPE, eye protection and ventilation.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Plasma cutting</h3><ol><li>Secure and ground the metal.</li><li>Load/mark the cutting path.</li><li>Set gas flow and current.</li><li>Ignite the plasma arc.</li><li>Cut at a controlled speed.</li><li>Inspect kerf, burrs and dimensions.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> conductive metal sheet/bar.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> fast cutting of conductive metals and complex CNC paths.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> heat-affected zone/burrs may require finishing.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> gas/current, program/alignment, speed, kerf width and edge finish.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> arc/burn protection, eye protection and ventilation.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Laser cutting</h3><ol><li>Prepare and secure flat sheet.</li><li>Load and verify the CAD/CAM toolpath.</li><li>Set focus, power, assist gas and speed.</li><li>Run the CNC laser cut/etch.</li><li>Allow the part to cool and remove it.</li><li>Inspect dimensions and edges.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> complex flat-sheet designs, cutting and etching.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> accurate, repeatable and suitable for complex profiles.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> heat/burn marks can occur if settings are wrong.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> alignment, focus, power/speed, secure work, kerf, dross/burrs and burn marks.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> extraction, fire risk and never view the laser directly.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Punching / stamping</h3><ol><li>Inspect the sheet.</li><li>Position accurately in the die set.</li><li>Select the correct punch/die and check clearance/sharpness.</li><li>Set press force and stroke.</li><li>Punch the hole/profile.</li><li>Inspect burrs, dimensions and deformation.</li></ol><p><strong>Uses:</strong> holes, profiles, hinges and brackets.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> fast and repeatable for repeated sheet features.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> dedicated tooling; burrs/waste are produced.</p><p><strong>QC:</strong> depth/stroke, tool sharpness, alignment and repeated shape.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> pinch/crush and sharp-edge hazards.</p></div></div></section>
