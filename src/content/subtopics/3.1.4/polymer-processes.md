<section class="note-panel"><h2>Vacuum forming</h2><div class="study-card"><p><strong>What it is:</strong> A thermoforming process in which a heated thermoplastic sheet is shaped over a single mould by removing the air underneath it. Atmospheric pressure then pulls the softened sheet tightly onto the mould.</p><h3>Step by step</h3><ol><li>Place a suitable mould/former in the vacuum former. The mould needs draft angles, no undercuts, rounded corners and small vent holes so air can escape.</li><li>Clamp a thermoplastic sheet such as HIPS, acrylic or PVC securely in the frame above the mould.</li><li>Heat the sheet evenly until it reaches forming temperature and sags uniformly.</li><li>Raise the mould into the softened sheet.</li><li>Switch on the vacuum pump. Air is removed through the vent holes and atmospheric pressure pulls the sheet tightly over the mould surface.</li><li>Keep the vacuum applied while the plastic cools and sets to the mould shape.</li><li>Lower/release the mould, remove the formed component, trim away excess sheet and finish the edges.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> blister packaging, food trays, yoghurt pots, baths, signage and protective covers.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> relatively low-cost moulds; good for batch production; moulds are easy to modify; a wide range of colours can be used.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> trimming creates waste; deep shapes can become thin; poor mould design can cause webbing, tearing or incomplete pull-down; not suitable for highly complex shapes or undercuts.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> check even heating and uniform sag before forming; inspect for thinning at deep draws, webbing between corners, tearing and incomplete pull-down; measure wall-thickness consistency and dimensional accuracy after trimming; ensure vent holes, draft angles and rounded corners are correct.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> burns from heaters and hot plastic; pinch hazards from the moving platen; sharp trimmed edges; eye protection and heat-resistant gloves where required.</p></div></section>

<section class="note-panel"><h2>Thermoforming / pressure forming</h2><div class="study-card"><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Similar to vacuum forming, but the heated sheet is formed between matched positive and negative moulds. Vacuum and pressure allow deeper shapes, sharper detail and clearer textures/logos.</p><h3>Step by step</h3><ol><li>Clamp the thermoplastic sheet securely in a frame.</li><li>Heat the sheet evenly until it reaches its forming temperature.</li><li>Position the softened sheet accurately between the matched positive and negative moulds.</li><li>Close the moulds so the sheet is trapped securely between them.</li><li>Apply vacuum and pressure so the plastic is forced accurately into the mould details.</li><li>Keep the sheet held in the mould while it cools and solidifies.</li><li>Open the mould, remove the component and trim it, often using die cutting for accuracy.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> high-quality packaging, appliance panels, food containers and automotive interior components.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> deeper forms and sharper detail than simple vacuum forming; logos and textures reproduce more clearly; suitable for batch and mass production.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> more complex and costly matched tooling; uneven heating can still cause distortion or uneven wall thickness.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> inspect clarity of logos/textures, depth accuracy, trimming accuracy and wall-thickness consistency; reject distortion or uneven thickness caused by uneven heating.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> burns from heated sheet and moulds; crush risk from closing moulds; sharp edges after trimming/die cutting; machine guarding and PPE are essential.</p></div></section>

<section class="note-panel"><h2>Compression moulding</h2><div class="study-card"><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Heat and pressure shape a precisely measured charge of material inside a closed heated mould. Accurate charge mass is important because too much creates flash and too little causes incomplete moulding.</p><h3>Step by step</h3><ol><li>Clean the mould, apply release agent and preheat it to the correct temperature.</li><li>Accurately weigh a pre-measured slug/charge of material.</li><li>Place the charge into the heated negative mould cavity.</li><li>Close the positive mould using a press so heat and pressure force the material to spread and fill the cavity.</li><li>Maintain pressure while the material flows, cures and cools sufficiently.</li><li>Open the mould and eject the component.</li><li>Trim any flash and inspect the finished moulding.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> electrical components, heat-resistant housings and rubber/thermoset products.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> produces strong, dense components with consistent thickness; repeatable for batch and larger-scale production.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> moulding/curing cycle takes time; excess flash may require trimming; charge mass must be controlled accurately.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> check charge weight, full cavity filling, voids, short moulds, flash, surface defects, thickness and dimensional accuracy; verify full curing where thermosets are used.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> high heat and pressure; crushing risk from the press; fumes from heated polymers; use guarding, ventilation and heat-resistant gloves as required.</p></div></section>

<section class="note-panel"><h2>Lamination / hand lay-up composites</h2><div class="study-card"><p><strong>What it is:</strong> A manual composite process used to build large or complex GRP/CFRP shapes by layering reinforcement and resin in a negative mould.</p><h3>Step by step</h3><ol><li>Prepare an accurate negative mould, clean it and coat it with a release agent such as silicone.</li><li>Apply a pigmented gel coat evenly to create the finished outer surface and allow it to become tacky.</li><li>Lay the fibre reinforcement into the mould.</li><li>Apply resin in the correct resin/catalyst ratio and work it thoroughly into the fibres.</li><li>Use rollers to spread the resin and remove trapped air bubbles; add extra roving/reinforcement where additional support is required.</li><li>Build up layers to the required thickness and leave the laminate to cure fully, allowing for shrinkage.</li><li>Remove the cured component from the mould, trim the edges and complete post-production finishing.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> boat hulls, car body panels, kayaks, slides, storage tanks and other large composite structures.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> low setup cost and relatively simple equipment; suitable for very large or complex shapes; pigment can be incorporated into the gel coat.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> labour intensive and time-consuming; mould making and curing take time; trapped air, dry fibres or incorrect resin ratio can create weak points and delamination.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> check resin-to-catalyst ratio, even resin coverage, correct reinforcement, air bubbles, dry fibres, voids, delamination and thickness; inspect the gel-coat finish and use tap testing where appropriate to detect voids.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> toxic resin fumes; skin/eye irritation; glass/carbon-fibre dust during trimming; gloves, goggles, suitable respiratory protection and good ventilation/extraction are required.</p></div></section>

<section class="note-panel"><h2>Line bending</h2><div class="study-card"><p><strong>What it is:</strong> A strip heater softens a narrow line in a thermoplastic sheet so a controlled straight bend can be formed around a jig/former.</p><h3>Step by step</h3><ol><li>Cut the thermoplastic sheet accurately to the required size.</li><li>Mark the bend line clearly.</li><li>Position the marked line directly above the strip heater/heated wire.</li><li>Heat until the plastic becomes soft along the bend line; thicker sheet may need turning so it heats evenly.</li><li>Remove the softened sheet and bend it around a jig or former to the required angle.</li><li>Hold it in position until the polymer cools and sets.</li><li>Inspect the bend and remove any minor surface marks if required.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> acrylic display stands, guards, brackets, shelves and signage.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> low-cost equipment; accurate straight bends; suitable for one-off and small-batch production.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> only forms local straight bends; overheating can cause bubbling/burning; thick sheets may heat unevenly.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> check bend-angle accuracy, straightness, surface clarity and absence of bubbles/burn marks; make sure the bend occurs exactly on the marked line and the jig holds the required angle.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> burns from the heating element and hot polymer; exposed hot-wire and fire risks if overheated; supervision and appropriate PPE required.</p></div></section>

<section class="note-panel"><h2>Injection moulding</h2><div class="study-card"><p><strong>What it is:</strong> A high-pressure mass-production process in which molten thermoplastic is injected into a closed metal mould. It can produce complex, detailed components with excellent repeatability and surface finish.</p><h3>Step by step</h3><ol><li>Feed plastic granules into the hopper.</li><li>A rotating Archimedes screw carries the granules through a heated barrel, where heater bands and friction melt the polymer.</li><li>The screw retracts as molten polymer builds in front of it, measuring a precise shot.</li><li>The two mould halves close and clamp shut under high force.</li><li>The screw/ram moves forward and injects the molten polymer at high pressure through the nozzle, sprue, runners and gates into the mould cavity.</li><li>Hold pressure while the cavity fills and the polymer begins to cool, helping compensate for shrinkage.</li><li>Once solid, the mould opens and ejector pins release the component; the sprue/runner or any flash is removed if necessary.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> bottle caps, phone cases, toys, clips, medical components, electrical housings and detailed plastic parts.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> very fast and repeatable at high volume; low unit cost in mass production; excellent detail and surface finish; features such as clips, ribs and living hinges can be moulded in; little finishing is normally needed.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> very high mould/tooling and setup cost; economical mainly at high volumes; incorrect settings can repeatedly produce defective parts.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> monitor melt temperature, injection/holding pressure and cycle time; inspect for flash, short shots, sink marks, warping and surface defects; check part weight and dimensions for repeatability.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> extremely high temperatures and pressures; hot nozzle/molten polymer; crushing hazards at the mould; hydraulic machinery; strict guarding, interlocks and trained operators required.</p></div></section>

<section class="note-panel"><h2>Extrusion</h2><div class="study-card"><p><strong>What it is:</strong> A continuous process used to manufacture long polymer products with the same cross-section throughout their length.</p><h3>Step by step</h3><ol><li>Feed plastic granules into a hopper.</li><li>A motor turns an Archimedes screw inside a heated barrel.</li><li>The rotating screw carries the granules forward while heat and friction melt the polymer.</li><li>The screw forces the molten polymer continuously through a shaped die, creating the required constant cross-section.</li><li>The hot extrusion passes through air or water cooling so it solidifies without excessive distortion.</li><li>The finished extrusion is pulled through continuously and either cut to length or wound onto reels, depending on the product.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> pipes, tubing, hose pipes, window frames/edging, solid rods and other continuous plastic profiles.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> efficient continuous process; low unit cost at high volumes; very long lengths can be produced and length can be changed without changing the die.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> limited to products with a constant cross-section; dies are expensive; poor cooling can cause warping or dimensional variation.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> continuously measure cross-section dimensions; inspect surface quality; monitor barrel/die temperature and material flow; control cooling rate to prevent warping or distortion.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> burns from molten polymer and the hot die; entanglement with moving machinery; cutting hazards; guards and emergency stops required.</p></div></section>

<section class="note-panel"><h2>Blow moulding</h2><div class="study-card"><p><strong>What it is:</strong> A process for producing seamless hollow plastic products by inflating a heated hollow tube called a <strong>parison</strong> inside a two-part mould.</p><h3>Step by step</h3><ol><li>Form a hollow tube of heated thermoplastic called a parison.</li><li>Position the parison between the open halves of the mould.</li><li>Close and clamp the mould around the parison, sealing it.</li><li>Blow compressed air into the parison at pressure so it expands until it contacts the mould walls.</li><li>Keep the product in the mould while the polymer cools and solidifies.</li><li>Open the mould and remove the hollow product.</li><li>Trim excess material/flash and inspect the neck, seams and walls.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> shampoo and detergent bottles, other containers and fuel tanks.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> efficient for mass production; produces hollow, airtight/watertight shapes with no join along the main body; minimal finishing.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> high tooling/setup costs; wall thickness can vary if the parison or process is poorly controlled; trimming may be required.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> check wall and base thickness; inspect neck and thread accuracy; carry out leak testing for airtight/watertight integrity; inspect weak seams and incomplete forming.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> hot polymer and moulds; high-pressure compressed air; moving mould parts; machine guarding essential.</p></div></section>

<section class="note-panel"><h2>Rotational moulding</h2><div class="study-card"><p><strong>What it is:</strong> A low-pressure process used for large seamless hollow products. A measured quantity of polymer powder melts and coats the inside of a mould while the mould rotates on two axes.</p><h3>Step by step</h3><ol><li>Measure the required quantity of polymer powder/granules and place it inside the mould.</li><li>Seal the mould.</li><li>Heat the mould in an oven while it rotates slowly on two axes.</li><li>The polymer melts and repeatedly coats the internal mould surface, building an even hollow wall.</li><li>Move the mould to the cooling stage and continue rotating so the material does not pool or sag while solidifying.</li><li>Once cooled, stop the rotation, open the mould and remove the seamless hollow component.</li><li>Trim and inspect the finished product.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> water tanks, wheelie bins, kayaks, traffic/road products and playground equipment.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> seamless hollow forms; can produce large and complex/double-wall shapes; relatively lower tooling costs than injection or blow moulding; low internal stress.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> long heating and cooling cycles; limited range of suitable materials; wall quality depends on accurate charge, heating and rotation.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> check powder quantity, heating and biaxial rotation; inspect wall-thickness uniformity, incomplete fusion, porosity and pooling; check dimensions after cooling and trimming.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> hot moulds and ovens; rotating machinery; manual-handling risk from large moulds/products; heat-resistant gloves and lifting aids where required.</p></div></section>

<section class="note-panel"><h2>Calendaring</h2><div class="study-card"><p><strong>What it is:</strong> A continuous process for producing polymer sheet and film with controlled thickness by passing softened material through a series of rollers.</p><h3>Step by step</h3><ol><li>Heat/blend the polymer until it reaches a soft, putty-like workable state; material may also be supplied from an extrusion stage.</li><li>Feed the softened polymer into a series of heated calendaring rollers.</li><li>Pass it through progressively smaller roller gaps so the material is spread and reduced to the required thickness.</li><li>Where required, pass the sheet through an embossing roller to add a surface texture or pattern.</li><li>Pass the sheet through spaced chill rollers to cool it while maintaining the required thickness and surface.</li><li>Trim the edges and wind the finished sheet/film onto rolls or cut it into sheets.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> PVC sheet, vinyl flooring and thin plastic films.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> smooth, uniform sheet/film; good thickness control; efficient continuous mass production.</p><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> high setup cost; large equipment/space requirement; not economical for short production runs.</p><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> measure thickness across the sheet; inspect surface smoothness and embossing quality; monitor roller temperature, roller gap and alignment to prevent thickness/surface defects.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> crush and entanglement hazards at rollers; burns from heated rollers; fumes; strict guarding and emergency-stop systems required.</p></div></section>
