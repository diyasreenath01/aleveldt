<section class="note-panel"><h2>Polymer Processes</h2><div class="note-intro"><p>Each process is explained through its key stages, suitable products, production advantages, limitations and quality checks.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Vacuum forming</h2><div class="study-card"><p><strong>What it is:</strong> A heated thermoplastic sheet is shaped over a mould/former by removing the air underneath it.</p><h3>Step by step</h3><ol><li>Place the mould/former in the vacuum former and clamp the thermoplastic sheet securely in the frame above it.</li><li>Heat the sheet evenly with the radiant heater until the polymer softens and begins to sag uniformly.</li><li>Raise the mould into the softened sheet and switch on the vacuum pump so air is removed through the vent holes.</li><li>Atmospheric pressure pulls the sheet tightly over the mould; keep the vacuum applied while the polymer cools and sets to the required shape.</li><li>Release the vacuum, lower/remove the mould and take out the formed sheet.</li><li>Trim away the excess material and check the moulding for webbing, thinning, incomplete forming and dimensional accuracy.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Blister packaging, food trays, yoghurt pots and protective covers.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong></p><ol><li>Low-cost single-sided formers are economical for short and medium batches.</li><li>Heated sheet forms lightweight packaging quickly, with short cycle times.</li><li>The former can be changed or modified relatively cheaply for new tray shapes.</li></ol><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong></p><ol><li>Deep draws stretch the sheet thin at corners, weakening the product.</li><li>Excess sheet must be trimmed away, creating waste.</li><li>Only open-backed shapes are formed directly; closed bottles cannot be made this way.</li></ol><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> even heating, tapered sides/draft angles, rounded corners, smooth mould surface and adequate air holes; check full mould contact and wall thickness.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> burns from heaters/hot plastic, moving platen and sharp trimmed edges.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Pressure forming (a type of thermoforming)</h2><div class="study-card"><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Thermoforming is the broader family of processes in which a heated thermoplastic sheet is shaped over or into tooling. Vacuum forming uses reduced air pressure; pressure forming additionally applies positive air pressure for sharper detail. Matched-mould forming is another approach and is not required for all pressure forming.</p><h3>Step by step</h3><ol><li>Clamp the thermoplastic sheet securely and heat it evenly until it reaches its forming temperature.</li><li>Position the softened sheet over the mould; a pressure box and a suitable seal allow pressure to act on one side. Some specialist processes also use matched tooling.</li><li>Seal the forming area and ensure the sheet is correctly positioned above the tooling.</li><li>Apply positive air pressure, often assisted by vacuum beneath the sheet, so it conforms to the tool's details.</li><li>Maintain forming pressure as necessary while the shaped sheet cools and solidifies.</li><li>Release the pressure, remove the formed sheet, trim excess material and check dimensions and wall thickness.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> High-quality packaging, food containers and formed panels; for example, appliance panels and equipment covers.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong></p><ol><li>Positive air pressure reproduces sharper corners and surface textures than simple vacuum forming.</li><li>Logos and detailed features can be moulded directly, reducing secondary finishing.</li><li>Tooling can cost less than injection moulding for medium production runs.</li></ol><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong></p><ol><li>Pressure-rated equipment and tooling cost more than simple vacuum forming.</li><li>Deep shapes can have uneven wall thickness as the sheet stretches.</li><li>Excess sheet needs trimming, creating waste and extra processing.</li></ol><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> clarity of detail, depth, trimming accuracy, even heating and wall-thickness consistency.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> burns from heated sheet/moulds and crush risk from closing moulds.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Compression moulding</h2><div class="study-card"><p><strong>What it is:</strong> A measured charge of material is shaped inside a heated mould using heat and pressure.</p><h3>Step by step</h3><ol><li>Clean and preheat the mould, then accurately measure the required charge of moulding material.</li><li>Place the charge into the heated negative mould cavity.</li><li>Close the positive mould using the press so heat and pressure force the material to flow throughout the cavity.</li><li>Maintain the pressure while the material cures and takes the mould shape.</li><li>Open the mould and remove/eject the component once it has set sufficiently.</li><li>Trim any flash and inspect the component for complete filling, voids, surface defects and dimensional accuracy.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Electrical components and thermoset/rubber mouldings; for example, electrical switches and melamine tableware.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong></p><ol><li>Heated moulds produce durable thermoset components that retain shape at elevated temperatures.</li><li>Fibre-filled moulding compounds can provide good strength and stiffness.</li><li>Reusable moulds produce consistent parts over repeated production runs.</li></ol><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong></p><ol><li>Curing inside the mould lengthens each cycle.</li><li>Excess flash may need trimming, adding finishing time.</li><li>Incorrect charge size or pressure can cause incomplete filling or defects.</li></ol><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> charge weight, full cavity filling, pressure, cure, voids, flash and dimensions.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> hot moulds, high pressure, crushing and fumes.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Lamination / hand lay-up composites</h2><div class="study-card"><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Resin and fibre reinforcement are built up in a mould to create a GRP/CFRP composite component.</p><h3>Step by step</h3><ol><li>Prepare the negative mould, clean it and apply a release agent so the finished component can be removed.</li><li>Apply the pigmented gel coat evenly to create the outer surface and allow it to become tacky.</li><li>Lay the fibre reinforcement into the mould and apply resin using the correct resin/catalyst ratio.</li><li>Use rollers to spread the resin through the fibres and remove trapped air, adding further layers or roving where extra reinforcement is required.</li><li>Leave the laminate to cure fully to the required thickness.</li><li>Remove the cured component from the mould, trim it accurately and check for bubbles, dry fibres, voids or delamination.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Boat hulls, car body panels, kayaks and large composite structures; for example, boat hulls and fibreglass panels.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong></p><ol><li>Fibres can be oriented along load directions to improve strength-to-weight ratio.</li><li>Large curved shells can be made using relatively simple moulds.</li><li>Multiple layers allow local reinforcement where extra strength is needed.</li></ol><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong></p><ol><li>Manual lay-up is labour intensive and slow for mass production.</li><li>Trapped air or poor resin wet-out can create weak areas.</li><li>Resin fumes and skin contact require ventilation and protective equipment.</li></ol><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> resin/catalyst ratio, even resin coverage, air removal, reinforcement, cure and laminate thickness.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> resin fumes and skin/eye irritation; glass/carbon-fibre dust during trimming; use ventilation/extraction and PPE.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Line bending</h2><div class="study-card"><p><strong>What it is:</strong> A strip heater softens a narrow line in thermoplastic sheet so it can be bent accurately around a jig/former.</p><h3>Step by step</h3><ol><li>Cut the thermoplastic sheet to size, mark the bend line and position it directly above the strip heater.</li><li>Heat the marked line evenly until the polymer becomes soft; thicker material may need turning to heat both sides.</li><li>Remove the softened sheet and bend it around the prepared jig/former to the required angle.</li><li>Hold the sheet against the jig while it cools so it sets and does not spring back.</li><li>Remove it from the jig and inspect the angle, dimensions and surface for bubbling, burning or cracking.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Acrylic display stands, guards, brackets and signage; for example, display stands and signs.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong></p><ol><li>A strip heater forms accurate straight folds without an expensive mould.</li><li>A jig helps repeat the same angle for batches of acrylic displays.</li><li>Only a narrow strip is heated, leaving most of the sheet unchanged.</li></ol><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong></p><ol><li>It cannot form complex compound curves or enclosed hollow shapes.</li><li>Overheating may cause bubbles, scorching or discolouration.</li><li>The softened sheet must be held until cool or the angle may change.</li></ol><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> bend-line position, even heating, jig angle and absence of bubbles/burn marks.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> burns from the heating element and hot polymer.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Injection moulding</h2><div class="study-card"><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Molten thermoplastic is injected under high pressure into a closed metal mould.</p><h3>Step by step</h3><ol><li>Feed thermoplastic granules through the hopper into the heated barrel, where the rotating Archimedes screw moves them forward and the material melts.</li><li>As molten polymer collects in front of the screw, the screw winds back so a measured amount of material is prepared for the moulding cycle.</li><li>Close and clamp the mould halves, then move the screw forward like a ram to force the molten polymer through the nozzle and into the mould cavity.</li><li>Maintain pressure while the cavity fills, then allow the polymer to cool and solidify inside the mould.</li><li>Open the mould and use ejector pins to release the finished component.</li><li>Remove the sprue/flash where required and check for short shots, flash, sink marks, warping and incorrect dimensions.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Bottle caps, casings, toys, clips and detailed plastic components; for example, bottle caps and electronic housings.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong></p><ol><li>A reusable precision mould produces thousands of identical detailed components.</li><li>Fast automated cycles and multi-cavity tooling lower unit cost at high volumes.</li><li>Complex features such as clips and bosses can be moulded in, reducing assembly.</li></ol><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong></p><ol><li>Precision mould manufacture has a high initial cost.</li><li>Uneven cooling can cause sink marks or warping.</li><li>Changing the design may require costly mould modification or replacement.</li></ol><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> melt temperature, injection pressure, complete filling, shrinkage, flash, short shots and dimensions.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> high temperature and pressure, hot nozzle/molten polymer and crushing at the mould.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Extrusion</h2><div class="study-card"><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Molten thermoplastic is forced continuously through a shaped die to produce a constant cross-section.</p><h3>Step by step</h3><ol><li>Feed plastic granules through the hopper into the heated barrel.</li><li>A motor turns the Archimedes screw, moving the granules forward while heat and friction melt the polymer.</li><li>The screw forces the molten polymer continuously through a shaped die which forms the required cross-section.</li><li>The hot extrusion passes through a cooling stage using air or water so it solidifies without excessive distortion.</li><li>The finished extrusion is pulled through continuously and cut to the required length or wound onto reels.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Solid rods, hose pipes, pipes, window profiles and other continuous sections; for example, pipes and cable insulation.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong></p><ol><li>Continuous processing efficiently produces long lengths of tubing or profiles.</li><li>A shaped die provides a consistent cross-section and reduces later machining.</li><li>Profiles can be cut to different lengths without changing the die.</li></ol><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong></p><ol><li>Cross-section must remain constant along the length.</li><li>Specialist dies and extrusion equipment require investment.</li><li>Incorrect cooling or pulling speed can distort the profile.</li></ol><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> barrel/die temperature, material flow, cross-section dimensions and cooling.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> hot die/molten polymer, moving machinery and cutting hazards.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Blow moulding</h2><div class="study-card"><p><strong>What it is:</strong> A heated hollow tube called a parison is inflated inside a two-part mould to produce a hollow plastic product.</p><h3>Step by step</h3><ol><li>Form the heated thermoplastic parison and position it between the open halves of the mould.</li><li>Close and clamp the mould around the parison so it is sealed in position.</li><li>Force compressed air into the parison so it expands and presses against the inside surfaces of the mould.</li><li>Keep the product in the mould while the polymer cools and solidifies to the required hollow shape.</li><li>Open the mould and remove the component.</li><li>Trim excess material and inspect the walls, neck/seams and product for leaks.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Shampoo and detergent bottles and other hollow containers; for example, detergent bottles and fuel tanks.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong></p><ol><li>Inflating a parison or preform creates a hollow bottle without joining separate halves.</li><li>Reusable moulds and automated cycles make large bottle runs economical.</li><li>Thin-walled containers are lightweight, reducing material use and transport mass.</li></ol><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong></p><ol><li>Plastic may stretch unevenly, causing thin weak walls at corners.</li><li>Flash or pinch-off waste may need trimming, especially in extrusion blow moulding.</li><li>Limited fine detail and possible leaks at pinch-off areas require quality checks.</li></ol><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> wall/base thickness, blow pressure, sealing, neck accuracy and leak testing.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> hot polymer/moulds, compressed air and moving mould parts.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Rotational moulding</h2><div class="study-card"><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Polymer powder melts and coats the inside of a heated mould while the mould rotates on two axes.</p><h3>Step by step</h3><ol><li>Measure the required quantity of polymer powder/granules, place it inside the mould and seal the mould.</li><li>Heat the mould while it rotates slowly on two axes so the polymer begins to melt.</li><li>Continue rotating as the molten polymer repeatedly coats the inside surfaces and builds the hollow wall.</li><li>Move the mould to the cooling stage but continue rotation so the material does not pool or sag while solidifying.</li><li>Once cooled, stop the rotation, open the mould and remove the seamless hollow component.</li><li>Trim and inspect wall-thickness uniformity, incomplete fusion, porosity and dimensional accuracy.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> Water tanks, wheelie bins, kayaks, traffic products and playground equipment; for example, water tanks and kayaks.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong></p><ol><li>Produces large hollow products without welded seams.</li><li>Low-pressure moulds are relatively inexpensive for large components.</li><li>Wall thickness can be increased by using more polymer powder.</li></ol><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong></p><ol><li>Long heating and cooling cycles limit production speed.</li><li>Wall thickness and small details are less precise than injection moulding.</li><li>Poor heating can cause incomplete fusion or internal voids.</li></ol><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> powder quantity, heating, biaxial rotation, cooling and wall thickness.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> hot moulds/ovens, rotating machinery and manual-handling risks.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Calendaring</h2><div class="study-card"><p><strong>What it is:</strong> Softened polymer is passed through a series of rollers to produce continuous sheet or film of controlled thickness.</p><h3>Step by step</h3><ol><li>Heat the polymer until it becomes soft and workable, then feed it into the calendaring rollers.</li><li>Pass the material through a series of heated rollers with progressively smaller gaps so its thickness is reduced evenly.</li><li>Where required, pass the sheet through an embossing roller to add a texture or pattern.</li><li>Pass the formed sheet through chill rollers so it cools while maintaining the required thickness and surface.</li><li>Trim the sheet edges and wind the finished material onto rolls or cut it into sheets.</li></ol><p><strong>Common uses:</strong> PVC sheets, vinyl flooring and plastic films; for example, pvc flooring and films.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong></p><ol><li>Roller gaps produce continuous film or sheet of consistent thickness.</li><li>Embossed rollers can add surface texture during manufacture.</li><li>Continuous output is efficient for large volumes of flooring or flexible sheet.</li></ol><p><strong>Disadvantages:</strong></p><ol><li>Large heated roller machinery is expensive.</li><li>The process is limited mainly to sheet and film products.</li><li>Poor roller temperature or gap control causes uneven thickness or surface defects.</li></ol><p><strong>Quality control:</strong> sheet thickness, surface/embossing quality, roller temperature, gap and alignment.</p><p><strong>Safety:</strong> crushing/entanglement at rollers, burns, fumes and need for guards/emergency stops.</p></div></section>
