<section class="note-panel"><h2>Wood Processes</h2></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Steam Bending</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>Steam bending reshapes solid timber using <strong>heat and moisture</strong> to soften the lignin that bonds wood fibres.</p>
<p>Timber is placed in a sealed steam box where it absorbs heat and moisture, making the fibres flexible.<br> </p>
<p>The softened wood is bent immediately around a former or jig and clamped securely.<br> </p>
<p>As the wood cools and dries, it retains the new shape.<br> </p>
<p>Straight-grained, seasoned (not kiln-dried) hardwoods such as oak and ash are most suitable.<br> </p>
<p><strong>Advantages</strong></p>
<p>Fibres remain continuous, maintaining strength.<br> </p>
<p>Produces smooth, attractive curves.<br> </p>
<p>No material removal.<br> </p>
<p>Suitable for thick sections.<br> </p>
<p><strong>Disadvantages</strong></p>
<p>Time consuming and requires specialist setup.<br> </p>
<p>Risk of splitting on the outer radius.<br> </p>
<p>Spring-back can occur if not clamped long enough.<br> </p>
<p>Limited to suitable timber species.<br> </p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Laminating</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>Laminating creates curved or strong components by <strong>bonding thin layers of wood together</strong>.</p>
<p>Thin veneers or strips are glued together with the grain aligned, then bent over a former.<br> </p>
<p>Pressure is applied using clamps or a vacuum press until the adhesive cures.<br> </p>
<p>The layered structure increases strength and reduces movement.<br> </p>
<p>Once cured, excess material is trimmed and the component is finished.<br> </p>
<p><strong>Advantages</strong></p>
<p>Very strong and dimensionally stable.<br> </p>
<p>Allows complex, repeatable curved forms.<br> </p>
<p>High-quality aesthetic finish.<br> </p>
<p>Suitable for batch production.<br> </p>
<p><strong>Disadvantages</strong></p>
<p>Time consuming.<br> </p>
<p>Requires accurate formers and clamping.<br> </p>
<p>Risk of delamination if adhesive fails.<br> </p>
<p>Glue lines may be visible.<br> </p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>WASTING / MACHINING PROCESSES</h2></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Turning (Lathe Work)</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>Turning shapes timber by rotating it against hand tools on a lathe.</p>
<p>The workpiece is secured between centres, on a faceplate, or in a chuck depending on the form required.<br> </p>
<p>Cutting tools such as gouges and chisels remove material to create cylindrical or symmetrical forms.<br> </p>
<p>Sanding and finishing are often carried out while the workpiece is rotating.<br> </p>
<p>Common products include chair legs, spindles, bowls and handles.<br> </p>
<p><strong>Advantages</strong></p>
<p>Produces smooth, symmetrical components.<br> </p>
<p>Fast once set up.<br> </p>
<p>High-quality surface finish.<br> </p>
<p><strong>Disadvantages</strong></p>
<p>Limited to rotational forms.<br> </p>
<p>Requires skill and training.<br> </p>
<p>Safety risks if work is poorly secured.<br> </p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Routing</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>Routing removes material using a <strong>high-speed rotating cutter</strong>.</p>
<p>A router is guided along the workpiece manually or using a jig to cut grooves, rebates or decorative edges.<br> </p>
<p>The depth of cut and cutter profile determine the final shape.<br> </p>
<p>Can be done using manual routers or CNC routers for increased accuracy.<br> </p>
<p><strong>Advantages</strong></p>
<p>Accurate and repeatable.<br> </p>
<p>Suitable for complex edge profiles.<br> </p>
<p>Can be automated using CNC.<br> </p>
<p><strong>Disadvantages</strong></p>
<p>Noisy and dusty.<br> </p>
<p>Risk of tear-out.<br> </p>
<p>Requires careful control.<br> </p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>CNC Routing / Milling</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>CNC machines automate machining using <strong>CAD/CAM systems</strong>.</p>
<p>A 2D or 3D CAD model is converted into a CNC toolpath.<br> </p>
<p>The material is fixed to the machine bed and the program controls cutter movement.<br> </p>
<p>CNC is commonly used for panels, joints, decorative features and batch production.<br> </p>
<p><strong>Advantages</strong></p>
<p>Very high accuracy and repeatability.<br> </p>
<p>Complex shapes possible.<br> </p>
<p>Ideal for batch and mass production.<br> </p>
<p><strong>Disadvantages</strong></p>
<p>Expensive equipment.<br> </p>
<p>Skilled programming required.<br> </p>
<p>Errors are repeated if the file is incorrect.<br> </p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>ADDITION / FABRICATION PROCESSES</h2></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Traditional Wood Joints</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>Traditional joints rely on <strong>surface area and glue strength</strong>.</p>
<p>The greater the gluing area, the stronger the joint.<br> </p>
<p>Used mainly with solid timber.<br> </p>
<p>Examples include dovetail, mortise and tenon, housing and finger joints.<br> </p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>KNOCK-DOWN (KD) FITTINGS</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>KD fittings are <strong>temporary mechanical joints</strong> used primarily in <strong>flat-pack furniture</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Why KD fittings are used</h3>
<p>Sheet materials such as MDF and chipboard are weak in tension and unsuitable for traditional joints.<br> </p>
<p>Products must be transported flat to reduce storage and shipping costs.<br> </p>
<p>Assembly must be possible by the consumer using basic tools.<br> </p>
<p>Standardised fittings allow mass production and interchangeability.<br> </p></div></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Cam-lock connectors</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>Cam-lock connectors consist of a <strong>metal cam and a steel dowel</strong>.</p>
<p>The dowel is screwed into one panel and inserted into a hole in the second panel.<br> </p>
<p>A circular cam is inserted into a pre-drilled recess.<br> </p>
<p>When turned with a screwdriver, the cam pulls the dowel tight, locking the panels together.<br> </p>
<p>Widely used in flat-pack furniture such as bookcases and wardrobes.<br> </p>
<p><strong>Advantages</strong></p>
<p>Quick assembly.<br> </p>
<p>Hidden fixing improves appearance.<br> </p>
<p>Strong for panel-based construction.<br> </p>
<p><strong>Disadvantages</strong></p>
<p>Precision drilling required.<br> </p>
<p>Can loosen with repeated assembly.<br> </p>
<p>Limited resistance to racking forces.<br> </p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Barrel nut and bolt</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>A barrel nut is a cylindrical nut inserted into a cross-drilled hole.</p>
<p>A bolt passes through one panel and screws into the barrel nut.<br> </p>
<p>Tightening pulls the two components together at right angles.<br> </p>
<p>Commonly used in bed frames and furniture requiring repeated dismantling.<br> </p>
<p><strong>Advantages</strong></p>
<p>Strong mechanical joint.<br> </p>
<p>Can be dismantled and reassembled.</p>
<p>Suitable for thicker panels.<br> </p>
<p><strong>Disadvantages</strong></p>
<p>Visible fixings.<br> </p>
<p>Requires accurate drilling.<br> </p>
<p>Can loosen over time.<br> </p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Modesty blocks</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>Modesty blocks are <strong>small moulded plastic blocks</strong> fixed inside corners.</p>
<p>Screws pass through the block into both panels.</p>
<p>Spread the load and prevent screw pull-out in chipboard.</p>
<p>Common in cabinets and kitchen units.<br> </p>
<p><strong>Advantages</strong></p>
<p>Simple and cheap.</p>
<p>Reduces damage to panels.</p>
<p>Easy to assemble.<br> </p>
<p><strong>Disadvantages</strong></p>
<p>Visible internally.</p>
<p>Limited structural strength.</p>
<p>Lower quality feel.<br> </p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Screws</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>Screws provide <strong>direct mechanical fixing</strong>.</p>
<p>Pilot holes prevent splitting.</p>
<p>Clearance holes allow panels to pull together.</p>
<p>Countersinking allows flush finishes.<br> </p>
<p><strong>Advantages</strong></p>
<p>Immediate strength.</p>
<p>Easy to assemble and disassemble.<br> </p>
<p><strong>Disadvantages</strong></p>
<p>Visible fixings.</p>
<p>Risk of splitting if poorly prepared</p>

<section class="note-panel"><h2>Addition / fabrication processes</h2>
<div class="note-grid"><div class="note-group-title"><h3>Traditional wood jointing</h3></div>
<div class="study-card"><h4>Joint strength increases with greater glued contact area</h4>
<p>Joints are selected based on <strong>strength requirements, appearance, and production method<br> </strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h4>Common joints and uses</h4>
<p><strong>Dovetail joint</strong> – drawers; strong in multiple directions<br> </p>
<p><strong>Comb / finger joint</strong> – box construction; large glue area<br> </p>
<p><strong>Housing joint</strong> – framework, cabinets, shelving<br> </p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h4>Half-lap joint – simple frames or boxes</h4></div>
<div class="study-card"><h4>Dowel joint – flat-pack furniture (bookcases, wardrobes)</h4>
<p><strong>Mortise and tenon joint</strong> – frame construction (tables, chairs); high strength<br> </p></div></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Component jointing (KD fittings)</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p><strong>Knock-down (KD) fittings</strong></p>
<p>Used in <strong>flat-pack furniture<br> </strong></p>
<p>Require <strong>few simple tools<br> </strong></p>
<p><strong>Standardised and interchangeable</strong>, allowing mass production and easy assembly<br> </p></div>
<div class="note-grid"><div class="note-group-title"><h3>Types of KD fittings</h3></div>
<div class="study-card"><h4>Modesty blocks</h4>
<p>Small rigid <strong>polymer blocks<br> </strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h4>Moulded holes take screws</h4>
<p>Used to join panels in cupboards and storage units<br> </p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h4>Barrel nuts and bolts</h4></div>
<div class="study-card"><h4>Use a cross-dowel inserted into one component</h4>
<p>Bolt passes through the second component and tightens into the cross-dowel<br> </p>
<p>Typically tightened with an <strong>Allen key<br> </strong></p>
<p>Commonly used in <strong>bed frames<br> </strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h4>Cam-lock connectors</h4></div>
<div class="study-card"><h4>Metal dowel screws into one component</h4></div>
<div class="study-card"><h4>Cam (disk) fits into a pre-drilled hole</h4></div>
<div class="study-card"><h4>Rotating the cam pulls components tightly together</h4>
<p>Widely used in <strong>flat-pack furniture</strong> (e.g. bookcases)<br> </p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h4>Wood screws</h4>
<p>Used when thread is needed only in the lower component<br> </p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h4>Clearance hole in top piece</h4>
<p><strong>Pilot hole</strong> (smaller than thread) in bottom piece for grip<br> </p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h4>Coach bolts</h4></div>
<div class="study-card"><h4>Domed head with square section beneath</h4>
<p>Square section bites into timber to prevent rotation<br> </p>
<p>Used for <strong>door furniture</strong> and <strong>street furniture<br> </strong></p></div></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Forming processes</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>Uses <strong>heat and steam</strong> to soften wood fibres<br> </p></div>
<div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Lamination</h3>
<p>Bonds layers of <strong>veneers or thin boards</strong> (e.g. 3 mm plywood)<br> </p>
<p>Layers are glued and bent over a <strong>former<br> </strong></p>
<p>Once dry, layers form a <strong>strong, shaped component<br> </strong></p>
<p>Held during curing using <strong>clamps or vacuum bags<br> </strong></p></div>
<div class="note-group-title"><h3>Steam bending</h3></div>
<div class="study-card"><h4>Wood is placed in a steam box</h4></div>
<div class="study-card"><h4>Bent over a former and clamped until dry</h4></div>
<div class="study-card"><h4>Quicker and less wasteful than lamination</h4>
<p>Lamination requires glue curing time and trimming<br> </p></div></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Machine processes</h2>
<div class="note-grid"><div class="study-card"><h3>Turning</h3>
<p>Wood is machined on a <strong>lathe</strong>.</p>
<p><strong>Methods</strong></p>
<p><strong>Turning between centres</strong> – spindles (chair and table legs)<br> </p>
<p><strong>Turning on a faceplate</strong> – bowls, domes<br> </p>
<p><strong>Turning in a chuck</strong> – gripping work to machine ends or internal surfaces<br> </p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Milling (A-level only)</h3>
<p>Used for <strong>small, basic machining tasks<br> </strong></p>
<p>Suitable for rough prototypes or small holes/channels<br> </p>
<p><strong>Slower process</strong> than CNC routing<br> </p>
<p>Smaller working area than CNC routers<br> </p>
<p>Can be <strong>manual or CNC<br> </strong></p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Routing</h3>
<p>Used to cut <strong>slots, holes, and decorative mouldings<br> </strong></p>
<p>Common on edges (e.g. table tops)<br> </p>
<p>Can be <strong>manual (plunge router)</strong> or <strong>CNC<br> </strong></p></div></div></section>
