## Woods

Wood selection depends on **strength, toughness, hardness, density, grain, stability, appearance, workability, durability, cost and source**. Designers also need to understand how natural timber behaves differently from manufactured boards.

### Natural timber

AQA expects familiarity with softwoods such as **pine, spruce, Douglas fir, redwood, cedar and larch**, and hardwoods such as **oak, ash, mahogany, teak, birch and beech**.

Softwood and hardwood describe botanical groups, not simply whether the timber feels soft or hard.

Natural timber is **anisotropic**: its properties vary with grain direction. Grain can give attractive appearance and good strength along the fibres, but moisture changes can cause shrinkage, swelling, warping or splitting.

### Manufactured boards

Important examples include **plywood, marine plywood, aeroply, flexible plywood, chipboard and MDF**. Manufactured boards make efficient use of wood material and are available in consistent sheet sizes.

- **Plywood:** cross-laminated veneers give strength and dimensional stability.
- **Marine plywood:** suitable where improved moisture resistance is needed.
- **Flexible plywood:** designed to bend around curves.
- **MDF:** smooth, uniform and easy to machine; useful for furniture and painted products.
- **Chipboard:** economical and widely used in flat-pack furniture, often with a decorative laminate.

### Design issues

Consider grain direction, knots and defects, moisture, surface finish, joining method and whether the timber needs protection from decay. Veneers and melamine-faced boards can give a decorative or durable surface without using solid premium timber throughout.

### Exam approach

Use **specific wood → property → product requirement → justification** rather than saying only that wood is 'strong' or 'looks nice'.
