## Woods

Wood selection depends on **strength, toughness, hardness, density, grain, stability, appearance, workability, durability, cost and source**. Designers also need to understand how natural timber behaves differently from manufactured boards.

### Structure and behaviour of wood

Wood fibres run mainly along the length of the tree or branch. This directional structure means natural timber is **anisotropic**: its strength and other properties are different along and across the grain.

Wood also responds to its environment. Changes in moisture content can cause it to **expand, shrink, warp or split**, so grain direction, seasoning and the conditions in which a product will be used matter when selecting and designing with timber.

### Natural timber

AQA expects familiarity with softwoods such as **pine, spruce, Douglas fir, redwood, cedar and larch**, and hardwoods such as **oak, ash, mahogany, teak, birch and beech**.

Softwood and hardwood are botanical classifications rather than simple descriptions of physical hardness. A designer should select a species by the properties needed for the product rather than assuming every hardwood is harder than every softwood.

### Manufactured boards

Important examples include **plywood, marine plywood, aeroply, flexible plywood, chipboard and MDF**.

- **Plywood:** veneers are bonded in layers with grain directions arranged to improve strength and dimensional stability.
- **Marine plywood:** uses construction and adhesive systems intended for demanding moisture exposure.
- **Flexible plywood:** thin construction allows it to form around curves.
- **MDF:** uniform, smooth and easy to machine, making it useful for furniture, models and painted products.
- **Chipboard:** economical sheet material commonly used for flat-pack furniture and often covered with a decorative surface.

Manufactured boards make efficient use of wood material and are supplied in predictable sheet sizes, which can make cutting and batch manufacture more efficient than working from irregular natural timber.

### Stock forms

The teaching material identifies several common ways wood is supplied:

- **rough sawn:** timber with rough surfaces after initial conversion/seasoning
- **PSE (planed square edge):** supplied with a planed accurate edge/face for further working
- **PAR (planed all round):** planed on its faces and edges to give accurate, smooth stock
- **mouldings:** pre-shaped sections such as skirting, frames and decorative profiles
- **veneers:** thin layers of wood bonded to another material to provide a real-wood surface
- **manufactured boards:** large processed sheets such as MDF, plywood and chipboard
- **natural timber sections:** solid timber cut from the tree and supplied in usable sections.

Choosing a stock form close to the final shape can reduce machining, waste and production time.

### Design considerations

Grain direction, knots and defects, moisture, surface finish, joining method and resistance to decay all affect suitability. Veneers and decorative-faced boards can provide the appearance of a premium timber without using solid high-value wood throughout the product.

A material choice should therefore connect the timber's characteristics to the application. For example, plywood is useful where a stable sheet is required because its cross-laminated construction reduces the strong directional behaviour found in a single piece of natural timber.
