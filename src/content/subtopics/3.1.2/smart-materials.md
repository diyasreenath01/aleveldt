## Smart materials

A **smart material** changes one or more of its properties in a controlled and useful way when exposed to an external stimulus. The change may be reversible when the stimulus is removed.

Useful stimuli include **temperature, light/UV, mechanical stress or pressure, electricity and moisture**. What matters in a product is the connection between the stimulus, the material's response and the function that response provides.

### Thermochromic pigments

Thermochromic pigments **change colour when their temperature changes**. The colour change can give the user immediate visual information without a separate electronic display.

**Applications:** fever/temperature strips, room thermometers, children's cutlery and crockery, warning indicators, novelty products and colour-changing clothing.

They are useful where an approximate visual indication is enough, but they should not automatically be treated as a substitute for a precise temperature sensor where accuracy is critical.

### Photochromic pigments

Photochromic materials change colour or darken in response to **light, particularly UV**, and return towards their original state when the stimulus reduces.

**Application:** photochromic spectacle lenses darken in bright outdoor conditions and become clearer again in lower UV conditions. The response allows one product to adapt to changing light without the user swapping lenses.

### Shape memory alloys — Nitinol

**Nitinol** is a nickel-titanium shape memory alloy. It can be set into a particular shape, deformed and then triggered by heat or electrical heating to return towards its programmed shape.

Useful characteristics include **shape recovery, superelastic behaviour, low mass and durability**. Teaching examples include spectacle frames and temperature-activated mechanisms in hot-water, heating, air-conditioning and alarm systems. Shape memory behaviour also has specialist medical and dental applications.

The material can simplify a mechanism because the material itself provides movement. However, it is more expensive and more complex to manufacture than many conventional materials, and its strength, activation temperature and response must suit the product.

### Piezoelectric materials

Piezoelectric materials generate an **electrical charge when mechanically stressed**. The effect can also operate in reverse, with an applied electrical signal producing a small physical movement or vibration.

**Applications:** pressure and impact sensors, buzzers, electronic ignition and vibration sensing. They are valuable where mechanical and electrical energy need to be converted without a conventional moving mechanism.

### Phosphorescent pigments

Phosphorescent materials absorb light energy and release it slowly afterwards, creating an **afterglow**.

**Applications:** emergency signs, watch faces, switches and wayfinding markings where visibility after the main light source has disappeared is useful.

### Electroluminescent materials

Electroluminescent wire or panels emit light when an electrical field/current is applied. Thin flexible forms can follow curved surfaces.

**Applications:** illuminated clothing, signs, vehicle interiors and product detailing. They can provide a lightweight continuous line of light where conventional lamps would be awkward.

### Choosing a smart material

A smart material is useful only when its response solves a genuine product requirement. For example, a thermochromic pigment is valuable when a colour change communicates temperature clearly, while Nitinol is useful where controlled shape recovery can replace or simplify a mechanical actuator. Cost, accuracy, response time, operating conditions and durability still need to be considered.
