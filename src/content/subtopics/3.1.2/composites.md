# Composites

A **composite** combines two or more different materials so the resulting material gains useful properties that the individual materials do not provide as effectively on their own. In fibre composites, the **reinforcement** commonly provides tensile strength and stiffness while the **matrix** binds the reinforcement, transfers loads, protects it and helps provide compressive strength and shape.

The suitability of a composite depends on what each constituent contributes, how the materials are arranged and the requirements of the product.

## Key composites

| Composite | What it is made from | Key properties / why it is useful | Typical applications |
|---|---|---|---|
| **CFRP – carbon fibre reinforced plastic** | Carbon fibres in a polymer resin matrix, commonly epoxy | Exceptional strength-to-weight ratio, stiff, high tensile strength, corrosion resistant and low thermal expansion; expensive and difficult to repair/recycle | Aircraft structures, racing cars, bicycles and sports equipment |
| **GRP – glass reinforced plastic** | Glass fibres or mat in polyester or epoxy resin | Lightweight, corrosion resistant, durable and capable of forming complex 3D shapes; difficult to recycle | Boat hulls, car body panels, tanks and large moulded shells |
| **Tungsten carbide** | Tungsten carbide ceramic particles bonded in a metallic binder such as cobalt | Extremely hard and wear resistant; retains a cutting edge but is relatively brittle | Cutting-tool tips, drill bits and wear-resistant tooling |
| **Aluminium composite board** | Thin aluminium skins bonded to a polymer core | Lightweight, rigid, corrosion resistant, smooth and suitable for large panels | Signs, cladding, display panels and transport applications |
| **Concrete** | Cement, sand and aggregate mixed with water | Very high compressive strength but relatively poor tensile strength | Foundations, blocks and structural elements mainly loaded in compression |
| **Reinforced concrete** | Concrete containing steel reinforcement | Concrete carries compression while steel provides tensile strength | Bridges, beams, floors and foundations |
| **Fibre cement** | Cement matrix reinforced with fibres, commonly cellulose | Tougher and less brittle than unreinforced cement; durable and weather resistant | Roofing and façade/cladding panels |
| **Glulam** | Timber laminations bonded with durable adhesive, with grain mainly parallel | Strong, stable and capable of forming long or curved structural sections | Beams, arches, bridges and large-span roofs |

## CFRP

Carbon fibres are extremely fine and can be grouped into threads and woven into fabric before being bonded with a resin matrix. The fibres provide very high tensile strength while the resin holds them in position, transfers loads and helps resist compression.

This produces an excellent **strength-to-weight ratio**. Low mass is valuable in aircraft because reducing mass can reduce energy and fuel use in service. It is also useful in performance products such as bicycles and sports equipment where high stiffness and strength are needed without excessive weight.

Fibre orientation matters. A composite is strongest and stiffest in the directions in which its reinforcing fibres are arranged, so designers can orient layers to resist the expected loads.

## GRP

GRP combines glass fibre reinforcement with a thermosetting resin such as polyester or epoxy. The glass improves tensile performance while the resin forms the matrix, holds the fibres in position and provides the moulded shape.

It is particularly useful for boat hulls and large shells because it is lightweight, corrosion resistant, resistant to water and can be laid into complex mould shapes. Limitations include labour and time during lay-up, possible voids or delamination, difficult repair and poor recyclability.

## Construction composites

**Concrete** is strong in compression but weak in tension. Adding steel reinforcement creates **reinforced concrete**, where concrete carries compressive loads while the steel carries tensile loads.

**Fibre cement** adds fibres to a cement matrix, improving toughness and allowing relatively thin, durable construction products.

**Glulam** is made from timber laminations bonded together with durable adhesive. Laminating allows large and curved structural sections to be manufactured and can distribute natural defects across multiple layers.

## Selecting a composite

A composite should be justified by the contribution of its different materials. For example, reinforced concrete is suitable for a structural beam because concrete performs well in compression while embedded steel provides the tensile strength that plain concrete lacks.

The advantages must also be balanced against manufacturing difficulty, cost, repair and end-of-life. A lightweight high-performance composite may improve performance during use but still be difficult to separate or recycle.
