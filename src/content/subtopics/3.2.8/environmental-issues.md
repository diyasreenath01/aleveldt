<section class="note-panel"><h2>Environmental Issues</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p><strong>Sustainable development</strong> means meeting the needs of the current generation without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs. Responsible designers consider environmental, social and economic effects throughout a product's life.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Sustainable materials and components</h3><p>Designers should aim to minimise environmental impact while still meeting the product's functional requirements. Important considerations include:</p><ul>
<li>conserving materials, for example by nesting components to reduce waste</li>
<li>reducing energy use during manufacture</li>
<li>selecting materials and components that make the product as sustainable and environmentally responsible as possible</li>
<li>considering the product's <strong>carbon footprint</strong></li>
<li>considering <strong>product miles</strong>, the lifetime distance travelled from production to use.</li>
</ul></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Carbon footprint</h3><p>A <strong>carbon footprint</strong> is the total amount of carbon dioxide released as a result of the activities associated with a person, organisation or product.</p><p>A <strong>primary carbon footprint</strong> concerns direct emissions, such as CO₂ produced by burning fossil fuels for transport or energy use. A <strong>secondary carbon footprint</strong> concerns indirect emissions associated with products and their supply chains.</p><p>Designers therefore need to consider more than factory emissions. Material extraction and processing, transport, use and end-of-life treatment can all affect the overall impact.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Economic, social and environmental factors</h3><p>Sustainable development involves more than environmental impact alone.</p><ul>
<li><strong>Economic:</strong> material cost, use of finite resources, sourcing, energy and processing costs, pollution-control costs and the cost of providing appropriate working conditions.</li>
<li><strong>Social:</strong> the effect of processing pollution on people's health and the working conditions of people involved in manufacture.</li>
<li><strong>Environmental:</strong> use of finite resources, energy source, pollution, recycling and what happens to materials at the end of the product's life.</li>
</ul></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Environmental impact of packaging</h3><p>Packaging must protect and preserve the product while avoiding unnecessary material. Designers and manufacturers can reduce impact by:</p><ul>
<li>making packaging lightweight</li>
<li>using recycled content</li>
<li>making packaging recyclable or reusable</li>
<li>using refills and concentrates</li>
<li>using the minimum practical amount of packaging material.</li>
</ul><p>Reducing packaging is only successful if the product remains adequately protected. Packaging that is too minimal can lead to damaged products and additional waste.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Circular economy: biological and technical nutrients</h3>
<p>A <strong>linear economy</strong> follows <strong>take → make → use → dispose</strong> (cradle-to-grave). A <strong>circular economy</strong> aims to design out waste and pollution, keep products and materials in use at their highest practical value, and regenerate natural systems. This is closely linked to <strong>cradle-to-cradle</strong> design, where materials are treated as resources for another cycle rather than waste.</p>
<p>The <strong>butterfly diagram</strong> shows two material cycles. <strong>Biological nutrients</strong> can safely return to the biosphere; <strong>technical nutrients</strong> should remain in managed industrial cycles. The word <em>nutrient</em> here means a material that can feed a future cycle, not necessarily a food nutrient.</p>
<table><thead><tr><th>Biological cycle</th><th>Technical cycle</th></tr></thead><tbody>
<tr><td><strong>Materials:</strong> suitable biodegradable, non-toxic biological materials such as food waste, untreated natural fibres and some untreated wood.</td><td><strong>Materials:</strong> metals, glass, synthetic polymers and durable manufactured components that should not be released into nature.</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Route:</strong> use → collection → composting or anaerobic digestion → nutrients returned safely to soil and ecosystems.</td><td><strong>Route:</strong> use → maintenance/repair → reuse/redistribution → refurbishment/remanufacture → material recycling when higher-value options are no longer practical.</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Goal:</strong> regenerate natural systems and return useful nutrients, while making the most of the material before it biodegrades.</td><td><strong>Goal:</strong> retain the product's function, components and material value for as long as possible, reducing demand for virgin resources.</td></tr>
</tbody></table>
<p><strong>Biological example:</strong> uncontaminated food scraps can be composted; microorganisms break them down and the resulting compost can improve soil. In <strong>anaerobic digestion</strong>, microorganisms break down suitable organic waste without oxygen, producing biogas and digestate that may be used as a soil amendment if safe.</p>
<p><strong>Technical example:</strong> an aluminium bicycle frame can be maintained and repaired, passed to another user, or recycled into new metal products when it can no longer be used. Recycling generally requires more processing than continued use or repair, so it is not automatically the first choice.</p>
<p><strong>Cascading</strong> means using a biological material for several applications before its final safe return to nature: for example, untreated natural fibres might be reused as stuffing or insulation before appropriate biological treatment.</p>
<p><strong>Design implications:</strong> choose safe materials and finishes; make parts easy to separate; avoid permanently bonding incompatible materials; enable repair and replacement; and plan collection or take-back systems. A product made from natural material is <strong>not automatically</strong> a biological nutrient: toxic dyes, coatings, adhesives or mixed synthetic fibres can prevent safe return to soil. Likewise, a polymer labelled biodegradable may need specific industrial composting conditions.</p>
<p><strong>Evaluation:</strong> circular systems still use energy and transport, and collection, sorting and material contamination can limit recovery. Designers must consider the whole life cycle rather than assuming that compostable or recyclable always means environmentally preferable.</p>
<p><a href="https://www.ellenmacarthurfoundation.org/circular-economy-diagram/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">View the Ellen MacArthur Foundation's butterfly diagram</a> to see both cycles and the order of technical recovery loops.</p>
</div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Evaluating environmental decisions</h3><p>No single feature makes a product sustainable. A lighter product may use less energy in transport or use, but its material may require more energy to process or be difficult to recycle. A durable, repairable product may use more material initially but remain useful for much longer.</p><p>Environmental decisions should therefore be considered across the product's life cycle and alongside economic and social consequences.</p></div></section>
