<section class="note-panel"><h2>Social, Moral and Ethical Issues in Design</h2><div class="note-intro"><p>Designers must consider the effects of products on <strong>users, workers, communities and the environment</strong> throughout a product's life.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Sustainability and Ethical Production</h3><p>A product's environmental impact should be considered from <strong>raw material extraction through manufacture, transport, use and disposal</strong>. A renewable material is not automatically sustainable: its source, processing and end of life also matter.</p><p><strong>Ethical production</strong> includes safe working conditions, fair treatment of workers, responsible sourcing and honest claims to consumers. A company's efforts to manage its social and environmental effects are often called <strong>corporate social responsibility (CSR)</strong>.</p><p>Examples of responsible decisions include using <strong>FSC certified timber</strong>, reducing waste, designing products that can be repaired, and checking working conditions in the supply chain.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Cultural Acceptability</h3><p>A product's <strong>colour, imagery, wording, materials or function</strong> may be interpreted differently by different users. Religious beliefs and local customs can also affect whether a product is suitable.</p><p>Designers should research the intended market and test ideas with users rather than assume that one design will be accepted everywhere.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Inclusive Design</h3><p><strong>Inclusive design</strong> aims to make mainstream products accessible and usable by as many people as reasonably possible.</p><p>Examples include <strong>low floor buses</strong> for wheelchair users, <strong>textured pedestrian crossings</strong> for people with sight impairments, and <strong>easy grip utensils</strong> for people with limited hand strength. These features often benefit other users too, such as people with pushchairs, luggage or temporary injuries.</p><p>Designers should identify who may be excluded, use relevant measurements and user research, and test solutions with the people who will use them.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Designing for Social Needs</h3><p>Design can respond to problems involving <strong>health, safety, poverty and access to resources</strong>. Examples include wind up radios that work without disposable batteries, safer alternatives to kerosene lighting, prosthetic limbs and patient specific medical implants.</p><p>A solution must be suitable for its setting. <strong>Appropriate technology</strong> should be affordable, safe, and possible to operate and maintain using available resources and skills.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Emergency Design and Migration</h3><p>People displaced by conflict or disaster may need <strong>shelter, clean water, food preparation and healthcare</strong>. Designers have developed flat pack shelters, portable water treatment systems, solar cookers and mobile medical facilities.</p><p>An emergency shelter should be assessed for <strong>transport efficiency, assembly time, weather protection, ventilation, safety and suitability for the local climate</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Fairtrade</h3><p><strong>Fairtrade</strong> seeks better trading conditions for farmers and workers, particularly in developing countries. Designers and manufacturers can support fairer production by checking the source of materials, choosing certified inputs where suitable and making accurate claims on packaging.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>The Six Rs of Sustainability</h3><ul><li><strong>Rethink:</strong> Consider whether the product or a different solution is needed.</li><li><strong>Refuse:</strong> Avoid unnecessary products, materials or packaging.</li><li><strong>Reduce:</strong> Use less material and energy.</li><li><strong>Reuse:</strong> Use a product or component again.</li><li><strong>Repair:</strong> Fix or replace parts to extend a product's life.</li><li><strong>Recycle:</strong> Recover materials when the product can no longer be used.</li></ul><p>Designing out unnecessary material and extending a product's life can reduce waste before recycling becomes necessary.</p></div></section>
