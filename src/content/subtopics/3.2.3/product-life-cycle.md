<section class="note-panel"><h2>Product Life Cycle</h2><div class="note-intro"><p>The <strong>product life cycle (PLC)</strong> shows how a product's sales change from launch until it is withdrawn. The shape and length of the cycle vary between products, so the model is useful for interpreting market behaviour rather than predicting an exact timetable.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Stages of the Product Life Cycle</h3><ul><li><strong>Introduction:</strong> The product is launched. Sales are usually low while customers become aware of it. Development, tooling and promotion costs may still need to be recovered.</li><li><strong>Growth:</strong> Sales rise as more customers buy the product. Competitors may introduce alternatives, so manufacturers may improve features, production efficiency or branding.</li><li><strong>Maturity:</strong> Sales reach a high level and stabilise. Competition can be strong and manufacturers may use differentiation, cost reduction or updated versions to maintain demand.</li><li><strong>Decline:</strong> Sales fall as customer needs, fashion or technology change. Continuing production may become less economical and the product may be withdrawn.</li></ul></div>

<div class="study-card"><h3>Extension Strategies</h3><p>An <strong>extension strategy</strong> attempts to delay decline by improving performance, changing appearance, reaching a different market, adding features or releasing an updated version.</p><p>An extension is worthwhile only if the extra development, manufacturing and marketing cost is justified by the additional demand. Adding features can extend commercial life, for example, but unnecessary complexity may increase price, energy use or the number of components that can fail.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Demand Pull and Technology Push</h3><p><strong>Demand pull</strong> occurs when identified customer needs encourage a new or improved product. It can reduce the risk of developing something users do not want, although research cannot guarantee commercial success.</p><p><strong>Technology push</strong> occurs when a new technology enables a product or capability that a company then introduces to the market. It can create entirely new opportunities, but technical novelty alone does not make a successful product: customers must value the benefit enough to accept its cost, complexity or changes in behaviour.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Planned Obsolescence</h3><p><strong>Planned obsolescence</strong> is the deliberate design of a product so that it needs replacing sooner than necessary. Unavailable spare parts, difficult repair or loss of software compatibility can contribute to this.</p><p>Frequent replacement can generate repeat sales and allow users to adopt newer technology, but it can increase consumer cost, material consumption and waste. A longer-lasting, repairable product may have a higher initial price yet reduce lifecycle impacts if it remains useful for significantly longer.</p></div></section>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Evolution and redevelopment</h3><p>AQA includes <strong>evolution</strong> as part of the product life cycle. Products can be refined and redeveloped as user expectations, technology, materials and competing products change. This can involve improving an existing model rather than immediately replacing it with a completely unrelated product.</p><p>Redevelopment should solve a meaningful problem or create a valued benefit. A thinner product, for example, may appear more advanced but could be a poor improvement if it reduces battery capacity, durability or repairability. Whether a change is successful depends on its overall effect on the intended users, manufacturer and product lifecycle.</p></div>
