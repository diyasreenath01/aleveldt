<section class="note-panel"><h2>Major Developments in Technology</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>Technological developments change <strong>what products can do</strong>, <strong>how designers develop them</strong>, and <strong>how they are manufactured</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Microelectronics</h3><p><strong>Microelectronics</strong> involves miniature electronic components, particularly <strong>integrated circuits (ICs)</strong>. An IC contains many transistors on one chip. Increasing the number of transistors has allowed products to become smaller and more powerful.</p><p><strong>Transistors</strong> replaced large, power hungry vacuum tubes, making portable electronic products practical. Integrated circuits then allowed more functions to fit into devices such as computers and mobile phones.</p><p>Early electronic products needed large cabinets to contain bulky, fragile and power-hungry components such as thermionic valves and extensive wiring. Solid-state microelectronics condensed circuitry onto small silicon wafers, allowing designers to create <strong>smaller, slimmer and handheld products</strong>.</p><p>Portable music players show how several developments can change one product:</p><ul><li><strong>Compact cassettes:</strong> Enclosed magnetic tape made music genuinely portable and protected the tape inside a robust shell.</li><li><strong>Compact discs and lasers:</strong> Semiconductor lasers read digital data from reflective pits on a disc without physical contact. Unlike a stylus or tape head, this avoided mechanical wear during reading and improved reliability.</li><li><strong>Rechargeable batteries:</strong> Nickel-metal hydride (Ni-MH) cells provided rechargeable, high-current power for portable electronics and helped reduce dependence on disposable cells and mains cables. Later lithium-ion batteries offered high energy density and greater flexibility in battery shape, helping products become thinner and lighter.</li><li><strong>MP3 compression:</strong> Reduced the storage needed for each song, allowing much larger music libraries on small devices.</li><li><strong>Micro hard drives:</strong> Miniature spinning disks provided gigabyte-scale storage in portable devices, although their moving parts could be vulnerable to shock and vibration.</li><li><strong>Flash memory:</strong> Stored large numbers of files without moving parts. Compared with spinning hard drives, solid-state storage improves shock resistance, reduces electrical consumption and allows thinner devices.</li><li><strong>LCD displays:</strong> Replaced many mechanical dials and indicator lamps with compact interfaces capable of displaying information such as track names and battery level.</li><li><strong>Capacitive touchscreens:</strong> Replaced many physical controls with a glass surface that can detect touch and multi-touch gestures.</li><li><strong>Streaming:</strong> High-speed wireless connections allow music to be accessed online rather than requiring all audio files to be stored locally on the device.</li></ul><p>More powerful electronics also require designers to consider <strong>battery life, heat, repairability and electronic waste</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>How Technology Changes Product Form</h3><p>Technological development can change both the <strong>physical form</strong> of a product and the way it is used. Smaller components, improved batteries, digital storage and new interfaces have allowed many products to become more portable and multifunctional.</p><ul><li><strong>Radios:</strong> Large living-room products developed into compact battery-powered portable devices.</li><li><strong>Cameras:</strong> Chemical film and mechanical systems were increasingly replaced by digital sensors and LCD screens.</li><li><strong>Timepieces:</strong> Mechanical springs and escapements were supplemented or replaced by quartz technology and multifunctional digital displays.</li></ul></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>The Internet of Things</h3><p>The <strong>Internet of Things (IoT)</strong> connects physical devices to networks so they can collect and exchange data. For example, sensors in factory equipment can detect changes in performance and help predict when maintenance is needed. Designers must also consider data privacy, security and whether the product can function without a connection.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>How Technology Changed Design and Manufacture</h2><div class="note-intro"><p><strong>Computer aided design (CAD)</strong> lets designers create, edit and test digital models. <strong>Computer aided manufacture (CAM)</strong> uses digital information to control manufacturing equipment, such as CNC machines.</p><p>Digital models can be shared with other designers, modified quickly and tested before a physical prototype is made. In factories, CNC machinery, robotics and digital quality checks can improve accuracy and repeatability. However, digital predictions still need to be checked through physical testing.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Key developments</h3><ul><li><strong>3D printing:</strong> Builds a part layer by layer. It is useful for prototypes, customised parts and complex forms.</li><li><strong>Finite element analysis (FEA):</strong> Predicts how a model may respond to forces or heat, helping designers identify weak areas.</li><li><strong>Computational fluid dynamics (CFD):</strong> Models how liquids or gases flow around or through a design.</li><li><strong>Virtual reality (VR):</strong> Allows designers to experience a digital model at scale and assess features such as layout and ergonomics.</li><li><strong>Cloud based CAD:</strong> Allows people in different locations to work on shared design files.</li></ul></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>New Materials</h2><div class="note-intro"><p>The choice of material should be explained through a link between its <strong>properties</strong> and its <strong>use</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Glulam</h3><p>Layers of bonded timber form strong, lightweight beams that can be curved. Typical uses include buildings and bridges with large spans.</p><p><strong>Example:</strong> Glulam can be formed into a curved roof beam. Bonding several timber layers together helps produce the required shape while distributing defects found in individual pieces of wood.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Kevlar</h3><p>Strong, tough and lightweight fibre. Used for protective equipment, puncture resistant tyres and aircraft components.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Graphene</h3><p>Extremely thin carbon sheet with high tensile strength and good electrical and thermal conductivity. Potential uses include sensors, electronics, coatings and batteries.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Precious metal clay</h3><p>Mouldable metal particles that form a solid metal object after firing. Used for jewellery and decorative products.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>New Manufacturing Methods</h2>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Electrohydraulic Forming</h3><p>An electrical discharge creates a shockwave in water, forcing sheet metal against a former. This can form detailed shapes quickly, including <strong>car parts and appliance casings</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Metal Additive Manufacturing</h3><p>A laser fuses metal powder <strong>layer by layer</strong> to build a part from a digital model. This can produce complex shapes and internal channels that would be difficult to machine. It is useful for <strong>prototypes and customised parts</strong>, although finishing may still be required.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Fibre Injection Moulding</h3><p>A polymer mixed with reinforcing fibres is injected into a mould. The fibres can improve the <strong>strength and stiffness</strong> of lightweight parts used in products such as vehicles and sports equipment.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Laser Beam Welding</h3><p>A concentrated laser joins metal with a <strong>narrow, precise weld</strong>. It is useful for thin components and can cause less distortion than a wider heat source.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Physical Vapour Deposition</h3><p><strong>PVD</strong> deposits a thin coating onto a product's surface. The coating can improve <strong>wear resistance or appearance</strong>, for example on cutting tools or decorative products.</p></div></section>
