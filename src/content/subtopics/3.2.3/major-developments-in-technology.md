<section class="note-panel"><h2>Major Developments in Technology</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>Technological developments change <strong>what products can do</strong>, <strong>how designers develop them</strong>, and <strong>how they are manufactured</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Microelectronics and Miniaturisation</h3><p><strong>Microelectronics</strong> involves very small electronic components and circuits, particularly <strong>transistors</strong> and <strong>integrated circuits (ICs)</strong>. Their development has allowed electronic products to become progressively <strong>smaller, lighter, more reliable, more energy efficient and more powerful</strong>.</p><p>A useful development sequence to remember is <strong>thermionic valve → discrete transistor → integrated circuit → modern microprocessor / system-on-chip</strong>.</p><figure class="note-figure"><img src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/955ACORN.jpg" alt="Real thermionic valve and transistor shown together for size comparison" loading="lazy"><figcaption><strong>Valve → transistor:</strong> this real component comparison shows how solid-state electronics dramatically reduced component size.</figcaption></figure></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Thermionic Valves / Vacuum Tubes</h3><p>Early electronic products used <strong>thermionic valves</strong>, also called <strong>vacuum tubes</strong>. A valve controls the flow of electric current inside a sealed glass vacuum. An electric current heats a metal filament or <strong>cathode</strong>, releasing electrons by <strong>thermionic emission</strong>. A positively charged <strong>anode</strong> attracts the electrons, while a control grid can regulate the flow of current.</p><p>Valves made complex electronic products possible and were used in <strong>radios, amplifiers, televisions, communications equipment and early computers</strong>.</p><p>However, valves created major design limitations:</p><ul><li><strong>Large and bulky:</strong> glass tubes and associated wiring required large product housings.</li><li><strong>High power consumption:</strong> the filament had to be heated continuously.</li><li><strong>Heat:</strong> significant energy was wasted as heat, so equipment could require cooling.</li><li><strong>Fragile:</strong> glass envelopes and filaments could break or fail.</li><li><strong>Poor portability:</strong> their size and power requirements made small battery-powered products difficult.</li></ul><p>These limitations explain why early electronic equipment and computers were physically very large despite performing relatively limited functions.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>1947: The Transistor Breakthrough</h3><p>In <strong>1947</strong>, the <strong>transistor</strong> was developed. Unlike a valve, a transistor controls current using a solid <strong>semiconductor</strong> rather than a heated filament inside a vacuum.</p><p>A <strong>semiconductor</strong> is a material whose electrical conductivity lies between that of a conductor and an insulator and can be controlled electrically. Semiconductor materials such as <strong>silicon</strong> enabled the development of solid-state electronics.</p><p>A transistor can work as a <strong>switch</strong> or an <strong>amplifier</strong>. A small input can control a larger current, allowing electronic signals to be switched or amplified.</p><p>Compared with thermionic valves, transistors offered:</p><ul><li><strong>Much smaller size</strong> — allowing smaller product housings and greater miniaturisation.</li><li><strong>Lower power consumption</strong> — no heated filament was required.</li><li><strong>Less heat</strong> — improving efficiency and reducing cooling requirements.</li><li><strong>Greater durability</strong> — solid-state components were less fragile than glass valves.</li><li><strong>Battery operation</strong> — low voltage requirements made genuinely portable electronics practical.</li><li><strong>Improved reliability</strong> — fewer fragile components meant fewer failures.</li></ul></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Product Example: Regency TR-1 Pocket Radio</h3><p>The <strong>Regency TR-1</strong>, introduced in <strong>1954</strong>, is a useful example of how a technological development changed product design. Earlier domestic radios were typically large products housed in wooden cabinets and connected to mains electricity. The TR-1 used four small transistors instead of bulky valves and could fit into a coat pocket.</p><p>This changed both the <strong>physical form</strong> and the <strong>way the product was used</strong>. Radio became personal and portable: users could carry music and news rather than using a fixed radio in the home.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Integrated Circuits (ICs)</h3><p>As electronic circuits became more complex, connecting thousands of separate transistors, resistors and capacitors became bulky and difficult. In the late 1950s, the <strong>integrated circuit (IC)</strong> was developed, allowing an entire electronic circuit to be formed on a single piece of semiconductor material.</p><p>By the <strong>mid-1960s</strong>, microelectronic ICs could contain around <strong>500 transistors on one small chip</strong>. Instead of assembling every transistor separately, many electronic functions could therefore be integrated into one compact component.</p><p>This further reduced product size while increasing <strong>processing capability, reliability and functionality</strong>. It also reduced the amount of separate wiring and assembly required.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>ULSI and Modern Microelectronics</h3><p>Continued improvements in semiconductor manufacture allowed more transistors to be placed onto increasingly small chips. By the <strong>1980s</strong>, highly integrated microprocessors could contain <strong>over one million transistors</strong>. This helped make compact computers, laptops and mobile electronic products commercially practical.</p><p>Modern fabrication works at <strong>nanometre scale</strong> and can place <strong>billions of transistors</strong> onto a single chip. A smartphone can therefore combine processing, graphics, communications and many other functions in a pocket-sized product.</p><p>The overall trend is <strong>more electronic functions in less physical space</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>How Microelectronics Changed Product Design and Manufacture</h3><ul><li><strong>Miniaturisation:</strong> products became smaller, thinner and lighter.</li><li><strong>Portability:</strong> low-power electronics made battery-powered products practical.</li><li><strong>More functions:</strong> many electronic functions can be combined into one product.</li><li><strong>Improved reliability:</strong> solid-state components have fewer fragile or moving parts.</li><li><strong>Lower energy use and heat:</strong> modern components require less power than thermionic valves.</li><li><strong>Reduced cost:</strong> large numbers of electronic components can be manufactured together on chips.</li><li><strong>Improved usability:</strong> digital displays, touch controls and intelligent functions can be incorporated into products.</li><li><strong>Changes to manufacture:</strong> electronic assembly has moved from extensive hand-wired components towards highly automated manufacture of miniature circuits and printed circuit assemblies.</li></ul></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Developments in Battery Technology</h3><p><strong>Primary batteries</strong> are non-rechargeable and are replaced when depleted. <strong>Secondary batteries</strong> are rechargeable and can be used repeatedly. Battery development has allowed products to become <strong>smaller, lighter and portable</strong>.</p><p>Early batteries were large, heavy and could leak. Dry cells improved portability, while modern rechargeable <strong>lithium-ion</strong> batteries store much more energy for their size and mass. Thin <strong>lithium-polymer pouch cells</strong> also give designers greater freedom over the shape and internal layout of products.</p><p>This has helped make <strong>slim smartphones, laptops, wireless earbuds, smartwatches, cordless power tools and electric vehicles</strong> practical.</p><ul><li><strong>Miniaturisation:</strong> smaller batteries allow thinner and more compact products.</li><li><strong>Portability:</strong> cordless products can operate away from mains electricity.</li><li><strong>Product form:</strong> thin pouch cells allow more streamlined housings.</li><li><strong>Safety:</strong> modern battery packs may use a <strong>Battery Management System (BMS)</strong> to monitor charging and temperature and help prevent unsafe conditions.</li><li><strong>Environment:</strong> rechargeable batteries reduce reliance on single-use cells, but raw-material extraction, recycling and disposal still create environmental concerns.</li></ul><p><strong>Design trade-off:</strong> glued-in batteries can help products become thinner and more water-resistant, but make repair and replacement harder. Removable batteries can extend product life and improve repairability, but may require a larger enclosure and additional seals.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Microelectronics in Modern Products</h3><p>Portable music players show how several developments can change one product:</p><div class="note-image-grid" style="display:grid;grid-template-columns:repeat(auto-fit,minmax(180px,1fr));gap:14px;margin:18px 0"><figure class="note-figure" style="margin:0"><img src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/Compactcassette.jpg" alt="Real compact audio cassette" loading="lazy"><figcaption><strong>Compact cassette</strong> — magnetic tape in a protective shell.</figcaption></figure><figure class="note-figure" style="margin:0"><img src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/CompactDisc.jpg" alt="Real compact disc" loading="lazy"><figcaption><strong>Compact disc</strong> — optical digital storage read by laser.</figcaption></figure><figure class="note-figure" style="margin:0"><img src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/IPod_classic.png" alt="Real iPod Classic digital music player" loading="lazy"><figcaption><strong>Digital music player</strong> — large music libraries became portable.</figcaption></figure></div><ul><li><strong>Compact cassettes:</strong> enclosed magnetic tape made music portable and protected the tape inside a robust shell.</li><li><strong>Compact discs and lasers:</strong> semiconductor lasers read digital data without physical contact, reducing mechanical wear during reading.</li><li><strong>Rechargeable batteries:</strong> made repeated portable use possible; later lithium-ion batteries helped products become thinner and lighter.</li><li><strong>MP3 compression:</strong> reduced the storage needed for each song.</li><li><strong>Micro hard drives:</strong> provided gigabyte-scale storage in portable devices, although moving parts could be vulnerable to shock.</li><li><strong>Flash memory:</strong> stored large numbers of files without moving parts, improving shock resistance and reducing power consumption.</li><li><strong>LCD displays:</strong> allowed compact products to display information such as track names and battery level.</li><li><strong>Capacitive touchscreens:</strong> replaced many physical controls with a touch-sensitive glass interface.</li><li><strong>Streaming:</strong> wireless connections allow content to be accessed online rather than stored entirely on the device.</li></ul><p>More powerful electronics also require designers to consider <strong>battery life, heat management, repairability, software support, data security and electronic waste</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>How Technology Changes Product Form</h3><p>Technological development can change both the <strong>physical form</strong> of a product and the way it is used. Smaller components, improved batteries, digital storage and new interfaces have allowed many products to become more portable and multifunctional.</p><ul><li><strong>Radios:</strong> large living-room products developed into compact battery-powered portable devices.</li><li><strong>Cameras:</strong> chemical film and mechanical systems were increasingly replaced by digital sensors and LCD screens.</li><li><strong>Timepieces:</strong> mechanical springs and escapements were supplemented or replaced by quartz technology and multifunctional digital displays.</li></ul></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>The Internet of Things</h3><p>The <strong>Internet of Things (IoT)</strong> connects physical devices to networks so they can collect and exchange data. For example, sensors in factory equipment can detect changes in performance and help predict when maintenance is needed. Designers must also consider data privacy, security and whether the product can function without a connection.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>How Technology Changed Design and Manufacture</h2><div class="note-intro"><p><strong>Computer aided design (CAD)</strong> lets designers create, edit and test digital models. <strong>Computer aided manufacture (CAM)</strong> uses digital information to control manufacturing equipment, such as CNC machines.</p><p>Digital models can be shared with other designers, modified quickly and tested before a physical prototype is made. In factories, CNC machinery, robotics and digital quality checks can improve accuracy and repeatability. However, digital predictions still need to be checked through physical testing.</p></div><div class="note-image-grid" style="display:grid;grid-template-columns:repeat(auto-fit,minmax(240px,1fr));gap:14px;margin:18px 0"><figure class="note-figure" style="margin:0"><img src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/3dReconstruct_Mechanical_Part.png" alt="Real engineering software screenshot showing a meshed mechanical part for FEA and CFD analysis" loading="lazy"><figcaption><strong>FEA / CFD model:</strong> a digital mesh divides a component into many small elements so its behaviour can be analysed.</figcaption></figure><figure class="note-figure" style="margin:0"><img src="https://www.iws.fraunhofer.de/en/newsandmedia/press_releases/2019/presseinformation_2019-06/jcr%3Acontent/contentPar/sectioncomponent/sectionParsys/textwithinlinedimage/imageComponent1/image.img.jpg/1647105852182/PM-2019-06-fraunhofer-iws-600-hybrid-future-am-1.jpg" alt="Real metal additive manufacturing process using a laser deposition head" loading="lazy"><figcaption><strong>Metal additive manufacturing:</strong> material is deposited and fused to build geometry from digital data.</figcaption></figure></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Key developments</h3><ul><li><strong>3D printing:</strong> builds a part layer by layer. It is useful for prototypes, customised parts and complex forms.</li><li><strong>Finite element analysis (FEA):</strong> predicts how a model may respond to forces or heat, helping designers identify weak areas.</li><li><strong>Computational fluid dynamics (CFD):</strong> models how liquids or gases flow around or through a design.</li><li><strong>Virtual reality (VR):</strong> allows designers to experience a digital model at scale and assess features such as layout and ergonomics.</li><li><strong>Cloud based CAD:</strong> allows people in different locations to work on shared design files.</li></ul></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>Modern and New Materials</h2><div class="note-intro"><p><strong>Modern materials</strong> are relatively recent materials, or existing materials used, combined or treated in new ways to improve their functional or aesthetic properties. They can help designers solve <strong>design problems, technical constraints and environmental issues</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Nanomaterials and Nanotechnology</h3><p><strong>Nanomaterials</strong> contain extremely small particles measured in nanometres. At this scale, materials can provide useful surface or performance properties without adding a thick or heavy layer to a product.</p><p>They can be used in very thin films and coatings. Examples include <strong>oleophobic coatings</strong> on smartphone screens that help repel greasy fingerprints and <strong>hydrophobic coatings</strong> that repel water.</p><p>Nanotechnology can add useful properties to an existing surface while keeping the product lightweight and its dimensions almost unchanged.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Graphene</h3><figure class="note-figure"><img src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/Basic_Graphene.png" alt="Molecular structure of a graphene sheet showing its hexagonal carbon lattice" loading="lazy"><figcaption><strong>Graphene structure:</strong> carbon atoms form an extremely thin hexagonal sheet.</figcaption></figure><p><strong>Graphene</strong> is an extremely thin carbon material. It has <strong>very high tensile strength, low mass, flexibility, transparency, heat resistance and high electrical conductivity</strong>.</p><p>Potential applications include <strong>flexible electronics, sensors, energy storage, composite materials and protective products</strong>. Its electrical conductivity and flexibility are particularly useful where designers want electronic functions without a thick, rigid material.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Glulam</h3><figure class="note-figure"><img src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/Glulam.JPG" alt="Real building structure using glued laminated timber beams" loading="lazy"><figcaption><strong>Glulam in use:</strong> laminated timber members can create strong structural frames and large spans.</figcaption></figure><p><strong>Glulam</strong> (glued laminated timber) is made from layers of timber bonded together with durable, moisture-resistant adhesive, with the grain generally running parallel along the member.</p><p>It has a good <strong>strength-to-weight ratio</strong>, is structurally stable, corrosion resistant and can be manufactured in large curved forms. Laminating the timber also helps reduce the effect of defects and weaknesses found in individual pieces.</p><p>Typical applications include <strong>beams, bridges, domes, arches and large-span roof structures</strong>. Timber can be sustainably sourced, although the bonded construction can make end-of-life recycling more difficult.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Kevlar</h3><figure class="note-figure"><img src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/Staal_van_aramidevezel_van_het_merk_Kevlar.jpg" alt="Real sample of yellow Kevlar aramid fibres" loading="lazy"><figcaption><strong>Kevlar fibre:</strong> the characteristic aramid fibres combine low mass with very high tensile strength.</figcaption></figure><p><strong>Kevlar</strong> is a strong, lightweight aramid fibre. When woven it can form a strong and flexible fabric, and it can also be combined with resin or other materials to form composites.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> high strength-to-weight ratio, abrasion resistance, resistance to stretching and good performance at elevated temperatures.</p><p><strong>Limitations:</strong> it can absorb water, is difficult to cut and manufacture, has poorer compressive strength than tensile strength, can be affected by UV exposure and is difficult to recycle.</p><p>Applications include <strong>protective clothing and body armour, cut-resistant gloves, puncture-resistant tyres, boats, aircraft components and motorsport products</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Precious Metal Clay</h3><figure class="note-figure"><img src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/MetalClayPendant.jpg" alt="Pendant produced using precious metal clay" loading="lazy"><figcaption><strong>Precious metal clay:</strong> the mouldable material can be shaped before firing to leave a solid metal object.</figcaption></figure><p><strong>Precious metal clay</strong> contains very small particles of metals such as silver, gold, bronze or copper mixed with an organic binder and water. Before firing it can be shaped by hand or with moulds in a similar way to conventional clay.</p><p>During firing, the binder burns away and the metal particles sinter together, leaving a solid metal object. The material shrinks during firing, so the designer must allow for this when deciding the original dimensions.</p><p>It is particularly useful for <strong>jewellery, beads and small decorative products</strong> because detailed forms can be created without traditional metal-forming techniques.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>New Manufacturing Methods</h2><div class="note-intro"><p>These methods are important technological developments because advances in <strong>digital control, lasers, high-energy electrical systems, composite materials and vacuum technology</strong> have allowed manufacturers to make products in ways that were difficult or impossible using older processes.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Electrohydraulic Forming</h3><p><strong>Electrohydraulic forming</strong> uses a high-energy electrical discharge in a liquid to create a shock wave that forces metal into a die or former. It can replace conventional press forming for shapes that are difficult to produce with normal pressing.</p><p><strong>Process:</strong></p><ol><li>A metal sheet is placed over a die and submerged in fluid.</li><li>Electrodes are positioned in the fluid.</li><li>A high-voltage electrical discharge is released.</li><li>The discharge creates a shock wave in the liquid.</li><li>The shock wave forces the metal into the die shape.</li><li>The formed component is removed.</li></ol><p><strong>Applications:</strong> automotive panels, aerospace components and prototype forming.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> can form complex shapes and cavities, no direct tool contact so there is less tool wear, suitable for thin metals and capable of high accuracy.</p><p><strong>Limitations:</strong> requires specialised high-voltage equipment, increasing setup cost and limiting its use compared with conventional forming methods.</p><p><strong>Technological link:</strong> rapid capacitor discharge through electrodes creates a controlled shock wave in water, replacing the direct force of a mechanical press and allowing complex sheet-metal forms to be produced using a single-sided die.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Direct Metal Laser Sintering (DMLS)</h3><figure class="note-figure"><img src="https://www.iws.fraunhofer.de/en/newsandmedia/press_releases/2019/presseinformation_2019-06/jcr%3Acontent/contentPar/sectioncomponent/sectionParsys/textwithinlinedimage/imageComponent1/image.img.jpg/1647105852182/PM-2019-06-fraunhofer-iws-600-hybrid-future-am-1.jpg" alt="Real metal additive manufacturing using a laser deposition head" loading="lazy"><figcaption><strong>Metal additive manufacturing:</strong> metal components can be built directly from digital data rather than relying on conventional tooling.</figcaption></figure><p><strong>Direct Metal Laser Sintering (DMLS)</strong> is an additive manufacturing process in which a laser fuses metal powder <strong>layer by layer</strong> to create a component from digital data. It allows complex metal parts and internal structures to be produced without conventional moulds or tooling.</p><p><strong>Process:</strong></p><ol><li>A thin layer of metal powder is spread across the build platform.</li><li>A laser scans the required areas of the surface.</li><li>The laser melts or sinters the powder to form solid metal.</li><li>The platform lowers slightly.</li><li>A new layer of powder is added.</li><li>The process repeats until the complete 3D component has been built.</li></ol><p><strong>Applications:</strong> aerospace parts, medical implants and customised engineering components.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> creates complex internal structures, requires no conventional tooling, produces little material waste and can achieve high precision. It is particularly useful for lightweight and customised components that would be difficult to manufacture conventionally.</p><p><strong>Limitations:</strong> equipment and production are expensive and the process is relatively slow for mass production, so it is mainly used for specialised or high-value components.</p><p><strong>Technological link:</strong> 3D CAD, layer-slicing software and computer-controlled lasers allow metal powder to be fused directly from digital data, removing the need for moulds or dies and enabling complex internal and customised forms.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Fibre Injection Moulding</h3><p><strong>Fibre injection moulding</strong> uses polymer pellets containing reinforcing <strong>glass or carbon fibres</strong>. The material is heated and injection moulded to produce a fibre-reinforced composite component directly, rather than making a composite through a separate lay-up process.</p><p><strong>Process:</strong></p><ol><li>Polymer pellets and reinforcing fibres are heated and mixed.</li><li>The material becomes a molten composite.</li><li>It is injected under pressure into a mould cavity.</li><li>The mould remains closed while the material cools.</li><li>The material solidifies into the required shape.</li><li>The component is ejected.</li></ol><p><strong>Applications:</strong> lightweight automotive parts, sports equipment, consumer products and medical equipment.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> stronger than standard unreinforced plastics, lightweight, good surface finish and suitable for high production rates with consistent quality.</p><p><strong>Limitations:</strong> fibre-filled polymers can be more difficult to recycle than a single polymer, creating an end-of-life environmental issue.</p><p><strong>Technological link:</strong> fibre-reinforced thermoplastics and accurately controlled injection-moulding machines allow glass or carbon fibres to be moulded into the component during a fast production cycle, producing stronger, lighter parts than unreinforced plastics.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Laser Beam Welding</h3><figure class="note-figure"><img src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/High-power_laser_welding.jpg" alt="Real high-power laser welding test on metal" loading="lazy"><figcaption><strong>Laser beam welding:</strong> a concentrated laser creates a narrow, precise weld while shielding gas protects the weld area.</figcaption></figure><p><strong>Laser beam welding</strong> uses the intense heat of a focused laser beam to melt and join metal components. It can replace conventional MIG or TIG welding where greater precision, speed or automation is required.</p><p><strong>Process:</strong></p><ol><li>Two metal parts are accurately aligned.</li><li>A focused laser beam is directed at the joint.</li><li>The laser produces intense localised heat.</li><li>The metal melts at the joint.</li><li>The molten metal fuses together.</li><li>The joint cools to form a strong weld.</li></ol><p><strong>Applications:</strong> automotive manufacture, shipbuilding, construction, railway equipment and other applications requiring precise metal joints.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> very precise and clean welds, minimal heat distortion, high processing speed and good suitability for automated production.</p><p><strong>Limitations:</strong> the equipment is expensive and skilled operation is required, which can increase manufacturing costs.</p><p><strong>Technological link:</strong> high-power focused lasers combined with CNC or robotic control produce narrow, repeatable welds at high speed with less heat distortion than conventional MIG or TIG welding.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Physical Vapour Deposition (PVD)</h3><p><strong>Physical Vapour Deposition</strong> is a finishing process that deposits a very thin, hard coating onto a product in a vacuum chamber. A solid coating material is vaporised and then condenses onto the surface of the component. It can be used as an alternative to processes such as electroplating.</p><p><strong>Process:</strong></p><ol><li>A solid coating material is placed in a vacuum chamber.</li><li>The coating material is heated or bombarded.</li><li>It vaporises into atoms.</li><li>The atoms travel through the vacuum.</li><li>They condense onto the product surface.</li><li>A thin, hard coating forms.</li></ol><p><strong>Applications:</strong> semiconductor components, food packaging, machine-tool tips, cutting tools and decorative products.</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong> produces very thin and durable coatings, improves wear and corrosion resistance and can be environmentally cleaner than some traditional coating processes.</p><p><strong>Limitations:</strong> vacuum conditions and specialised equipment increase production complexity and cost.</p><p><strong>Technological link:</strong> high-vacuum and controlled deposition technology allows vaporised material to form extremely thin, precise coatings, improving wear or corrosion resistance without significantly changing component dimensions.</p></div></section>
