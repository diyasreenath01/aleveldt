## Major developments in technology

Technological developments change **what products can do, how they are designed and how they are manufactured**. The important point is to understand the development and the design consequence it creates.

### Microelectronics and miniaturisation

Electronic components have developed from bulky **thermionic valves** to **transistors, integrated circuits (ICs) and microprocessors**.

Transistors and ICs are smaller, more reliable and use less power than thermionic valves. This allowed electronic products to become **smaller, lighter, more portable and more complex** while using less physical space for electronic control.

Miniaturisation also allows more functions to be combined into one product. This is closely linked with developments in batteries, digital storage, sensors and wireless communication.

### Battery technology

Improved rechargeable batteries, particularly lithium-ion cells, provide relatively high energy storage for their size and mass. This supports portable electronics, cordless products and electric vehicles.

The benefit of portability must be balanced against battery lifespan, replacement, extraction of raw materials and end-of-life recycling. A smaller rechargeable product is not automatically more sustainable.

### Technological convergence

**Technological convergence** occurs when several technologies combine within one product. Modern products can combine processing, communication, sensors, cameras, storage and software rather than requiring separate devices for each function.

This can improve convenience and reduce the number of separate products required, although greater complexity can make repair and upgrading more difficult.

## Digital developments in design

### CAD and digital data

**Computer aided design (CAD)** allows accurate 2D drawings and 3D models to be created, edited and shared. Digital models can be transferred into manufacturing and prototyping systems.

Common file formats include:

- **DXF** for transferring 2D drawing/vector information between CAD and manufacturing software
- **STL** for representing the surface geometry of a 3D model, commonly for rapid prototyping and additive manufacture.

Digital transfer improves speed and repeatability, but incorrect dimensions or settings can reproduce the same error repeatedly, so designs still require checking and validation.

### FEA and CFD

**Finite Element Analysis (FEA)** uses a digital model to predict behaviour such as stress, strain and deformation. Designers can identify highly stressed or over-engineered areas before producing a physical prototype.

**Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD)** models the movement of liquids or gases and can investigate factors such as airflow, cooling and pressure.

Both can reduce the number of physical prototypes required and allow alternatives to be compared quickly. Their accuracy depends on realistic geometry, material data, loads and boundary conditions, so physical testing may still be needed to validate results.

### Virtual reality and cloud CAD

**Virtual reality (VR)** can allow designers or users to experience a full-scale digital environment and assess factors such as layout, reach, visibility and spatial relationships before manufacture.

**Cloud CAD** allows people in different locations to work on shared design information. This supports collaborative development and easier sharing of current design data, but depends on suitable software, connectivity and version control.

## Developments in manufacture

### CAD/CAM integration

**Computer aided manufacture (CAM)** uses digital information to control manufacturing equipment such as CNC routers, mills, lathes and laser cutters. Linking CAD and CAM reduces repeated manual transfer of dimensions and can improve accuracy and repeatability.

However, the process still depends on correct setup, tooling, machine parameters and skilled checking.

### Rapid prototyping and additive manufacture

Additive manufacture builds a physical object directly from digital data, normally layer by layer. It allows designers to move quickly from a CAD model to a prototype and can produce complex or customised forms without conventional moulds or dies.

It is particularly useful for prototypes, one-offs and low-volume specialised parts. For high-volume production, build speed, material cost, finish and post-processing may make conventional processes more appropriate.

### New materials and manufacturing methods

Developments in materials and manufacturing can create new design possibilities by improving properties, allowing more complex geometry or increasing manufacturing precision. When considering a new technology, designers should judge it by the actual requirement rather than assuming that a newer process or material is automatically better.

Important considerations include **performance, accuracy, production volume, cost, speed, energy use, repairability and end-of-life recovery**.
