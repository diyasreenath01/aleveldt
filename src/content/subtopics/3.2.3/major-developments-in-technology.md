<section class="note-panel"><h2>Major Developments in Technology</h2>
<div class="note-intro"><p>Technological developments change <strong>what products can do</strong>, <strong>how designers develop them</strong>, and <strong>how they are manufactured</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Microelectronics and Miniaturisation</h3><p><strong>Microelectronics</strong> uses very small electronic components, particularly <strong>transistors</strong> and <strong>integrated circuits (ICs)</strong>. Their development has made products smaller, lighter, more reliable, more energy efficient and more powerful.</p><p><strong>Key development:</strong> thermionic valve → transistor → integrated circuit → modern microprocessor / IC.</p><figure class="note-figure"><img src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/955ACORN.jpg" alt="Real thermionic valve and transistor shown together for size comparison" loading="lazy"><figcaption><strong>Valve → transistor:</strong> solid-state electronics dramatically reduced component size.</figcaption></figure></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>From Vacuum Tubes to Transistors</h3><p>Early electronic products used <strong>thermionic valves (vacuum tubes)</strong> to control and amplify electrical signals. They were used in radios, televisions and early computers, but were <strong>large, fragile, power hungry and produced a lot of heat</strong>. This limited product size and portability.</p><p>The <strong>transistor</strong>, developed in <strong>1947</strong>, used semiconductor material instead of a heated vacuum tube. A transistor can act as a <strong>switch or amplifier</strong>.</p><p>Compared with valves, transistors were <strong>much smaller, more durable, more reliable and used less power</strong>. They also produced less heat and made <strong>battery-powered portable electronics</strong> practical.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Integrated Circuits and Miniaturisation</h3><p>In the late 1950s, the <strong>integrated circuit (IC)</strong> allowed many electronic components to be formed on a single semiconductor chip. By the mid-1960s, ICs could contain around <strong>500 transistors</strong>; by the 1980s highly integrated chips could contain <strong>over one million</strong>. Modern chips can contain <strong>billions of transistors</strong>.</p><p>Increasing the number of transistors on a small chip means products can provide <strong>more functions in less physical space</strong>, helping create compact computers, smartphones and other multifunctional products.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Impact on Product Design</h3><ul><li><strong>Smaller and lighter products</strong> through miniaturisation.</li><li><strong>Greater portability</strong> because low-power electronics can run from batteries.</li><li><strong>Lower power consumption and less heat</strong> than valve-based electronics.</li><li><strong>Greater reliability</strong> because solid-state components are less fragile.</li><li><strong>More functions</strong> can be built into a single product.</li><li><strong>Lower manufacturing cost</strong> as large numbers of components can be produced together on chips.</li><li><strong>Improved usability</strong> through digital displays, touch controls and intelligent functions.</li></ul><p><strong>Exam link:</strong> Explain the chain of effect rather than simply saying products became smaller: <strong>smaller components → lower power and heat → battery operation → smaller product housings → portability → more functions and improved usability</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Exam Example: Regency TR-1 Pocket Radio</h3><p>The <strong>Regency TR-1 (1954)</strong> used four transistors instead of bulky valves. Earlier radios were generally large, mains-powered household products; the TR-1 could fit into a pocket and run from a battery.</p><p>This shows how a technological development changed both <strong>product form and user behaviour</strong>: radio became a personal, portable product rather than something fixed in the home.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Microelectronics in Modern Products</h3><p>Portable music players show how several developments can combine:</p><div class="note-image-grid" style="display:grid;grid-template-columns:repeat(auto-fit,minmax(180px,1fr));gap:14px;margin:18px 0"><figure class="note-figure" style="margin:0"><img src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/Compactcassette.jpg" alt="Real compact audio cassette" loading="lazy"><figcaption><strong>Compact cassette</strong> — magnetic tape in a protective shell.</figcaption></figure><figure class="note-figure" style="margin:0"><img src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/CompactDisc.jpg" alt="Real compact disc" loading="lazy"><figcaption><strong>Compact disc</strong> — optical digital storage read by laser.</figcaption></figure><figure class="note-figure" style="margin:0"><img src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/IPod_classic.png" alt="Real iPod Classic digital music player" loading="lazy"><figcaption><strong>Digital music player</strong> — large music libraries became portable.</figcaption></figure></div><ul><li><strong>Rechargeable batteries</strong> made repeated portable use possible.</li><li><strong>MP3 compression</strong> reduced the storage needed for each song.</li><li><strong>Flash memory</strong> stored large numbers of files without moving parts.</li><li><strong>LCD displays and touchscreens</strong> allowed compact digital interfaces.</li><li><strong>Streaming</strong> allowed content to be accessed online rather than stored entirely on the device.</li></ul><p>More powerful electronics also require designers to consider <strong>battery life, heat, repairability and electronic waste</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>The Internet of Things</h3><p>The <strong>Internet of Things (IoT)</strong> connects physical devices to networks so they can collect and exchange data. For example, sensors in factory equipment can detect changes in performance and help predict when maintenance is needed. Designers must also consider data privacy, security and whether the product can function without a connection.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>How Technology Changed Design and Manufacture</h2><div class="note-intro"><p><strong>Computer aided design (CAD)</strong> lets designers create, edit and test digital models. <strong>Computer aided manufacture (CAM)</strong> uses digital information to control manufacturing equipment, such as CNC machines.</p><p>Digital models can be shared with other designers, modified quickly and tested before a physical prototype is made. In factories, CNC machinery, robotics and digital quality checks can improve accuracy and repeatability. However, digital predictions still need to be checked through physical testing.</p></div><div class="note-image-grid" style="display:grid;grid-template-columns:repeat(auto-fit,minmax(240px,1fr));gap:14px;margin:18px 0"><figure class="note-figure" style="margin:0"><img src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/3dReconstruct_Mechanical_Part.png" alt="Real engineering software screenshot showing a meshed mechanical part for FEA and CFD analysis" loading="lazy"><figcaption><strong>FEA / CFD model:</strong> a digital mesh divides a component into many small elements so its behaviour can be analysed.</figcaption></figure><figure class="note-figure" style="margin:0"><img src="https://www.iws.fraunhofer.de/en/newsandmedia/press_releases/2019/presseinformation_2019-06/jcr%3Acontent/contentPar/sectioncomponent/sectionParsys/textwithinlinedimage/imageComponent1/image.img.jpg/1647105852182/PM-2019-06-fraunhofer-iws-600-hybrid-future-am-1.jpg" alt="Real metal additive manufacturing process using a laser deposition head" loading="lazy"><figcaption><strong>Metal additive manufacturing:</strong> material is deposited and fused to build geometry from digital data.</figcaption></figure></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Key developments</h3><ul><li><strong>3D printing:</strong> Builds a part layer by layer. It is useful for prototypes, customised parts and complex forms.</li><li><strong>Finite element analysis (FEA):</strong> Predicts how a model may respond to forces or heat, helping designers identify weak areas.</li><li><strong>Computational fluid dynamics (CFD):</strong> Models how liquids or gases flow around or through a design.</li><li><strong>Virtual reality (VR):</strong> Allows designers to experience a digital model at scale and assess features such as layout and ergonomics.</li><li><strong>Cloud based CAD:</strong> Allows people in different locations to work on shared design files.</li></ul></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>New Materials</h2><div class="note-intro"><p>The choice of material should be explained through a link between its <strong>properties</strong> and its <strong>use</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Glulam</h3><figure class="note-figure"><img src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/Glulam.JPG" alt="Real building structure using glued laminated timber beams" loading="lazy"><figcaption><strong>Glulam in use:</strong> laminated timber members can create strong structural frames and large spans.</figcaption></figure><p>Layers of bonded timber form strong, lightweight beams that can be curved. Typical uses include buildings and bridges with large spans.</p><p><strong>Example:</strong> Glulam can be formed into a curved roof beam. Bonding several timber layers together helps produce the required shape while distributing defects found in individual pieces of wood.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Kevlar</h3><figure class="note-figure"><img src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/Staal_van_aramidevezel_van_het_merk_Kevlar.jpg" alt="Real sample of yellow Kevlar aramid fibres" loading="lazy"><figcaption><strong>Kevlar fibre:</strong> the characteristic aramid fibres combine low mass with very high tensile strength.</figcaption></figure><p>Strong, tough and lightweight fibre. Used for protective equipment, puncture resistant tyres and aircraft components.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Graphene</h3><figure class="note-figure"><img src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/Basic_Graphene.png" alt="Molecular structure of a graphene sheet showing its hexagonal carbon lattice" loading="lazy"><figcaption><strong>Graphene structure:</strong> carbon atoms form a one-atom-thick hexagonal lattice.</figcaption></figure><p>Extremely thin carbon sheet with high tensile strength and good electrical and thermal conductivity. Potential uses include sensors, electronics, coatings and batteries.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Precious metal clay</h3><figure class="note-figure"><img src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/MetalClayPendant.jpg" alt="Pendant produced using precious metal clay" loading="lazy"><figcaption><strong>Precious metal clay:</strong> the mouldable material can be shaped before firing to leave a solid metal object.</figcaption></figure><p>Mouldable metal particles that form a solid metal object after firing. Used for jewellery and decorative products.</p></div></section>
<section class="note-panel"><h2>New Manufacturing Methods</h2>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Electrohydraulic Forming</h3><p>An electrical discharge creates a shockwave in water, forcing sheet metal against a former. This can form detailed shapes quickly, including <strong>car parts and appliance casings</strong>.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Metal Additive Manufacturing</h3><figure class="note-figure"><img src="https://www.iws.fraunhofer.de/en/newsandmedia/press_releases/2019/presseinformation_2019-06/jcr%3Acontent/contentPar/sectioncomponent/sectionParsys/textwithinlinedimage/imageComponent1/image.img.jpg/1647105852182/PM-2019-06-fraunhofer-iws-600-hybrid-future-am-1.jpg" alt="Real metal additive manufacturing using a laser deposition head" loading="lazy"><figcaption><strong>Metal additive manufacturing:</strong> a focused energy source fuses deposited material as the component is built up.</figcaption></figure><p>A laser fuses metal powder <strong>layer by layer</strong> to build a part from a digital model. This can produce complex shapes and internal channels that would be difficult to machine. It is useful for <strong>prototypes and customised parts</strong>, although finishing may still be required.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Fibre Injection Moulding</h3><p>A polymer mixed with reinforcing fibres is injected into a mould. The fibres can improve the <strong>strength and stiffness</strong> of lightweight parts used in products such as vehicles and sports equipment.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Laser Beam Welding</h3><figure class="note-figure"><img src="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/High-power_laser_welding.jpg" alt="Real high-power laser welding test on metal" loading="lazy"><figcaption><strong>Laser beam welding:</strong> a concentrated laser creates a narrow, precise weld while shielding gas protects the weld area.</figcaption></figure><p>A concentrated laser joins metal with a <strong>narrow, precise weld</strong>. It is useful for thin components and can cause less distortion than a wider heat source.</p></div>
<div class="study-card"><h3>Physical Vapour Deposition</h3><p><strong>PVD</strong> deposits a thin coating onto a product's surface. The coating can improve <strong>wear resistance or appearance</strong>, for example on cutting tools or decorative products.</p></div></section>
