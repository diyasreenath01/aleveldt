## Socio-economic influences

**Socio-economic influences** are changes in society and the economy that affect what people need, want and can afford, and therefore influence product design and manufacture.

For AQA, the important contexts are **post-WW1 Bauhaus and mass-produced furniture**, **WW2 rationing and Utility products**, and **contemporary fashion and demand for mass-produced furniture and decorative products**.

### Post-WW1: Bauhaus and mass production

After the First World War there was a need for practical products that could be manufactured efficiently for a wider population. The **Bauhaus**, founded in 1919, brought art, craft and industrial production together.

Bauhaus design generally favoured **function, simple geometric forms, limited decoration and suitability for industrial manufacture**. Designers increasingly considered how furniture and products could be made using standardised components and repeatable manufacturing processes rather than relying entirely on individual craft production.

The important connection is that changing social needs and industrial capability encouraged designers to create products suitable for **larger-scale, more economical production**.

### WW2 rationing and Utility products

During and after the Second World War, shortages and rationing restricted access to materials and manufacturing capacity. Products therefore had to use available resources efficiently.

The **Utility Scheme**, introduced in Britain in 1942, controlled the design and manufacture of essential goods such as furniture. Utility furniture was typically:

- functional and durable
- economical in its use of materials
- simple in form with limited decoration
- suitable for efficient, standardised manufacture.

This shows how economic and material restrictions can directly affect aesthetics, material choice and manufacturing methods. When resources are scarce, unnecessary decoration and material use become harder to justify.

### Contemporary society: fashion and mass-produced products

In contemporary markets, greater consumer choice, changing lifestyles and fashion can encourage frequent changes in product styling. Manufacturers use **mass production** to make large quantities at a low unit cost, while materials and processes such as moulded polymers allow complex shapes, colours and surface details to be produced repeatedly.

Consumers may replace products because styles, colours or features change even when an existing product still functions. This can increase sales and provide greater choice, but it can also contribute to **shorter product lifecycles, increased resource use and waste**.

### Mass-produced furniture

Modern furniture illustrates the relationship between economic conditions, lifestyle and manufacture. **Flat-pack and knock-down construction** can reduce storage and transport volume and allows products to be assembled by the user. Standardised components and large-scale manufacture can reduce production and distribution costs, making furniture accessible to a wider market.

However, very low prices can also encourage replacement rather than repair. Designers therefore need to balance **affordability and efficient manufacture** with durability, repairability and responsible resource use.

### What designers must consider

Socio-economic change can influence:

- the price consumers are able or willing to pay
- the quantity and type of products required
- materials and manufacturing methods
- product styling and fashion
- durability and expected product life
- transport, storage and distribution
- environmental consequences of increased consumption.

The key relationship is **context → design response → consequence**. For example, wartime material shortages encouraged economical Utility products, while contemporary mass markets encourage efficient large-scale manufacture and rapidly changing styles.
