## Marketing and brand identity

Marketing helps a business identify **who the customer is**, understand what they need or value and communicate why a product should appeal to them. Market research can investigate age, lifestyle, income, preferences, buying behaviour and competing products, helping designers make decisions about function, aesthetics, materials and price.

### Customer identification

A product is more likely to succeed when the intended market is clearly identified. Designers can use market research to establish the needs, wants and purchasing behaviour of a particular customer group and use this evidence when developing the specification, product and marketing strategy.

Different customers may value different factors such as price, durability, appearance, sustainability, status or ease of use. Marketing therefore needs to communicate benefits that are relevant to the intended customer rather than trying to appeal equally to everyone.

### Brand and corporate identity

A recognisable **brand identity** can be created through names, logos, colour, typography, packaging, product styling and advertising. **Corporate identity** is the consistent visual and communication style used across a company and its products. Consistency helps customers recognise the organisation or product range and can build trust and loyalty.

### Labelling and packaging

**Labelling** communicates information such as product identity, contents, instructions, warnings, materials, recycling information or other information required for the product. It can also reinforce brand identity through consistent graphics and typography.

**Packaging** protects the product during storage, transport and sale while also communicating information and attracting the target customer. Distinctive shapes, colours and graphics can make a product recognisable, but packaging must still meet practical, environmental and legal requirements. Excessive packaging may improve shelf presence but increase material use, transport volume and waste.

### Promotion and global marketing

Products may be promoted through advertising, social media, online video, influencers and viral or referral marketing. Digital promotion can reach large audiences quickly and can be targeted, but a campaign is only effective if it reaches the intended market and communicates a convincing benefit.

Global markets can increase potential sales, but the same product or campaign may not work everywhere. Designers and marketers may need to consider **language, culture, symbolism, local preferences, regulations and purchasing power**. A globally consistent brand may therefore still need local adaptation.

### Product costing and profit

Commercial decisions must consider **direct costs** such as materials, labour and production consumables and **indirect costs** such as administration, maintenance, equipment depreciation, security and power.

**Profit = sales revenue − total costs.**

A low selling price may increase demand but reduce the profit made on each product. A higher price may increase margin but only works if the target market believes the product offers sufficient value. Costing therefore influences materials, manufacturing methods, production quantity and the final selling price.

### Entrepreneurs

**Entrepreneurs** identify opportunities, take financial risk and organise resources to bring products or services to market. They may identify an unmet need, develop a product or business model, obtain finance and coordinate design, manufacture and marketing.

Technical creativity alone is not enough: the idea also needs a genuine customer need, workable production, suitable pricing and effective marketing. Successful enterprise therefore links design decisions to commercial viability.
