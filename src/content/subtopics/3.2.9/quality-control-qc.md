<section class="note-panel"><h2>Quality Control</h2><div class="note-intro"><p><strong>Quality control (QC)</strong> involves <strong>monitoring, checking, measuring and testing</strong> materials, components, equipment and products throughout production to detect defects and ensure they conform to specified standards and acceptable tolerances.</p><p><strong>QA plans for quality; QC checks whether the required quality has been achieved.</strong> QC therefore focuses on checking actual materials, components, processes and outputs.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>QC throughout production</h3><p>Quality control should not only happen at the end. Checks can be planned at several stages:</p><ul><li><strong>Incoming raw materials and components:</strong> check dimensions, grade/specification, condition, colour or finish where relevant before faulty material enters production.</li><li><strong>During manufacture:</strong> check critical dimensions, hole positions, joints, surface quality, machine settings and other features at appropriate stages so errors are found before more time and material are added.</li><li><strong>Prototype and pre-production checks:</strong> measure the prototype against drawings and specification, check fit and assembly, test function and safety, and identify changes before committing to larger-scale manufacture.</li><li><strong>Completed products:</strong> inspect dimensions, finish, assembly and function before products are accepted or dispatched.</li></ul><p>A <strong>tolerance</strong> is the permitted variation from a specified dimension or value. QC determines whether a component or product lies within that acceptable range.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Go/no-go gauges</h3><p>A <strong>go/no-go gauge</strong> quickly checks whether a manufactured feature lies within its permitted limits. The ‘go’ part should fit and the ‘no-go’ part should not. It gives a fast pass/fail result rather than an exact measurement, making it useful when large numbers of repeated components need checking.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Digital and precision measuring</h3><p><strong>Digital vernier callipers</strong> can accurately measure internal, external and depth dimensions. <strong>Micrometers</strong> are used for very precise measurements such as material thickness or shaft diameter. <strong>Laser or probe scanning</strong> can also compare manufactured components with required dimensions or digital data.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Non-destructive testing</h3><p><strong>Non-destructive testing (NDT)</strong> checks a material or component for defects without damaging it. AQA examples include <strong>X-rays</strong> and <strong>ultrasound</strong>, which can reveal internal faults that may not be visible from the surface.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>Choosing the QC check</h3><p>The check must suit the feature being controlled. A go/no-go gauge is efficient for a repeated critical size; callipers or micrometers provide numerical measurements; visual inspection can identify finish defects; functional testing checks whether the assembled product performs as required.</p></div><div class="study-card"><h3>QA vs QC</h3><p><strong>QA is preventative:</strong> procedures and systems are planned to reduce the chance of faults occurring.<br><strong>QC is detective:</strong> materials, components and products are inspected, measured or tested to identify whether the required quality has actually been achieved.</p></div></section>
