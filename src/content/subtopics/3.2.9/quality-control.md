## Quality control (QC)

**Quality control (QC)** uses **inspection, measurement and testing** to identify materials, components or finished products that do not meet the specification. Checks can occur during production as well as at the end. **QA aims to prevent defects; QC detects them.**

### Inspection methods

- **Visual and surface inspection:** look for scratches, cracks, poor finishes, assembly faults or inconsistent colour.
- **Dimensional measurement:** use vernier calipers or micrometers to compare dimensions with specified limits and tolerances.
- **Material or chemical checks:** test samples where material composition or performance is critical.
- **Go/no-go gauge:** a gauge checks whether a feature lies within permitted limits. The **GO** side must fit or pass; the **NO-GO** side must not. It gives a fast pass/fail result without measuring the exact dimension.
- **Coordinate measuring machine (CMM):** a probe measures coordinates on a component to check the size, position and geometry of features against the design. CMM data can also support reverse engineering into CAD. Accuracy depends on the machine, probe, setup and calibration.

### How much is inspected?

- **100% inspection:** every item is checked. Useful for critical features or where failure would be costly, but it takes more time and resources.
- **Normal inspection (sampling):** selected items are checked, for example at set intervals during a production run. It is faster, but defects in unchecked items can be missed.
- **Reduced inspection:** fewer items are checked when a stable process and a reliable quality history justify less frequent sampling. Inspection may need to increase if faults appear.

The inspection level depends on product risk, production volume, process consistency and the cost of checking.

### Destructive and non-destructive testing

| Destructive testing | Non-destructive testing (NDT) |
|---|---|
| A sample is loaded or tested until it fails or becomes unusable, revealing performance limits. | A component is inspected without making it unusable, so it can remain in service or be sold. |
| Examples include tensile tests to fracture and impact tests that break a specimen. | Examples include visual checks, **X-rays** to reveal internal features or flaws, and **ultrasound** to detect internal discontinuities. |
| Provides direct evidence of strength or failure behaviour, but the tested sample is sacrificed. | Preserves the item, but the method must suit the material, defect and component geometry. |

**Controlled testing** uses a consistent method, suitable equipment and recorded conditions so results can be compared with specification limits. Destructive tests are normally performed on representative samples; non-destructive checks may be used on samples or every item.

### Using QC results

A product that falls outside its tolerance or fails a required test may be rejected, reworked or investigated. Recording failures helps identify recurring faults so production settings, tools or QA procedures can be corrected. **QC detects a problem; it does not, by itself, prevent the next defect.**
