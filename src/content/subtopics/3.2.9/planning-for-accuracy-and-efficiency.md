## Planning for accuracy and efficiency

A manufacturing plan identifies **materials, dimensions, tolerances, processes, sequence of work, equipment, deadlines and quality checks** before production begins. Good planning reduces avoidable delays, repeated setup, material waste and rework while helping the product meet its specification consistently.

### Planning manufacture

Operations should be arranged in a logical sequence. Important dimensions can be taken from common **datums**, while jigs, fixtures and templates can improve repeatability. Quality checks should be placed at stages where errors can be found before more time and material are added to a faulty component.

Efficiency does not simply mean working faster. The plan should balance **accuracy, quality, time, cost and material use**. A very tight tolerance, for example, may improve fit but is inefficient if the product does not require that level of precision.

### Planning at different production scales

For a **one-off prototype**, the plan may rely on skilled measuring, marking out, workshop processes and checks at important stages. Flexibility is useful because the design may still change.

For **small or medium-scale production**, jigs, fixtures, templates, CNC programs and standardised procedures can reduce repeated setup and improve consistency between products. Material ordering, labour and quality checks also need to be coordinated with the batch schedule.

For **large-scale production**, dedicated tooling, automation and production-line sequencing may be justified. Accuracy must be designed into the process because a small error can be repeated across thousands of products. Automated or rapid quality checks can detect process drift before large quantities become defective.

The most appropriate plan therefore changes with scale: increasing production quantity can justify greater investment in tooling, automation and process control.

### Critical Path Analysis

**Critical Path Analysis (CPA)** maps tasks, their durations and dependencies. Tasks that do not depend on each other may be completed concurrently. The **critical path** is the sequence of dependent tasks that determines the shortest possible completion time; delaying one of these tasks can delay the whole project.

CPA helps identify where time is most critical and where activities can run in parallel. Its usefulness depends on realistic task durations and dependencies, so unexpected supply or production problems may require the schedule to be revised.

### Scrum

**Scrum** organises collaborative work into short periods called **sprints**. The team agrees work to complete, reviews progress regularly and uses feedback to adjust later work. This can help a design team respond quickly to changing requirements or problems.

Scrum is flexible, whereas CPA is useful when task order and dependencies need to be planned clearly. The most appropriate approach depends on the type of project and how predictable the work is.
