## Planning for accuracy and efficiency

A manufacturing plan identifies **materials, dimensions, tolerances, processes, sequence of work, deadlines and quality checks** before production begins. Good planning reduces avoidable delays, repeated setup, material waste and rework.

### Planning manufacture

Operations should be arranged in a logical sequence. Important dimensions can be taken from common **datums**, while jigs, fixtures and templates can improve repeatability in batch manufacture. Quality checks should be placed at stages where errors can be found before more time and material are added to a faulty component.

Efficiency does not simply mean working faster. The plan should balance **accuracy, quality, time, cost and material use**. For example, a very tight tolerance may improve fit but is inefficient if the product does not require that level of precision.

### Critical Path Analysis

**Critical Path Analysis (CPA)** maps tasks, their durations and dependencies. Tasks that do not depend on each other may be completed at the same time. The **critical path** is the sequence of dependent tasks that determines the shortest possible completion time; delaying one of these tasks can delay the whole project.

CPA is useful for identifying where time is most critical, but its plan is only reliable if task durations and dependencies are realistic. Unexpected supply or production problems may require the schedule to be revised.

### Scrum

**Scrum** organises collaborative work into short periods called **sprints**. The team agrees work to complete, reviews progress regularly and uses feedback to adjust later work. This can help a design team respond quickly to changing requirements or problems.

Scrum is flexible, whereas CPA is useful when task order and dependencies need to be planned clearly. The most appropriate approach depends on the type of project and how predictable the work is.
